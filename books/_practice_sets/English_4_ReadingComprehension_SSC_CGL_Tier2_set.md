## Passage 1

The emergence of quantum computing represents both an unprecedented opportunity and a profound threat to contemporary cryptography. Modern encryption standards, including RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography, rely on the computational difficulty of factoring large numbers or solving discrete logarithms — tasks that classical computers would require billions of years to complete. However, quantum computers, leveraging principles of superposition and entanglement, can theoretically solve these problems in hours. This has spurred the development of post-quantum cryptography, a field dedicated to creating encryption algorithms resistant to quantum attacks. The transition to quantum-resistant standards is expected to take years, but the stakes are immense: a failure to adapt could compromise the security of global financial systems, military communications, and personal data. Tech giants like Google and IBM have already announced significant investments in this area, signaling a recognition that the cryptographic landscape must evolve to meet emerging threats. While the timeline for practical quantum computers capable of breaking current encryption remains debated, the consensus among security experts is that preparation must begin now, not when the threat materializes.

### Q1.
What is the main idea of the passage?
- (a) Quantum computing will replace classical computing.
- (b) Quantum computing threatens current encryption, prompting development of new standards.
- (c) RSA encryption is the most secure.
- (d) Quantum computers are slow.

**Answer:** (b) Quantum computing threatens current encryption, prompting development of new standards.
**Solution:** The passage covers both the threat (quantum breaking encryption) and the response (post-quantum cryptography, tech investments). Main idea = both sides.
**Strategy:** Main idea — synthesize threat + response.
**Time Target:** 90 sec

## Passage 2

While electric vehicles are often touted as the silver bullet for climate change, a more nuanced analysis reveals significant challenges. The production of lithium-ion batteries requires mining rare earth minerals, often in environmentally destructive ways. Furthermore, the electricity that powers these vehicles must itself come from somewhere — and in many countries, that source is still fossil fuels. The manufacturing footprint of EVs, when including battery production, can be comparable to that of traditional internal combustion engines, depending on the energy mix used in production. Lifecycle analyses suggest that EVs become cleaner than gasoline cars only after a certain mileage threshold, varying by region and energy grid. The transition to electric mobility, therefore, requires parallel investment in renewable energy infrastructure, ethical sourcing of materials, and battery recycling programs. Without these complementary measures, the mass adoption of EVs could merely shift environmental burden from tailpipes to mines and power plants, rather than eliminating it altogether.

**Solution:** The author acknowledges EVs as a "silver bullet" claim but presents challenges. Tone is balanced.
**Strategy:** Tone question — look for adjectives, evaluate overall stance.

## Passage 3

The phenomenon of "brain drain" — the migration of skilled professionals from developing to developed countries — has attracted significant attention in recent decades. While this migration brings benefits for the receiving nations, including talent acquisition and economic growth, the source countries suffer from a loss of valuable human capital. Some economists argue that remittances sent home by diaspora communities offset this loss, providing crucial foreign exchange and supporting families. However, the long-term impact remains debatable, as the loss of skilled workers can hamper innovation and institutional development in the source countries. Recent policy efforts have focused on creating conditions that encourage skilled diaspora members to return or contribute to their home countries through knowledge transfer, entrepreneurship, and investment. Whether these "diaspora engagement" strategies can meaningfully address the structural causes of brain drain — including limited research funding, weak governance, and economic instability — is the central question facing policymakers today.

### Q11.
What is the main idea of the passage?
- (a) Brain drain benefits everyone.
- (b) Brain drain has costs for source countries despite remittance benefits, and policy responses are evolving.
- (c) Brain drain is a new phenomenon.
- (d) Brain drain is the biggest problem facing developing countries.

**Answer:** (b) Brain drain has costs for source countries despite remittance benefits, and policy responses are evolving.
**Solution:** The passage presents brain drain's mixed effects (benefits for receiving countries, costs for source countries), then discusses evolving policy responses.
**Strategy:** Main idea — capture the complex dynamics.
**Time Target:** 90 sec

## Tier 2 Strategy

1. **Longer passages:** 400-500 words; allocate 1.5-2 min for reading.
2. **Inference-heavy:** 4-5 inference questions per passage set.
3. **Tone/Purpose:** Practice identifying author's stance through conclusion sentences.
4. **Vocabulary in context:** Don't rely on default meaning; read the surrounding sentence.
