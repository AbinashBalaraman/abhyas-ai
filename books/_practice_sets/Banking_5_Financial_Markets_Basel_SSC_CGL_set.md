### Q1. Money market deals with funds maturing in:
(a) Less than 6 months (b) Less than 1 year (c) Less than 2 years (d) Less than 5 years

**Answer: (b) Less than 1 year**

**Solution:** The money market deals with short-term funds (≤ 1 year). It provides liquidity and supports monetary policy implementation. Money market instruments include Call Money (overnight), Notice Money (2-14 days), Term Money (15-365 days), Treasury Bills (T-Bills: 91-day, 182-day, 364-day), Commercial Paper (CP: 15-365 days, min ₹5 lakh), Certificate of Deposit (CD: 7 days-1 year, min ₹5 lakh), Repo, Reverse Repo, CBLO, and Tri-Party Repo. The money market is highly liquid, low-risk, and wholesale.

### Q2. Treasury Bills are issued for:
(a) 91, 182, 364 days (b) 30, 90, 180 days (c) 100, 200, 300 days (d) 60, 120, 240 days

**Answer: (a) 91, 182, 364 days**

**Solution:** Treasury Bills (T-Bills) are zero-coupon government securities issued for 91-day, 182-day, and 364-day tenures. T-Bills are issued by the Government of India through RBI. They are sold at a discount and redeemed at face value. The difference is the return to the investor. T-Bills are considered risk-free (no default risk) and are used for short-term funding. T-Bills are also used in Open Market Operations (OMO) by RBI. The money market instruments are regulated by RBI.

### Q6. Basel I was published in:
(a) 1986 (b) 1988 (c) 1990 (d) 1992

**Answer: (b) 1988**

**Solution:** Basel I (1988 Basel Capital Accord) was published in 1988 by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS). It was enforced in G10 countries in 1992. Basel I focused on credit risk only, with a minimum Capital Adequacy Ratio (CAR) of 8% of risk-weighted assets (RWAs). Risk weights: 0%, 20%, 50%, 100%. A 1996 amendment added market risk framework. The BCBS is housed at the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) in Basel, Switzerland. Basel I was the first international banking regulation.

### Q7. Basel III CET1 minimum (without CCB) is:
(a) 3.5% (b) 4.5% (c) 5.5% (d) 6%

**Answer: (b) 4.5%**

**Solution:** Basel III CET1 (Common Equity Tier 1) minimum is 4.5% (without Capital Conservation Buffer - CCB). With CCB of 2.5%, the minimum becomes 7%. Tier 1 Capital minimum is 6% (8.5% with CCB), and Total Capital minimum is 8% (10.5% with CCB). Basel III was published in 2010/11 (post 2008 financial crisis). It introduced LCR (100%), NSFR (100%), Leverage Ratio (3%), and higher quality capital requirements. Indian banks typically maintain CAR of 15-18% (well above 8% minimum).

### Q8. LCR stands for:
(a) Liquidity Coverage Ratio (b) Loan Capital Ratio (c) Liquidity Cash Reserve (d) Liquid Credit Reserve

**Answer: (a) Liquidity Coverage Ratio**

**Solution:** LCR stands for Liquidity Coverage Ratio. It is a Basel III requirement ensuring banks maintain sufficient High Quality Liquid Assets (HQLA) to survive a 30-day stress scenario. Formula: LCR = HQLA / Net cash outflows over 30 days. Minimum LCR: 100%. HQLA includes Level 1 (cash, government securities) and Level 2A/2B assets (corporate bonds, equities with haircuts). LCR was introduced to ensure banks can meet short-term liquidity needs during stress. NSFR (Net Stable Funding Ratio) is the long-term counterpart.

**Time Target:** 30s | **Chapter Reference:** Banking/05_Financial_Markets_Basel.md, p.232-237, p.500 | **VERIFY:** No

### Q9. NSFR minimum required is:
(a) 75% (b) 90% (c) 100% (d) 110%

**Answer: (c) 100%**

**Solution:** The NSFR (Net Stable Funding Ratio) minimum is 100%. NSFR is a Basel III requirement ensuring banks have sufficient stable funding to cover their long-term assets over a 1-year horizon. Formula: NSFR = Available Stable Funding (ASF) / Required Stable Funding (RSF). NSFR is the long-term counterpart of LCR (short-term). NSFR limits excessive reliance on short-term wholesale funding. Indian banks must maintain NSFR ≥ 100% as per RBI. LCR and NSFR are both key Basel III liquidity standards.

**Time Target:** 30s | **Chapter Reference:** Banking/05_Financial_Markets_Basel.md, p.232-237, p.512 | **VERIFY:** No

### Q10. Capital Conservation Buffer under Basel III is:
(a) 1.5% (b) 2% (c) 2.5% (d) 3%

**Answer: (c) 2.5%**

**Solution:** The Capital Conservation Buffer (CCB) under Basel III is 2.5%. It is added to the minimum capital requirements, so Total Capital must be 8% + 2.5% = 10.5%. CET1 must be 4.5% + 2.5% = 7%. Tier 1 Capital must be 6% + 2.5% = 8.5%. CCB is built up during good times to absorb losses during stress. If a bank falls below the CCB, it faces restrictions on dividend distribution, share buybacks, and bonus payments. The CCB is mandatory for all banks under Basel III.

### Q12. The capital market deals with funds for tenure:
(a) < 1 year (b) > 1 year (c) < 6 months (d) < 3 months

**Answer: (b) > 1 year**

**Solution:** The capital market deals with long-term funds (> 1 year). It includes the primary market (new securities) and secondary market (trading existing securities). The primary market involves new issues like IPO (Initial Public Offering), FPO (Follow-on Public Offering), Rights Issue, Bonus Issue, QIP (Qualified Institutional Placement), and OFS (Offer for Sale). The secondary market is where existing securities are traded on stock exchanges (BSE, NSE). The capital market is regulated by SEBI. Capital market instruments include stocks, bonds, debentures, and government securities (long-term).

### Q14. The Leverage Ratio under Basel III is:
(a) 1% (b) 2% (c) 3% (d) 5%

**Answer: (c) 3%**

**Solution:** The Leverage Ratio under Basel III is 3% (minimum). It is defined as Tier 1 Capital / Total Exposure. The leverage ratio is a non-risk-based metric to limit excessive leverage in the banking system. It complements the risk-based capital requirements (CAR). The 3% minimum was set as part of Basel III reforms to prevent banks from taking excessive leverage. Indian banks must maintain a minimum leverage ratio of 3.5% (RBI's requirement is more stringent than Basel III's 3%).
