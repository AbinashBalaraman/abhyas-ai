## 1. Comprehensive Theory

### 1.1 Heat and Temperature

**Heat** is a form of energy that flows from a hotter body to a colder body. SI unit: joule (J). CGS unit: calorie (cal). 1 cal = 4.186 J [General Studies Reference, p. 101].

**Temperature** is the degree of hotness or coldness of a body. SI unit: kelvin (K). Common units: °C, °F.

**Relationship:** Heat is the cause; temperature is the effect. The temperature of a body tells us the direction of heat flow.

**Temperature scales:**

- **Celsius (°C):** $T(°C) = T(K) - 273.15$
- **Fahrenheit (°F):** $T(°F) = \frac{9}{5}T(°C) + 32$
- **Kelvin (K):** SI unit; 0 K = -273.15°C (absolute zero)

### 1.2 Thermometers

A thermometer measures temperature. Different types of thermometers use different properties of materials that change with temperature:

- **Mercury thermometer:** Uses expansion of mercury (liquid-in-glass). Range: -39°C to 357°C (freezing to boiling point of mercury).
- **Alcohol thermometer:** Uses coloured alcohol; used for low temperatures (alcohol freezes at -115°C).
- **Bimetallic thermometer:** Uses the differential expansion of two metals.
- **Resistance thermometer (RTD):** Uses the change in resistance of a metal with temperature.
- **Thermocouple:** Uses the emf generated at the junction of two dissimilar metals.
- **Pyrometer:** For very high temperatures (uses radiation).

### 1.3 Thermal Expansion

Most substances expand on heating and contract on cooling. There are three types of thermal expansion [General Studies Reference, p. 103-105]:

- **Linear expansion** (solids): Change in length, $L' = L(1 + \alpha \Delta T)$, where α is the coefficient of linear expansion.
- **Superficial (areal) expansion** (solids): Change in area.
- **Cubical (volumetric) expansion** (liquids and gases): Change in volume, $V' = V(1 + \gamma \Delta T)$, where γ is the coefficient of cubical expansion.

**Anomalous expansion of water:** Water contracts on heating from 0°C to 4°C and expands from 4°C to higher temperatures. Water has maximum density at 4°C. This is why ice floats on water and why aquatic life survives in cold climates.

**Applications of thermal expansion:**
- Expansion joints in bridges, railway tracks, concrete roads.
- Bimetallic strips in thermostats.
- Mercury or alcohol in thermometers.

### 1.4 Specific Heat Capacity

The amount of heat required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1°C (or 1 K) is called its **specific heat capacity** [General Studies Reference, p. 105].

$$Q = mc\Delta T$$

where:
- $Q$ = heat absorbed
- $m$ = mass
- $c$ = specific heat capacity
- $\Delta T$ = change in temperature

SI unit: J/(kg·K).

**Water has a very high specific heat capacity (4200 J/(kg·K))** — it can store a lot of heat with a small change in temperature. This is why water is used as a coolant in car radiators and as a heat reservoir.

**Water equivalent:** The mass of water that would absorb the same amount of heat as the given body for the same rise in temperature.

### 1.5 Change of State

Matter exists in three states: solid, liquid, gas. The state can be changed by heating or cooling [General Studies Reference, p. 4]:

- **Solid → Liquid:** Melting (fusion) — heat absorbed = latent heat of fusion.
- **Liquid → Gas:** Vaporisation — heat absorbed = latent heat of vaporisation.
- **Liquid → Solid:** Freezing (solidification) — heat released.
- **Gas → Liquid:** Condensation (liquefaction) — heat released.

**Latent heat of fusion ($L_f$):** Heat required to change 1 kg of solid to liquid at its melting point without temperature change. For ice: $L_f = 3.34 \times 10^5$ J/kg.

**Latent heat of vaporisation ($L_v$):** Heat required to change 1 kg of liquid to gas at its boiling point. For water: $L_v = 22.5 \times 10^5$ J/kg.

**Calorimetry principle:** Heat lost = Heat gained (for an isolated system in thermal equilibrium).

$$m_1 c_1 (T_1 - T) = m_2 c_2 (T - T_2)$$

where T is the final equilibrium temperature.

### 1.6 Transfer of Heat

Heat can be transferred by three processes [General Studies Reference, p. 106-108]:

#### (a) Conduction
- Transfer of heat through a material without actual movement of the particles.
- Occurs mainly in solids (especially metals).
- **Good conductors:** Silver, copper, aluminium, iron (metals in general).
- **Bad conductors (insulators):** Wood, plastic, rubber, air, wool, fibreglass.
- The handle of a utensil is made of wood or plastic (insulator) so the heat doesn't reach the hand.
- Igloos (snow houses) trap air, which is a bad conductor, keeping the inside warm.

#### (b) Convection
- Transfer of heat in fluids (liquids and gases) by the actual movement of the heated particles.
- Causes sea and land breezes, ventilation, boiling of water.
- **Sea breeze:** During the day, land heats up faster than the sea; hot air over land rises, cool air from the sea moves in → sea breeze.
- **Land breeze:** At night, land cools faster than the sea; hot air over the sea rises, cool air from the land moves in → land breeze.

#### (c) Radiation
- Transfer of heat in the form of electromagnetic waves (infrared radiation).
- Does not require a medium — can travel through vacuum.
- All bodies emit and absorb radiation.
- **Good absorbers are also good emitters** (Kirchhoff's law).
- **Black surfaces:** Best absorbers and emitters of heat.
- **White/Shiny surfaces:** Poor absorbers, good reflectors of heat.
- This is why we wear white/light-coloured clothes in summer (reflects heat) and dark clothes in winter (absorbs heat).

**Newton's Law of Cooling:** The rate of loss of heat of a body is directly proportional to the difference in temperature between the body and its surroundings [General Studies Reference, p. 108]:

$$\frac{dQ}{dt} \propto (T - T_s)$$

### 1.7 Thermodynamics (Brief)

**First Law of Thermodynamics:** The heat supplied to a system is equal to the sum of the change in internal energy and the work done by the system.

$$Q = \Delta U + W$$

**Heat engine:** A device that converts heat into work. Operates between a hot source (T₁) and cold sink (T₂).

$$\text{Efficiency}, \eta = \frac{W}{Q_1} = 1 - \frac{T_2}{T_1}$$ (Carnot engine, ideal)

### 1.8 Wave Motion

A **wave** is a disturbance that travels through a medium (or through space) transferring energy from one point to another without the transfer of matter [General Studies Reference, p. 117].

**Key terms:**

- **Wavelength (λ):** Distance between two consecutive crests or troughs (or two consecutive compressions/rarefactions). SI unit: metre.
- **Frequency (f or ν):** Number of oscillations per unit time. SI unit: hertz (Hz). 1 Hz = 1 s⁻¹.
- **Time period (T):** Time for one complete oscillation. T = 1/f.
- **Amplitude (A):** Maximum displacement from the mean position.
- **Wave speed (v):** Distance travelled by the wave per unit time. v = fλ.

**Types of waves** [General Studies Reference, p. 117-118]:

1. **Mechanical waves:** Require a medium for propagation.
   - **Transverse waves:** Particles vibrate perpendicular to the direction of wave propagation (e.g., waves on a string, light waves, water waves).
   - **Longitudinal waves:** Particles vibrate parallel to the direction of wave propagation (e.g., sound waves, waves in a spring).
2. **Electromagnetic waves:** Do not require a medium; can travel through vacuum. e.g., light, radio waves, X-rays.
3. **Matter waves:** Associated with particles (de Broglie waves; higher classes).

### 1.9 Sound Waves

**Sound** is a longitudinal mechanical wave that requires a medium for propagation. Sound cannot travel through vacuum. Speed of sound in air (at 20°C) ≈ 343 m/s [General Studies Reference, p. 119-120].

**Properties of sound:**

- Produced by vibrating objects.
- Requires a medium (cannot travel through vacuum).
- Longitudinal wave in air (particles vibrate parallel to wave direction).
- Speed: ~330 m/s in air, ~1500 m/s in water, ~5000 m/s in steel.
- Speed depends on: temperature, humidity, nature of medium.

**Speed of sound in different media [General Studies Reference, p. 120-122]:**

| Medium | Speed (m/s) |
|--------|------------|
| Air (20°C) | 343 |
| Air (0°C) | 331 |
| Water | ~1500 |
| Steel | ~5000-6000 |
| Glass | ~5000 |

**Speed of sound depends on:**
- Temperature: Increases with temperature (in air, ≈ 0.6 m/s per °C).
- Humidity: Sound travels faster in humid air.
- Nature of medium: Faster in solids than liquids, faster in liquids than gases.

**Important:** Speed of sound < Speed of light (about $3 \times 10^8$ m/s). This is why we see lightning before hearing thunder.

### 1.10 Characteristics of Sound

Three main characteristics of sound [General Studies Reference, p. 123-124]:

1. **Pitch (frequency):** High frequency = high pitch (e.g., whistle, flute). Low frequency = low pitch (e.g., drum, male voice). A woman's voice has higher pitch than a man's due to higher frequency of vocal cords.
2. **Loudness (amplitude):** Greater amplitude = louder sound. Measured in decibels (dB). Normal conversation ≈ 60 dB; pain threshold ≈ 120 dB.
3. **Quality (timbre):** Distinguishes sounds of the same pitch and loudness from different sources (e.g., two instruments playing the same note).

### 1.11 Reflection, Refraction, and Superposition of Sound

**Reflection of sound:** Sound reflects from hard surfaces (walls, cliffs), producing echoes. The minimum distance to hear an echo is ~17.2 m (so that the reflected sound reaches the ear at least 0.1 s after the original) [General Studies Reference, p. 122-123].

**Applications of reflection of sound:**
- **Echo:** Hearing the reflected sound.
- **Reverberation:** Multiple reflections causing sound to persist.
- **Stethoscope:** Multiple reflections of sound through tubes.
- **Hearing aid:** Collects and focuses sound.
- **SONAR:** Sound Navigation And Ranging — uses sound waves to detect underwater objects.
- **Soundboard:** Curved surface in concert halls for even distribution.

**Refraction of sound:** Bending of sound as it passes from one medium to another (or due to temperature/humidity variations in the same medium).

**Superposition of waves:** When two or more waves meet, the resultant displacement at any point is the algebraic sum of the individual displacements [General Studies Reference, p. 125-129].

**Constructive interference:** Waves in phase → amplitude adds up.
**Destructive interference:** Waves out of phase → amplitude cancels.

**Stationary (standing) waves:** Formed by superposition of two identical waves travelling in opposite directions. Have nodes (zero amplitude) and antinodes (maximum amplitude).

**Beats:** Periodic variation in loudness due to superposition of two sound waves of slightly different frequencies. Number of beats per second = difference in frequencies.

$$f_{beats} = |f_1 - f_2|$$

### 1.12 Infrasonic, Audible, and Ultrasonic Sound

The range of hearing for a normal human ear is **20 Hz to 20,000 Hz** [General Studies Reference, p. 123].

- **Infrasonic sound:** Frequency < 20 Hz. Produced by earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, elephants, whales. Cannot be heard by human ear.
- **Audible sound:** 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. Can be heard by human ear.
- **Ultrasonic sound:** Frequency > 20,000 Hz. Cannot be heard by human ear. Produced by bats, dolphins, some insects. Used in SONAR, ultrasonography (medical imaging), industrial cleaning, jewellery cleaning, dog whistles.

**Applications of ultrasound** [General Studies Reference, p. 124-125]:

- **SONAR:** Used by ships to detect underwater objects (depth, fish, submarines). Formula: depth = (speed of sound × time)/2.
- **Medical imaging (ultrasonography):** For examining foetus, internal organs, tumours.
- **Industrial cleaning:** Cleaning of jewellery, watches, electronic components.
- **Detecting cracks in metals:** Ultrasonic flaw detection.
- **Dog whistles:** For dog training (dogs can hear ultrasound).
- **Bats' navigation:** Echolocation.

**Applications of infrasound:**

- Detection of earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, nuclear explosions.
- Animal communication (elephants, whales).
- Studying the Earth's interior.

### 1.13 The Human Ear

The human ear is the organ of hearing. Main parts:

- **Outer ear (pinna + auditory canal):** Collects and funnels sound waves.
- **Middle ear (eardrum + three small bones — hammer, anvil, stirrup):** Amplifies and transmits vibrations to the inner ear.
- **Inner ear (cochlea + auditory nerve):** Converts vibrations into electrical signals, sent to the brain.

**Hearing range:** 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. Beyond this, sounds are inaudible.

**Hearing loss (deafness):** Can be due to damage to any part of the ear; can be partial or complete. Prolonged exposure to loud noise (> 85 dB) can cause permanent hearing loss.

### 1.14 Noise Pollution

**Noise:** Unpleasant, loud, unwanted sound. Above 80 dB is generally considered noise pollution.

**Effects of noise pollution:**
- Hearing loss (with prolonged exposure).
- Lack of sleep, hypertension, anxiety.
- Reduced work efficiency.

**Control:** Use of silencers, sound-absorbing materials, planting trees, ear plugs/muffs, banning loudspeakers at night.

## 3. Everyday Science & Applications

**Q1: Why is the handle of a metal utensil made of wood or plastic?**
A: Wood and plastic are bad conductors of heat, so the heat from the hot utensil does not reach the hand. [General Studies Reference, p. 106]

**Q2: Why do we wear woollen clothes in winter?**
A: Wool is a poor conductor; it traps air between its fibres, preventing body heat from escaping. [General Studies Reference, p. 107]

**Q3: Why does ice float on water?**
A: Water has maximum density at 4°C. Below 4°C, water expands; ice (at 0°C) is less dense than water at 4°C, so it floats. [General Studies Reference, p. 103-105]

**Q4: Why are houses in hot climates often painted white?**
A: White surfaces are poor absorbers of heat and good reflectors, keeping the house cooler. [General Studies Reference, p. 108]

**Q5: Why is a steel spoon placed in a hot drink — does it cool the drink?**
A: The spoon absorbs heat from the drink (conduction) and itself becomes hot; the heat is gradually lost to the surroundings. So it does help to cool the drink.

**Q6: Why is the inside of an oven hotter than the outside?**
A: The oven walls are insulated (bad conductors), so heat does not escape easily. [General Studies Reference, p. 106]

**Q7: Why are two thin blankets warmer than one thick blanket?**
A: A thick blanket gets compressed, losing the trapped air. Two thin blankets trap more air between them, providing better insulation. [General Studies Reference, p. 107]

**Q8: Why is sound heard more clearly at night than during the day?**
A: At night, the air near the ground is cooler than the air above, so sound waves bend back towards the ground. During the day, the reverse is true.

**Q9: Why does lightning appear before thunder?**
A: Light travels much faster than sound ($3 \times 10^8$ m/s vs ~343 m/s). We see lightning almost instantaneously, but sound takes time. [General Studies Reference, p. 120]

**Q10: Why can't sound travel through vacuum?**
A: Sound is a mechanical wave and needs a medium to propagate. There are no particles in vacuum to carry the vibrations. [General Studies Reference, p. 119]

**Q11: Why is a megaphone (or horn) used by a speaker?**
A: A megaphone reflects sound waves multiple times in a conical shape, directing the sound waves into a narrow beam so that the sound can be heard at a greater distance. [General Studies Reference, p. 122-123]

**Q12: Why is a curved soundboard used in concert halls?**
A: A curved soundboard reflects sound from the speaker in all directions, ensuring uniform distribution throughout the hall. [General Studies Reference, p. 122-123]

**Q13: Why are some musical instruments louder than others?**
A: The loudness depends on the amplitude of vibration. Instruments producing larger amplitudes are louder. [General Studies Reference, p. 123-124]

**Q14: Why is a woman's voice higher-pitched than a man's?**
A: The vocal cords of a woman vibrate at a higher frequency than those of a man, producing higher-pitched sound. [General Studies Reference, p. 123]

**Q15: Why are ultrasounds used in medical imaging?**
A: Ultrasound can penetrate soft tissues, reflect from internal organs, and form images without the harmful effects of X-rays. [General Studies Reference, p. 124-125]

**Q16: Why can a dog hear a dog whistle but a human cannot?**
A: The whistle produces ultrasound (>20 kHz), which is above the human hearing range but within the dog's hearing range. [General Studies Reference, p. 124]

**Q17: Why is the depth of an ocean measured using SONAR?**
A: SONAR uses sound waves (which can travel through water) to determine the depth. The time taken by the sound to reflect from the ocean floor and return is used to calculate the depth. [General Studies Reference, p. 124-125]

**Q18: Why does water in a metal pipe burst during very cold nights?**
A: Water expands on freezing; the increase in volume can burst the pipe. [General Studies Reference, p. 103-105]

**Q19: Why is mercury used in thermometers?**
A: Mercury is a liquid at room temperature, has uniform expansion, does not stick to glass, has a high boiling point and low freezing point, is opaque and shiny (easily visible). [General Studies Reference, p. 101-102]

**Q20: Why is a fire near a sea coast cooler during the day?**
A: Cool sea breeze blows from the sea towards the land, reducing the temperature. [General Studies Reference, p. 107]

## 5. Solved Numerical Problems

### Problem 1 (Easy — Specific heat)
**How much heat is required to raise the temperature of 2 kg of water from 20°C to 80°C? (Specific heat of water = 4200 J/(kg·K))**

**Solution:**
$Q = mc\Delta T = 2 \times 4200 \times (80-20) = 2 \times 4200 \times 60 = 504,000$ J = 504 kJ.

### Problem 2 (Easy — Latent heat)
**How much heat is required to convert 500 g of ice at 0°C to water at 0°C?**

**Solution:**
$Q = mL_f = 0.5 \times 3.34 \times 10^5 = 1.67 \times 10^5$ J = 167 kJ.

### Problem 3 (Easy — Wave equation)
**A wave has a frequency of 500 Hz and a wavelength of 0.66 m. Find its speed.**

**Solution:**
$v = f\lambda = 500 \times 0.66 = 330$ m/s.

### Problem 4 (Medium — Calorimetry)
**A piece of iron of mass 200 g at 100°C is placed in 500 g of water at 20°C. Find the final temperature. (Specific heat of iron = 450 J/(kg·K), water = 4200 J/(kg·K))**

**Solution:**
Heat lost by iron = Heat gained by water
$m_i c_i (100 - T) = m_w c_w (T - 20)$
$0.2 \times 450 \times (100 - T) = 0.5 \times 4200 \times (T - 20)$
$90 (100 - T) = 2100 (T - 20)$
$9000 - 90T = 2100T - 42000$
$51000 = 2190T$
$T ≈ 23.3°C$

### Problem 5 (Medium — SONAR)
**A SONAR detects an echo from a submarine after 0.6 s. The speed of sound in water is 1500 m/s. Find the depth of the submarine.**

**Solution:**
Depth = (speed × time)/2 = (1500 × 0.6)/2 = 450 m.

### Problem 6 (Medium — Linear expansion)
**A steel rod is 2 m long at 20°C. It is heated to 100°C. Find the increase in length. (α for steel = $1.2 \times 10^{-5}$ /°C)**

**Solution:**
$\Delta L = L \alpha \Delta T = 2 \times 1.2 \times 10^{-5} \times 80 = 1.92 \times 10^{-3}$ m = 1.92 mm.

### Problem 7 (Hard — Thermodynamics)
**A Carnot engine operates between 500 K and 300 K. If it absorbs 1000 J of heat, find the work done and the heat rejected.**

**Solution:**
Efficiency $\eta = 1 - T_2/T_1 = 1 - 300/500 = 0.4$ (40%).
Work = η × Q₁ = 0.4 × 1000 = 400 J.
Heat rejected Q₂ = Q₁ - W = 1000 - 400 = 600 J.

### Problem 8 (Hard — Beat frequency)
**Two tuning forks vibrate at 256 Hz and 260 Hz. Find the number of beats heard per second.**

**Solution:**
$f_{beats} = |f_1 - f_2| = |256 - 260| = 4$ Hz. So 4 beats are heard per second.
