## 1. Comprehensive Theory

### 1.1 Why Carbon is Special

Carbon is the most versatile element. It forms a vast number of compounds (more than all other elements combined). The main reasons are:

1. **Tetravalency:** Carbon has 4 valence electrons, so it can form 4 covalent bonds.
2. **Catenation:** Carbon can bond to other carbon atoms to form long chains, branched chains, and rings. This property is called **self-linking** or **catenation**.
3. **Multiple bonding:** Carbon atoms can form single (C–C), double (C=C), or triple (C≡C) bonds.
4. **Strong C–C bonds:** The C–C bond is very strong, providing stability to the molecules.

Carbon occurs in 0.02% of Earth's crust (as carbonates, coal, petroleum) and 0.03% of the atmosphere (as CO₂).

### 1.2 Covalent Bonding in Carbon

Carbon does not form ionic compounds easily because:
- It is difficult for the 6-proton nucleus to hold 4 extra electrons (C⁴⁻).
- It requires too much energy to remove 4 electrons (C⁴⁺).

So carbon **shares** its 4 valence electrons with other atoms, forming **covalent bonds**.

| Type of bond | Sharing of electron pairs | Example |
|--------------|--------------------------|---------|
| Single bond | 1 shared pair (2 electrons) | C–C, C–H |
| Double bond | 2 shared pairs (4 electrons) | C=C, C=O |
| Triple bond | 3 shared pairs (6 electrons) | C≡C, C≡N |

**Properties of covalent compounds:**
- **Low melting and boiling points** (weak intermolecular forces).
- **Generally poor conductors of electricity** (no free ions).
- **Insoluble in polar solvents** (water) but soluble in organic solvents.

### 1.3 Allotropes of Carbon

**Allotropes** are different forms of the same element with different physical properties. Carbon exists in many allotropic forms:

1. **Diamond:**
   - Each carbon is bonded to 4 other carbons in a tetrahedral structure.
   - Hardest natural substance.
   - High melting point.
   - Does not conduct electricity (all valence electrons in bonds).
   - Used in jewellery, cutting tools, drill bits.

2. **Graphite:**
   - Each carbon is bonded to 3 other carbons in hexagonal layers.
   - The fourth electron is delocalised between layers → conducts electricity.
   - Layers can slide over each other → slippery, used as a lubricant.
   - Used in pencil leads, electrodes, lubricants.

3. **Fullerenes:**
   - Form a separate class of carbon allotropes.
   - **C-60 (Buckminsterfullerene):** 60 carbon atoms arranged in a football (soccer ball) shape.
   - Used in lubricants, drug delivery, superconductors.

4. **Other forms:** Coal, charcoal, coke, lamp black, carbon black, graphene, carbon nanotubes.

### 1.4 Hydrocarbons

**Hydrocarbons** are compounds of carbon and hydrogen only. They are the simplest organic compounds.

**Types:**

1. **Saturated hydrocarbons (Alkanes):** All bonds are single bonds (C–C). General formula: $C_nH_{2n+2}$. Example: Methane (CH₄), Ethane (C₂H₆).
   - Less reactive, undergo substitution reactions.
   - Burn with a clean flame.

2. **Unsaturated hydrocarbons:**
   - **Alkenes:** Contain at least one C=C double bond. General formula: $C_nH_{2n}$. Example: Ethene (C₂H₄).
   - **Alkynes:** Contain at least one C≡C triple bond. General formula: $C_nH_{2n-2}$. Example: Ethyne (C₂H₂).
   - More reactive, undergo addition reactions.
   - Burn with a sooty/smoky flame.

### 1.5 Functional Groups

The atom or group of atoms that determines the chemical properties of an organic compound is called a **functional group**.

| Functional group | Class | Example | IUPAC name |
|------------------|-------|---------|------------|
| –OH (hydroxyl) | Alcohol | CH₃OH (methanol) | Methanol |
| –CHO (aldehyde) | Aldehyde | CH₃CHO | Ethanal |
| >C=O (ketone) | Ketone | CH₃COCH₃ | Propanone |
| –COOH (carboxyl) | Carboxylic acid | CH₃COOH | Ethanoic acid |
| –X (halogen) | Haloalkane | CH₃Cl | Chloromethane |
| –NH₂ (amino) | Amine | CH₃NH₂ | Methanamine |

### 1.6 Homologous Series

A **homologous series** is a series of organic compounds having the same functional group, similar chemical properties, and successive members differing by –CH₂– group (14 atomic mass units).

**Characteristics of homologous series:**
- All members have the same functional group.
- They can be represented by a general formula.
- Successive members differ by CH₂ (14 u).
- They show gradation in physical properties (m.p., b.p., density increase with molecular mass).
- Chemical properties are similar.

**Example: Alkane homologous series**

| n | Formula | Name | Boiling point (K) |
|---|---------|------|---------------------|
| 1 | CH₄ | Methane | 111 |
| 2 | C₂H₆ | Ethane | 184 |
| 3 | C₃H₈ | Propane | 231 |
| 4 | C₄H₁₀ | Butane | 272 |
| 5 | C₅H₁₂ | Pentane | 309 |

### 1.7 IUPAC Nomenclature

**IUPAC (International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry)** rules for naming organic compounds:

1. Identify the **longest carbon chain** (parent chain).
2. Number the carbon atoms so that the functional group or substituent gets the **lowest number**.
3. Identify the **functional group** and prefix (for substituent) or suffix (for functional group).
4. Combine the name with the appropriate prefix/suffix and number.

**Common prefixes** (number of carbons):
- 1 = meth-, 2 = eth-, 3 = prop-, 4 = but-, 5 = pent-, 6 = hex-, 7 = hept-, 8 = oct-, 9 = non-, 10 = dec-

**Suffix based on functional group:**
- –OH → -ol (alcohol)
- –CHO → -al (aldehyde)
- >C=O → -one (ketone)
- –COOH → -oic acid (carboxylic acid)
- C=C → -ene (alkene)
- C≡C → -yne (alkyne)

**Examples:**
- CH₄ → Methane (only C–C and C–H)
- C₂H₆ → Ethane
- C₂H₄ → Ethene (C=C → ene)
- C₂H₂ → Ethyne (C≡C → yne)
- CH₃OH → Methanol
- CH₃CHO → Ethanal
- CH₃COOH → Ethanoic acid

### 1.8 Ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH)

**Properties of ethanol**:
- Colourless liquid with a pleasant smell.
- Boiling point: 78°C (low due to weak H-bonding, not as strong as in water).
- Soluble in water in all proportions (due to H-bonding with water).
- Density: 0.789 g/cm³ (lighter than water).
- A good solvent.

**Chemical properties of ethanol**:
1. **Reaction with sodium:** $2CH_3CH_2OH + 2Na \rightarrow 2CH_3CH_2ONa + H_2\uparrow$ (sodium ethoxide + hydrogen)
2. **Dehydration (with conc. H₂SO₄):** $CH_3CH_2OH \xrightarrow{conc. H_2SO_4, 170°C} CH_2=CH_2 + H_2O$ (ethene)
3. **Combustion:** $CH_3CH_2OH + 3O_2 \rightarrow 2CO_2 + 3H_2O$ (clean blue flame)
4. **Oxidation (with acidified K₂Cr₂O₇):** $CH_3CH_2OH \rightarrow CH_3CHO$ (ethanal) → $CH_3COOH$ (ethanoic acid)

**Uses of ethanol**:
- In alcoholic beverages (alcoholic content varies).
- As a solvent in medicines, paints, varnishes, perfumes.
- As a fuel (additive to petrol → "gasohol").
- In thermometers (formerly).
- As an antiseptic (in skin sanitizers).

**Harmful effects of ethanol:** Drinking alcohol damages the liver, causes addiction, impairs judgment and coordination. Long-term use causes serious health issues.

### 1.9 Ethanoic Acid (CH₃COOH)

**Properties of ethanoic acid**:
- Common name: Acetic acid (the acid in vinegar — 5-8% solution).
- Boiling point: 391 K (118°C). **Solid below 16.6°C; the solid is called glacial acetic acid.**
- Pungent, sour smell.
- Soluble in water.
- Turns blue litmus red (acidic).
- Reacts with alkalis to form salt and water.
- Reacts with carbonates and bicarbonates to release CO₂.

**Chemical properties of ethanoic acid**:
1. **Esterification (with alcohol):** $CH_3COOH + CH_3CH_2OH \xrightarrow{conc. H_2SO_4, \text{heat}} CH_3COOCH_2CH_3 + H_2O$
   (Ethyl ethanoate — sweet-smelling ester)
2. **Saponification (with NaOH):** $CH_3COOH + NaOH \rightarrow CH_3COONa + H_2O$ (sodium ethanoate)
3. **Reaction with carbonates:** $2CH_3COOH + Na_2CO_3 \rightarrow 2CH_3COONa + H_2O + CO_2\uparrow$

**Uses of ethanoic acid**:
- In vinegar (food preservative, flavouring).
- In manufacture of plastics, dyes, pharmaceuticals.
- As a laboratory reagent.
- As a coagulating agent for rubber latex.

### 1.10 Soaps and Detergents

#### Soap
A **soap** is a sodium or potassium salt of a long-chain fatty acid (e.g., sodium stearate, C₁₇H₃₅COONa).

**Soap is prepared by saponification:**
$\text{Fat/Oil} + \text{NaOH} \rightarrow \text{Soap} + \text{Glycerol}$

**Structure of soap:** A soap molecule has two parts:
- **Hydrophobic tail** (long hydrocarbon chain, C₁₇H₃₅) — dissolves in oil/grease.
- **Hydrophilic head** (–COONa) — dissolves in water.

**Cleansing action:** Soap molecules arrange themselves around an oil/grease droplet (forming a micelle) with hydrophobic tails inside and hydrophilic heads outside. The micelle is washed away with water, taking the dirt with it. Soap thus **emulsifies** the oil/grease and helps wash it off.

**Limitations of soaps:**
- Do not lather well in hard water (containing Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺). Insoluble calcium/magnesium salts (scum) form:
  $2C_{17}H_{35}COONa + Ca^{2+} \rightarrow (C_{17}H_{35}COO)_2Ca \downarrow + 2Na^+$

#### Detergents
**Detergents** are sodium salts of long-chain sulphonic acids (or alkyl hydrogen sulphates). They work like soaps but lather even in hard water.

**Advantages of detergents over soaps:**
- Work in hard water (no scum formation).
- Work even in acidic water.
- More soluble in water.
- Cheaper.

**Disadvantages:** Some detergents are non-biodegradable and cause water pollution.

### 1.11 Polymers

A **polymer** is a large molecule made up of many small repeating units called **monomers**. The process of joining monomers is called **polymerisation** [General Studies Reference, p. 182-183].

**Types of polymerisation:**

1. **Addition polymerisation:** Monomers add together without loss of any atom. Monomers must have a C=C double bond.
   - Example: Ethene → Polyethene.
     $n CH_2=CH_2 \rightarrow -(CH_2-CH_2)_n-$

2. **Condensation polymerisation:** Monomers join with the loss of a small molecule (usually water).
   - Example: Terylene (Dacron) is made from ethane-1,2-diol and terephthalic acid.

**Common polymers:**

| Polymer | Monomer | Use |
|---------|---------|-----|
| Polyethene (Polythene) | Ethene | Plastic bags, bottles, insulation |
| Polypropene | Propene | Ropes, carpets, containers |
| PVC (Polyvinyl chloride) | Vinyl chloride | Pipes, cable insulation |
| Polystyrene | Styrene | Packaging, disposable cups |
| Teflon (PTFE) | Tetrafluoroethene | Non-stick cookware |
| Terylene (Polyester) | — | Synthetic fibres, fabrics |
| Nylon | Adipic acid + Hexamethylenediamine | Cloth, ropes, parachutes |
| Natural rubber | Isoprene | Tyres, tubes |
| Bakelite | Phenol + Formaldehyde | Electrical switches, combs |

**Natural polymers:** Cotton (cellulose), silk, wool, rubber, starch, proteins, DNA.

**Synthetic polymers:** Polyethene, PVC, nylon, terylene, bakelite, neoprene.

| Reaction | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| $CH_4 + 2O_2 \rightarrow CO_2 + 2H_2O$ | Combustion of methane |

| $CH_3CH_2OH + 3O_2 \rightarrow 2CO_2 + 3H_2O$ | Combustion of ethanol |
| $CH_3COOH + CH_3CH_2OH \rightarrow CH_3COOCH_2CH_3 + H_2O$ | Esterification |
| $2CH_3COOH + Na_2CO_3 \rightarrow 2CH_3COONa + H_2O + CO_2$ | Acid-carbonate reaction |
| $2CH_3CH_2OH + 2Na \rightarrow 2CH_3CH_2ONa + H_2$ | Ethanol + sodium |
| $nCH_2=CH_2 \rightarrow -(CH_2-CH_2)_n-$ | Polymerisation of ethene |

**General formulas:**
- Alkanes: $C_nH_{2n+2}$
- Alkenes: $C_nH_{2n}$
- Alkynes: $C_nH_{2n-2}$

## 3. Everyday Science & Applications

**Q1: Why is the flame of a candle often yellow/luminous?**
A: Unsaturated hydrocarbons in the wax burn with a sooty/luminous flame due to incomplete combustion.

**Q2: Why does an LPG stove burn with a clean blue flame?**
A: LPG (liquefied petroleum gas, mainly butane) is a saturated hydrocarbon, burns with a clean blue flame due to complete combustion.

**Q3: Why do alkenes and alkynes burn with a sooty flame?**
A: They have a high carbon content, leading to incomplete combustion and soot.

**Q4: Why does graphite conduct electricity while diamond does not?**
A: In graphite, the fourth electron of each carbon is delocalised and can move freely, conducting electricity. In diamond, all electrons are tied up in covalent bonds, so no free electrons.

**Q5: Why is graphite used as a lubricant?**
A: The hexagonal layers of carbon in graphite can slide over each other easily, making it slippery.

**Q6: Why is vinegar used as a food preservative?**
A: Vinegar is dilute acetic acid (ethanoic acid). Its acidic nature prevents the growth of bacteria and fungi, preserving the food.

**Q7: Why does soap not lather in hard water?**
A: Hard water contains Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions, which react with soap to form insoluble calcium/magnesium salts (scum), reducing lather.

**Q8: Why are detergents preferred over soaps in hard water?**
A: Detergents form soluble salts with Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺, so they lather well even in hard water.

**Q9: Why is ethanol used as an additive in petrol?**
A: Ethanol is a clean-burning fuel; adding it to petrol increases octane rating and reduces pollution.

**Q10: Why is the consumption of alcohol harmful?**
A: Alcohol affects the liver, brain, heart, and other organs. Long-term use causes liver cirrhosis, mental impairment, and addiction.

**Q11: Why are synthetic detergents sometimes harmful to the environment?**
A: Some detergents are non-biodegradable; they do not break down in nature, causing water pollution and foaming in rivers.

**Q12: Why is a "micelle" formed when soap is added to oily water?**
A: Soap molecules arrange themselves in spherical micelles with hydrophobic tails inside (trapping oil) and hydrophilic heads outside (in water). The micelle is then washed away with water.

**Q13: Why is the sour taste of vinegar a clue that it contains an acid?**
A: All acids taste sour, and ethanoic acid (acetic acid) in vinegar is a weak acid.

**Q14: Why is ethanoic acid called a "weak" acid despite being a strong acid in water?**
A: In water, ethanoic acid is only partially ionised (CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺), so it is a weak acid. [Higher classes]

**Q15: Why do esters have a fruity smell?**
A: Esters are volatile and have a sweet, fruity odour. This is why many natural fragrances (fruits, flowers) contain esters.

**Q16: Why are polymers useful in everyday life?**
A: Polymers like plastic, rubber, and synthetic fibres are lightweight, strong, durable, corrosion-resistant, and easy to manufacture.

**Q17: Why is PVC used for electrical insulation?**
A: PVC (polyvinyl chloride) is a poor conductor of electricity, so it is used to insulate electric wires and cables.

**Q18: Why is Teflon used for non-stick cookware?**
A: Teflon (PTFE) has a very low coefficient of friction and is chemically inert, so food doesn't stick to it.

**Q19: Why is natural rubber vulcanised?**
A: Vulcanisation (heating with sulphur) makes rubber harder, more elastic, and resistant to temperature changes.

**Q20: Why does a methylated spirit burn with a blue flame?**
A: Methylated spirit is mostly ethanol, a saturated compound that burns completely with a clean blue flame.

## 4. Important Facts & One-Liners

1. **Atomic number of carbon:** 6.
2. **Carbon's valency:** 4 (tetravalent).
3. **Catenation:** Self-linking property of carbon.
4. **Carbon forms** covalent bonds by sharing electrons.
5. **Allotropes of carbon:** Diamond, graphite, fullerenes (C-60).
6. **Hardest natural substance:** Diamond.
7. **Graphite conducts electricity** (delocalised electrons).
8. **Buckminsterfullerene (C-60)** looks like a football.
9. **Saturated hydrocarbons:** Alkanes (general formula $C_nH_{2n+2}$).
10. **Unsaturated hydrocarbons:** Alkenes ($C_nH_{2n}$) and Alkynes ($C_nH_{2n-2}$).
11. **Saturated hydrocarbons burn with** a clean (blue) flame.
12. **Unsaturated hydrocarbons burn with** a sooty (yellow/smoky) flame.
13. **Functional group** determines the chemical properties.
14. **Homologous series** members differ by –CH₂– (14 u).
15. **Ethanol formula:** C₂H₅OH or CH₃CH₂OH.
16. **Ethanol boils at** 78°C.
17. **Ethanoic acid formula:** CH₃COOH.
18. **Ethanoic acid** is also called acetic acid (vinegar).
19. **Glacial acetic acid:** Pure ethanoic acid (solid below 16.6°C).
20. **Esterification:** Acid + Alcohol → Ester + Water.
21. **Soap** = Sodium/potassium salt of long-chain fatty acid.
22. **Saponification:** Fat + NaOH → Soap + Glycerol.
23. **Micelle:** Spherical arrangement of soap molecules around oil droplets.
24. **Detergents** work in hard water; soaps do not.
25. **Polymer:** Large molecule made of many monomers. [General Studies Reference, p. 182-183]
26. **Monomer:** Small repeating unit in a polymer. [General Studies Reference, p. 182-183]
27. **Addition polymerisation:** No atoms lost. [General Studies Reference, p. 182-183]
28. **Condensation polymerisation:** Small molecule (e.g., water) lost. [General Studies Reference, p. 182-183]
29. **Polyethene monomer:** Ethene. [General Studies Reference, p. 182-183]
30. **Natural rubber monomer:** Isoprene. [General Studies Reference, p. 184]
