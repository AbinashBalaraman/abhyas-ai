## 1. Comprehensive Theory

### 1.1 Physical Properties of Metals

Metals in their pure state have a shining surface called **metallic lustre**. Other physical properties:

| Property | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| **Malleability** | Can be beaten into thin sheets. Gold and silver are the most malleable. |
| **Ductility** | Can be drawn into thin wires. Gold is the most ductile (2 km of wire from 1 g of gold). |
| **Hardness** | Generally hard (varies from metal to metal). |
| **Conductivity** | Good conductors of heat and electricity. Best conductors: silver and copper. |
| **Sonority** | Produce a ringing sound on striking a hard surface. (Hence school bells are made of metals.) |
| **Melting/Boiling points** | Generally high (exceptions: gallium, caesium have very low melting points). |
| **Density** | Generally high (exceptions: alkali metals Li, Na, K have low density). |
| **State at room temperature** | Solid (except mercury, which is liquid). |
| **Colour** | Mostly grey/silvery (exceptions: gold is yellow, copper is reddish). |

### 1.2 Physical Properties of Non-metals

| Property | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| **Lustre** | Generally non-lustrous (exception: iodine is lustrous). |
| **Malleability/Ductility** | Non-malleable, non-ductile (brittle; break into pieces when hammered). |
| **Conductivity** | Poor conductors of heat and electricity (exception: graphite conducts electricity). |
| **Sonority** | Not sonorous. |
| **Melting/Boiling points** | Generally low. |
| **State at room temperature** | Solid, liquid, or gas. (Bromine is the only liquid non-metal; gases include O₂, N₂, H₂, Cl₂.) |
| **Colour** | Various; many are colourless. |

### 1.3 Exceptions
- **Mercury (Hg):** Liquid metal at room temperature.
- **Sodium, Potassium, Lithium:** Soft metals that can be cut with a knife; low melting points.
- **Gallium, Caesium:** Melt on the palm of the hand.
- **Iodine:** Lustrous non-metal.
- **Diamond:** Non-metal, hardest natural substance; high melting point.
- **Graphite:** Non-metal, conducts electricity.
- **Carbon:** Exists in many allotropic forms (diamond, graphite, fullerenes, etc.).

### 1.4 Chemical Properties of Metals

#### (a) Reaction with Oxygen
Almost all metals combine with oxygen to form **metal oxides**:

$$\text{Metal} + \text{Oxygen} \rightarrow \text{Metal oxide}$$

Examples:
- $2Cu + O_2 \rightarrow 2CuO$ (copper(II) oxide, black)
- $4Al + 3O_2 \rightarrow 2Al_2O_3$ (aluminium oxide)
- $2Mg + O_2 \rightarrow 2MgO$ (white dazzling flame)

**Metal oxides are generally basic** (soluble in water form hydroxides; react with acids to form salts and water). Some metal oxides (e.g., Al₂O₃, ZnO) are **amphoteric** (react with both acids and bases).

#### (b) Reaction with Water
Metals react with water to form **metal hydroxide + hydrogen gas**:
- $2Na + 2H_2O \rightarrow 2NaOH + H_2\uparrow$ (sodium — vigorous)
- $Ca + 2H_2O \rightarrow Ca(OH)_2 + H_2\uparrow$ (calcium)
- $Mg + 2H_2O \rightarrow Mg(OH)_2 + H_2\uparrow$ (slow with cold water, fast with steam)
- $Fe + H_2O \rightarrow Fe_3O_4 + 4H_2$ (iron with steam, red hot)
- Cu, Ag, Au do not react with water.

#### (c) Reaction with Acids
Metals react with dilute acids to form **salt + hydrogen gas**:
- $Zn + H_2SO_4 \rightarrow ZnSO_4 + H_2\uparrow$
- $Fe + 2HCl \rightarrow FeCl_2 + H_2\uparrow$

Reactivity with acids depends on the reactivity of the metal. Less reactive metals (Cu, Ag, Au) do not react with dilute acids.

#### (d) Reaction with Solutions of Other Metal Salts
More reactive metals displace less reactive metals from their salt solutions:
- $Fe + CuSO_4 \rightarrow FeSO_4 + Cu$ (iron displaces copper)
- $Cu + 2AgNO_3 \rightarrow Cu(NO_3)_2 + 2Ag$ (copper displaces silver)
- $Zn + CuSO_4 \rightarrow ZnSO_4 + Cu$

The arrangement of metals in the order of decreasing reactivity is called the **reactivity series**:

|--------|-------|-----------|
| K | Potassium | Most reactive |
| Na | Sodium | |
| Ca | Calcium | |
| Mg | Magnesium | |
| Al | Aluminium | |
| Zn | Zinc | |
| Fe | Iron | |
| Ni | Nickel | |
| Sn | Tin | |
| Pb | Lead | |
| H | Hydrogen | |
| Cu | Copper | |

| Au | Gold | Least reactive |

**Mnemonic:** "**K**aali **N**aani **C**hore **M**aate **A**ur **Z**oo **F**eero **N**ahi **S**unte **P**apa **H**ai **C**hore **M**ote **A**ur **A**mmi **S**at **Sun**ti **H**ai" (or similar Hindi/English phrases).

**Reactivity series based on reaction with:**
- **Oxygen:** Metals at the top react vigorously with O₂ (form oxides). Metals at the bottom are least reactive (Au, Ag, Pt do not react with O₂).
- **Water:** K, Na react with cold water. Ca reacts with cold water (slowly). Mg reacts with hot water/steam. Al, Zn, Fe react with steam. Cu, Ag, Au do not react.
- **Acids:** K, Na, Ca, Mg react with dilute acids vigorously. Al, Zn, Fe react moderately. Pb reacts slowly. Cu does not react with dilute HCl/H₂SO₄ but reacts with conc. HNO₃. Ag, Au do not react.
- **Salt solutions:** More reactive metal displaces less reactive metal from its salt solution.

### 1.6 Ionic Compounds

Ionic compounds are formed by the transfer of electrons from a metal to a non-metal. The metal atom loses electrons to form a **cation** (+), and the non-metal gains electrons to form an **anion** (-).

Example: Na + Cl → Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl (sodium chloride)

**Properties of ionic compounds**:
- **Solid at room temperature** (strong electrostatic forces).
- **High melting and boiling points.**
- **Soluble in water** (polar solvent).
- **Conduct electricity in molten state or aqueous solution** (ions are free to move).
- **Do not conduct electricity in solid state.**
- Generally **brittle** (break under stress due to shifting of ions).

### 1.7 Occurrence of Metals

Metals occur in the earth's crust in the form of **minerals** (naturally occurring compounds). The mineral from which a metal can be profitably extracted is called an **ore** [General Studies Reference, p. 117-118].

**Examples of ores:**

| Metal | Ore | Formula |
|-------|-----|---------|
| Iron | Haematite | Fe₂O₃ |
| Iron | Magnetite | Fe₃O₄ |
| Iron | Siderite | FeCO₃ |
| Aluminium | Bauxite | Al₂O₃·2H₂O |
| Aluminium | Corundum | Al₂O₃ |
| Copper | Copper pyrite | CuFeS₂ |
| Copper | Cuprite | Cu₂O |
| Zinc | Zinc blende | ZnS |
| Zinc | Calamine | ZnCO₃ |
| Tin | Cassiterite | SnO₂ |
| Lead | Galena | PbS |

The impurities (sand, rocks, etc.) in the ore are called **gangue** [General Studies Reference, p. 117-118].

### 1.8 Extraction of Metals

**Metallurgy:** The process of extracting metals from their ores. The basic steps are [General Studies Reference, p. 118-120]:

1. **Concentration of ore:** Removing gangue from the ore. Methods: hydraulic washing, magnetic separation, froth flotation, leaching.
2. **Extraction of crude metal:** Three steps:
   - **Conversion to oxide** (calcination for carbonates, roasting for sulphides).
   - **Reduction** of oxide to metal (using carbon, CO, Al, or other reducing agents).
   - **Refining** (purification of crude metal).
3. **Refining of crude metal:** Final purification. Methods: distillation, liquation, electrolytic refining, zone refining, vapour phase refining.

**Extraction of Iron [General Studies Reference, p. 103-104]:**

Iron is extracted from haematite (Fe₂O₃) in a **blast furnace**.

Raw materials: haematite, coke (C), limestone (CaCO₃), hot air.

Reactions:
1. $C + O_2 \rightarrow CO_2$
2. $CO_2 + C \rightarrow 2CO$
3. $Fe_2O_3 + 3CO \rightarrow 2Fe + 3CO_2$ (reduction)
4. $CaCO_3 \rightarrow CaO + CO_2$
5. $CaO + SiO_2 \rightarrow CaSiO_3$ (slag, removed)

The iron obtained is **pig iron** (contains ~4% carbon and other impurities). Cast iron, wrought iron, and steel are different forms with varying carbon content.

**Extraction of Copper [General Studies Reference, p. 105]:**

Copper is extracted from copper pyrite (CuFeS₂).

1. Concentration by froth flotation.
2. Roasting: $2CuFeS_2 + O_2 \rightarrow Cu_2S + 2FeS + SO_2$
3. Smelting: $Cu_2S + FeS \rightarrow$ matte + slag.
4. Partial roasting: $Cu_2S + 2Cu_2O \rightarrow 6Cu + SO_2$ (self-reduction).
5. Electrolytic refining: Pure copper at cathode, impure at anode in CuSO₄ + H₂SO₄ solution.

**Extraction of Zinc [General Studies Reference, p. 108]:**

Zinc is extracted from zinc blende (ZnS).

1. Roasting: $2ZnS + 3O_2 \rightarrow 2ZnO + 2SO_2$
2. Reduction: $ZnO + C \rightarrow Zn + CO$ (or using coke).
3. Electrolytic refining: Pure Zn at cathode, impure at anode in ZnSO₄ solution.

**Extraction of Aluminium [General Studies Reference, p. 101-102]:**

Aluminium is extracted from bauxite (Al₂O₃·2H₂O).

1. Purification of bauxite (Bayer's process — using NaOH).
2. Electrolysis of fused alumina (Hall-Héroult process):
   - Cathode: Carbon (graphite); Anode: Carbon (graphite)
   - Electrolyte: Pure Al₂O₃ dissolved in molten cryolite (Na₃AlF₆) + fluorspar (CaF₂)
   - Reaction: $2Al_2O_3 \xrightarrow{\text{electrolysis}} 4Al + 3O_2$

### 1.9 Corrosion

**Corrosion** is the slow deterioration of a metal (usually) by the action of air, moisture, or chemicals on its surface. The most common example is the **rusting of iron**:

$$4Fe + 3O_2 + 2xH_2O \rightarrow 2Fe_2O_3 \cdot xH_2O \text{ (Rust)}$$

Rust is hydrated iron(III) oxide (Fe₂O₃·xH₂O). It is brown and flaky.

**Conditions for rusting:** Both **oxygen (air) and water (moisture)** are necessary. Rusting does not occur in dry air or in water without dissolved oxygen.

**Prevention of rusting**:
- **Painting** (barrier to air and water).
- **Oiling/greasing** (machines, tools).
- **Galvanisation:** Coating iron with a layer of zinc (e.g., galvanized iron).
- **Chromium plating** (e.g., bicycle handle).
- **Alloying** (e.g., stainless steel — iron + chromium + nickel + carbon).
- **Tin plating** (e.g., food cans).
- **Electroplating** with a more reactive metal.
- **Cathodic protection** (using sacrificial anodes of magnesium or zinc).

**Corrosion of other metals:**
- **Copper** forms a green coat of basic copper carbonate (CuCO₃·Cu(OH)₂) on exposure to moist air — called **patina**.
- **Silver** tarnishes (black Ag₂S) in the presence of H₂S in air.
- **Aluminium** forms a thin, transparent, protective oxide layer (Al₂O₃) that prevents further corrosion.

### 1.10 Alloys

An **alloy** is a homogeneous mixture of two or more metals, or a metal and a non-metal. Alloys are made to enhance the properties of the constituent metals (e.g., strength, hardness, resistance to corrosion).

**Common alloys:**

| Alloy | Composition | Properties | Use |
|-------|-------------|-----------|-----|
| **Brass** | Cu (80%) + Zn (20%) | Hard, malleable, corrosion resistant | Utensils, decorative items |
| **Bronze** | Cu (80%) + Sn (20%) | Hard, tough, corrosion resistant | Statues, medals, bells |
| **Steel** | Fe + C (0.1-2%) | Hard, strong, less brittle | Construction, tools, vehicles |
| **Stainless steel** | Fe + Cr + Ni + C | Hard, corrosion resistant | Surgical instruments, utensils |
| **Solder** | Pb + Sn (50:50) | Low melting point | Joining metals, electronic work |
| **Alnico** | Al + Ni + Co + Fe | Strongly magnetic | Permanent magnets |
| **Duralumin** | Al + Cu + Mg + Mn | Light, strong | Aircraft bodies |

## 3. Everyday Science & Applications

**Q1: Why is copper used to make hot water tanks and not steel?**
A: Copper is a better conductor of heat and does not corrode easily. Steel would rust when exposed to hot water and air.

**Q2: Why is aluminium used for making aircraft bodies?**
A: Aluminium is light, strong, corrosion-resistant (due to Al₂O₃ layer), and a good conductor of heat.

**Q3: Why is gold used for making jewellery?**
A: Gold is highly malleable, ductile, lustrous, and does not tarnish (least reactive).

**Q4: Why does a copper vessel develop a green coating when left exposed to air?**
A: Copper reacts with CO₂, O₂, and moisture in air to form basic copper carbonate (CuCO₃·Cu(OH)₂) — the green patina.

**Q5: Why is mercury used in thermometers?**
A: Mercury is the only metal that is liquid at room temperature, has uniform expansion, does not stick to glass, has a high boiling point and low freezing point, and is opaque and shiny.

**Q6: Why is sodium stored in kerosene?**
A: Sodium is highly reactive; it reacts vigorously with oxygen, moisture, and CO₂ in the air. Kerosene prevents contact with air and moisture.

**Q7: Why does an iron nail rust in moist air but not in dry air?**
A: Both **water (moisture) and oxygen** are required for rusting. In dry air, water is absent, so rusting does not occur.

**Q8: Why is stainless steel preferred over iron for making utensils?**
A: Stainless steel (Fe + Cr + Ni) is corrosion-resistant due to chromium, while iron rusts easily.

**Q9: Why is an alloy stronger than the constituent metals?**
A: The atoms of the second metal/element disrupt the orderly arrangement of atoms in the pure metal, making it harder for the layers to slide.

**Q10: Why is a metal like sodium not used for making jewellery?**
A: Sodium is highly reactive (corrodes in air, reacts with water), soft, and has a low melting point.

**Q11: Why are electric wires coated with PVC/rubber?**
A: PVC/rubber is an insulator; it prevents electric shocks.

**Q12: Why are bells made of metals?**
A: Metals are sonorous — they produce a ringing sound when struck.

**Q13: Why is graphite used as a lubricant in machines?**
A: Graphite has layers that can slide over each other easily, making it slippery and a good lubricant.

**Q14: Why is galvanisation done on iron?**
A: Zinc is more reactive than iron. The zinc coating protects iron from rusting; even if scratched, zinc is sacrificed (sacrificial anode), protecting iron.

**Q15: Why does aluminium not corrode easily despite being highly reactive?**
A: Aluminium forms a thin, transparent, and tightly adhering layer of Al₂O₃ on its surface, which prevents further reaction with air and water.

**Q16: Why is silver not used for making electrical wires despite being a better conductor than copper?**
A: Silver is very expensive.

**Q17: Why does copper not react with dilute HCl but reacts with dilute HNO₃?**
A: Copper is below hydrogen in the reactivity series, so it does not displace H from dilute HCl. HNO₃ is an oxidising acid that reacts with copper even without displacing H₂.

**Q18: Why is galvanised iron used for roofing instead of plain iron?**
A: Galvanised iron is coated with zinc, which prevents rusting of iron and lasts much longer.

**Q19: Why is calcium stored in kerosene like sodium?**
A: Calcium is also a highly reactive metal that reacts with moisture and oxygen.

**Q20: Why are gold ornaments alloyed with copper?**
A: Pure gold (24 carat) is very soft; alloying with copper makes it harder, more durable, and less expensive while retaining gold's appearance.

## 5. Solved Numerical/Conceptual Problems

### Problem 1 (Easy — Reactivity series)
**A metal X displaces Y from its salt solution, but Y does not displace Z from its salt solution. What is the order of reactivity (most to least reactive)?**

**Solution:**
If X displaces Y, X is more reactive than Y. If Y does not displace Z, Z is more reactive than Y (or they are about the same).
Order: X > Y, Z > Y.
So Z > X > Y is possible if Z is most reactive.

### Problem 2 (Easy — Ionic compound)
**Write the formula of the ionic compound formed between sodium and chlorine.**

**Solution:**
Na loses 1 electron → Na⁺; Cl gains 1 electron → Cl⁻.
Formula: NaCl.

### Problem 3 (Medium — Displacement)
**Iron nails are placed in copper sulphate solution. What change do you observe?**

**Solution:**
The blue colour of CuSO₄ fades, and a reddish-brown deposit of copper forms on the iron nails. The solution becomes light green (FeSO₄).
Reaction: $Fe + CuSO_4 \rightarrow FeSO_4 + Cu$ (Fe is more reactive than Cu)

### Problem 4 (Medium — Corrosion)
**Why is an iron gate painted frequently?**

**Solution:**
The paint acts as a barrier between iron and air/moisture, preventing rusting.

### Problem 5 (Medium — Alloy composition)
**Name the alloy used for making bells. What is its composition?**

**Solution:**
Bells are commonly made of **bell metal** (a type of bronze): Cu (80%) + Sn (20%). It's hard, sonorous, and produces a clear ringing sound.

### Problem 6 (Medium — Extraction of Fe)
**Write the reaction by which iron is obtained from iron oxide in a blast furnace.**

**Solution:**
$Fe_2O_3 + 3CO \rightarrow 2Fe + 3CO_2$
CO acts as the reducing agent.

### Problem 7 (Medium — Extraction of Al)
**Why is cryolite added to alumina in the electrolytic extraction of aluminium?**

**Solution:**
- Alumina has a very high melting point (~2050°C); cryolite lowers the melting point to about 950°C, saving energy.
- Cryolite also increases the electrical conductivity of the melt.

### Problem 8 (Hard — Reactivity and extraction)
**Why is iron extracted using a blast furnace but aluminium by electrolysis?**

**Solution:**
Iron is less reactive; its oxide can be reduced by carbon (coke) in a blast furnace. Aluminium is highly reactive; its oxide cannot be reduced by carbon. Electrolysis of fused alumina (in cryolite) is needed.
