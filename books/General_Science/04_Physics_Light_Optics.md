## 1. Comprehensive Theory

### 1.1 Nature of Light

Light is the form of energy that enables vision. We see objects because light reflected (or scattered) from them enters our eyes. Light travels in straight lines — demonstrated by the sharp shadow cast by a small source through an opaque object. This straight-line path is shown as a **ray of light**. A bundle of rays is called a **beam of light**.

**Speed of light in vacuum:** $c = 3 \times 10^8$ m/s. Light travels fastest in vacuum. In air, the speed is only marginally less. Light reduces considerably in glass or water.

**Historical perspective:** In the 17th century, Newton proposed the corpuscular (particle) theory; Huygens proposed the wave theory. In the 19th century, Maxwell unified electricity, magnetism, and light as electromagnetic waves. In the early 20th century, Einstein's photoelectric effect (1905) revived the particle nature, leading to the quantum (dual) nature of light — light behaves as both wave and particle, reconciling both views.

### 1.2 Reflection of Light

A highly polished surface, such as a mirror, reflects most of the light falling on it. The two **laws of reflection** are:

1. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.
2. The incident ray, the normal to the mirror at the point of incidence, and the reflected ray all lie in the same plane.

**Plane mirror image properties:** Image is always **virtual and erect**; the size of the image is **equal to that of the object**; the image is **as far behind the mirror** as the object is in front of it; the image is **laterally inverted**.

**Lateral inversion** is the apparent left-right reversal of an object (e.g., the word "AMBULANCE" is written in mirror-image form on the front of ambulances so that it can be read correctly in the rear-view mirror of vehicles ahead).

### 1.3 Spherical Mirrors

The reflecting surface of a spherical mirror forms a part of a sphere. There are two types:

- **Concave mirror:** Reflecting surface curved inwards (faces the centre of the sphere).
- **Convex mirror:** Reflecting surface curved outwards (bulges towards the object).

**Key terminology**:

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Pole (P)** | Centre of the reflecting surface |
| **Centre of curvature (C)** | Centre of the sphere of which the mirror is a part |
| **Radius of curvature (R)** | Distance PC |
| **Principal axis** | Straight line passing through P and C |
| **Principal focus (F)** | Point where parallel rays converge (concave) or appear to diverge from (convex) |
| **Focal length (f)** | Distance PF |
| **Aperture** | Diameter of the reflecting surface |

For spherical mirrors of small apertures: $R = 2f$ — i.e., the focus lies midway between P and C.

### 1.4 Image Formation by Concave Mirror (Table 9.1)

| Position of object | Position of image | Size | Nature |
|---|---|---|---|
| At infinity | At F | Highly diminished, point-sized | Real, inverted |
| Beyond C | Between F and C | Diminished | Real, inverted |
| At C | At C | Same size | Real, inverted |
| Between C and F | Beyond C | Enlarged | Real, inverted |
| At F | At infinity | — | Image not formed |
| Between P and F | Behind mirror | Enlarged | Virtual, erect |

Source:

**Uses of concave mirrors:** Torches, search-lights, vehicle headlights (to get powerful parallel beams); shaving mirrors (to see a larger image of the face); dentist's mirrors (to see large images of teeth); large concave mirrors in solar furnaces to concentrate sunlight.

### 1.5 Image Formation by Convex Mirror (Table 9.2)

| Position of object | Position of image | Size | Nature |
|---|---|---|---|
| At infinity | At F, behind the mirror | Highly diminished, point-sized | Virtual, erect |
| Between infinity and pole P | Between P and F, behind the mirror | Diminished | Virtual, erect |

Source:

**Uses of convex mirrors:** Used as rear-view (wing) mirrors in vehicles because they always give an erect image and have a wider field of view. The famous convex mirror at Agra Fort, placed on a wall facing the Taj Mahal, allows visitors to see the full image of the Taj on a small mirror.

### 1.6 New Cartesian Sign Convention

For spherical mirrors:

1. The object is always placed to the **left** of the mirror (light falls from the left).
2. All distances parallel to the principal axis are measured from the **pole** of the mirror.
3. Distances measured to the **right of the origin (along +x-axis)** are taken as **positive**; those measured to the **left (along −x-axis)** are taken as **negative**.
4. Distances measured perpendicular to and **above the principal axis (+y-axis)** are taken as **positive**.
5. Distances measured perpendicular to and **below the principal axis (−y-axis)** are taken as **negative**.

### 1.7 Mirror Formula and Magnification

The relationship between object-distance (u), image-distance (v), and focal length (f) of a spherical mirror:

$$\frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}$$

**Magnification** is the ratio of the height of the image to the height of the object:

$$m = \frac{h'}{h} = -\frac{v}{u}$$

- A **negative** magnification → **real image**
- A **positive** magnification → **virtual image**

The height of the object is positive (above axis); image height is positive for virtual images and negative for real images.

### 1.8 Refraction of Light

When light travels obliquely from one transparent medium to another, it changes direction. This is called **refraction**. Light does not change direction when incident normally (perpendicular) to the interface.

**Examples of refraction in daily life:**
- A coin at the bottom of a bucket of water appears raised.
- A pencil partly immersed in water appears displaced at the air-water interface.
- Letters under a glass slab appear raised.

**Laws of refraction:**

1. The incident ray, the refracted ray, and the normal to the interface of two transparent media at the point of incidence, all lie in the same plane.
2. The ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is a constant, for the light of a given colour and for the given pair of media. This law is also known as **Snell's law of refraction**.

$$\frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \text{constant} = n_{21}$$

When a ray travels from a rarer medium to a denser medium, it **bends towards the normal** (e.g., air to glass). When it travels from a denser medium to a rarer medium, it **bends away from the normal** (e.g., glass to air).

### 1.9 Refractive Index

The refractive index of medium 2 with respect to medium 1:

$$n_{21} = \frac{\text{Speed of light in medium 1}}{\text{Speed of light in medium 2}} = \frac{v_1}{v_2}$$

The **absolute refractive index** of a medium (with respect to vacuum):

$$n_m = \frac{c}{v} = \frac{\text{Speed of light in vacuum}}{\text{Speed of light in the medium}}$$

**Optical density** is different from mass density. A medium with a larger refractive index is **optically denser**. Kerosene is optically denser than water but has lower mass density.

**Table 9.3 — Refractive indices:**

| Material | Refractive Index | Material | Refractive Index |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air | 1.0003 | Crown glass | 1.52 |
| Ice | 1.31 | Rock salt | 1.54 |
| Water | 1.33 | Carbon disulphide | 1.63 |
| Alcohol | 1.36 | Dense flint glass | 1.65 |
| Kerosene | 1.44 | Ruby | 1.71 |
| Fused quartz | 1.46 | Sapphire | 1.77 |
| Turpentine oil | 1.47 | **Diamond** | **2.42** |
| Benzene | 1.50 | | |

**Diamond's high refractive index (2.42)** causes total internal reflection of light inside the diamond, making it sparkle brilliantly.

### 1.10 Refraction by Spherical Lenses

A lens is a transparent material bound by two surfaces, of which one or both surfaces are spherical. Two types:

- **Convex (converging) lens:** Thicker at the middle, converges parallel rays to a focus.
- **Concave (diverging) lens:** Thinner at the middle, diverges parallel rays.

**Key terms:**

- **Centres of curvature (C₁, C₂):** Centres of the spheres of which the lens surfaces form parts.
- **Principal axis:** Imaginary line through the two centres of curvature.
- **Optical centre (O):** Central point of the lens; a ray through O passes without deviation.
- **Aperture:** Effective diameter of the lens.
- **Principal focus (F):** Point where parallel rays converge (convex) or appear to diverge from (concave). A lens has two principal foci, F₁ and F₂.
- **Focal length (f):** Distance of the principal focus from the optical centre.

**Image formation by convex lens (Table 9.4):**

| Position of object | Position of image | Relative size | Nature |
|---|---|---|---|
| At infinity | At focus F₂ | Highly diminished, point-sized | Real and inverted |
| Beyond 2F₁ | Between F₂ and 2F₂ | Diminished | Real and inverted |
| At 2F₁ | At 2F₂ | Same size | Real and inverted |
| Between F₁ and 2F₁ | Beyond 2F₂ | Enlarged | Real and inverted |
| At focus F₁ | At infinity | Image not formed | — |
| Between F₁ and O | On the same side as object | Enlarged | Virtual and erect |

**Image formation by concave lens (Table 9.5):** A concave lens **always gives a virtual, erect, and diminished image**, regardless of object position.

### 1.11 Lens Formula and Magnification

The lens formula:

$$\frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}$$

The magnification produced by a lens is related to object distance u and image distance v:

$$m = \frac{h'}{h} = \frac{v}{u}$$

**Sign convention for lenses:** Similar to mirrors but measurements are from the optical centre. Focal length of a convex lens is **positive**; concave lens is **negative**.

### 1.12 Power of a Lens

The power of a lens is the reciprocal of its focal length:

$$P = \frac{1}{f \text{ (in metres)}}$$

**SI unit:** Dioptre (D). $1 \text{ D} = 1 \text{ m}^{-1}$.

- Power of convex lens: **positive**
- Power of concave lens: **negative**

**Net power of lenses in contact:** $P = P_1 + P_2 + P_3 + \ldots$. This additive property makes it easy to design lens systems for cameras, microscopes, telescopes.

### 1.13 Total Internal Reflection (TIR)

A special case of refraction. When light travels from a denser medium to a rarer medium, the refracted ray bends away from the normal. As the angle of incidence increases, the angle of refraction also increases, until at the **critical angle** the refracted ray grazes the surface (angle of refraction = 90°). Beyond the critical angle, the light is reflected back into the denser medium — this is **total internal reflection**.

**Conditions for TIR:**
1. Light must travel from a denser medium to a rarer medium.
2. The angle of incidence must exceed the critical angle.

**Applications:**
- Brilliance of diamond (high refractive index → low critical angle).
- Optical fibres in telecommunications (light signal travels by repeated TIR).
- Mirages in deserts.
- Sparkling of air bubbles in water.

### 1.14 The Human Eye

The human eye is one of the most valuable and sensitive sense organs. It is like a camera; its lens system forms an image on a light-sensitive screen called the **retina**.

**Anatomy of the eye:**

- **Cornea:** Transparent bulge on the front surface; most refraction occurs here.
- **Iris:** Dark muscular diaphragm behind the cornea that controls the size of the **pupil**.
- **Pupil:** Variable aperture that regulates the amount of light entering the eye.
- **Crystalline (eye) lens:** Adjustable lens providing finer adjustment of focal length.
- **Ciliary muscles:** Modify the curvature of the eye lens.
- **Retina:** Delicate membrane with light-sensitive cells (rods and cones); forms the screen.
- **Optic nerve:** Carries electrical signals to the brain.

**Eye dimensions:** Approximately spherical, ~2.3 cm in diameter.

### 1.15 Power of Accommodation

The ability of the eye lens to adjust its focal length to focus objects at varying distances is called **accommodation**.

- When ciliary muscles are **relaxed**, the lens becomes **thin** → focal length **increases** → distant objects are seen clearly.
- When ciliary muscles **contract**, the lens becomes **thick** → focal length **decreases** → nearby objects are seen clearly.

**Key limits:**

- **Least distance of distinct vision (near point):** ~25 cm for a normal adult.
- **Far point:** Infinity for a normal eye.

A normal eye can see objects clearly between **25 cm and infinity**.

**Cataract:** With old age, the crystalline lens becomes milky and cloudy, causing partial or complete loss of vision. Treatable with cataract surgery.

### 1.16 Defects of Vision and Their Correction

There are three main refractive defects of vision:

#### (a) Myopia (Near-sightedness)
- Person can see nearby objects clearly but cannot see distant objects distinctly.
- Far point is nearer than infinity.
- Image of a distant object forms **in front of the retina**.
- Causes: (i) excessive curvature of the eye lens, (ii) elongation of the eyeball.
- **Correction: Concave lens of suitable power**.

#### (b) Hypermetropia (Far-sightedness)
- Person can see distant objects clearly but cannot see nearby objects distinctly.
- Near point is farther away than 25 cm.
- Image of a close object forms **behind the retina**.
- Causes: (i) focal length of the eye lens is too long, (ii) the eyeball is too small.
- **Correction: Convex lens of appropriate power**.

#### (c) Presbyopia
- Power of accommodation decreases with ageing.
- Near point gradually recedes; difficulty in seeing nearby objects.
- Caused by weakening of ciliary muscles and diminishing flexibility of the eye lens.
- **Correction: Bi-focal lenses** (concave upper portion for distant vision, convex lower for near vision).

Modern alternatives: contact lenses and surgical interventions.

### 1.17 Atmospheric Refraction

The atmosphere has varying optical density — refractive index decreases with altitude. Light from stars and the sun passes through varying refractive indices, causing bending of light.

**Effects:**

1. **Twinkling of stars:** The apparent position of a star keeps fluctuating as starlight passes through varying atmospheric layers, causing the star's apparent position to change rapidly.
2. **Advance sunrise and delayed sunset:** The sun is visible about 2 minutes before actual sunrise and 2 minutes after actual sunset due to atmospheric refraction.
3. **Apparent flattening of the sun's disc** at sunrise/sunset.

### 1.18 Dispersion of White Light

When white light passes through a glass prism, it splits into seven colours: **VIBGYOR** (Violet, Indigo, Blue, Green, Yellow, Orange, Red). The band of coloured components is called the **spectrum**.

**Reason:** Different colours bend through different angles because each has a different refractive index in the prism. **Red bends the least**; **violet bends the most**.

### 1.19 The Rainbow

A natural phenomenon caused by dispersion, refraction, and total internal reflection of sunlight by water droplets in the atmosphere after rain. The rainbow is a circular arc of seven colours, with red on the outer side and violet on the inner side.

### 1.20 Tyndall Effect and Scattering of Light

**Tyndall effect:** Scattering of light by colloidal particles (e.g., a beam of sunlight entering a dark room through a slit, scattering off dust particles in the air to make the beam visible).

**Scattering of light:** When light passes through a medium, the particles in the medium scatter the light. The amount of scattering depends on the **size of the particles** and the **wavelength of light**.

**Rayleigh's law (qualitative):** The intensity of scattered light is inversely proportional to the fourth power of wavelength. So shorter wavelengths (blue, violet) scatter much more than longer wavelengths (red).

**Why is the sky blue?** Air molecules (much smaller than the wavelength of visible light) scatter shorter wavelengths (blue) more than longer wavelengths. So the sky appears blue.

**Why is the sun red at sunrise and sunset?** At sunrise/sunset, sunlight travels a longer distance through the atmosphere, scattering away most of the blue light; the remaining light reaching our eyes is predominantly red/orange.

**Why are danger signals red?** Red light is scattered the least by fog and smoke, so it can be seen from a distance even in adverse conditions.

## 3. Everyday Science & Applications

**Q1: Why is a pencil partly immersed in water in a glass tumbler appears bent at the water surface?**
A: Light from the underwater part of the pencil bends as it leaves water (denser) and enters air (rarer). The apparent position of the underwater portion shifts, making the pencil look bent at the air-water interface.

**Q2: Why is the word "AMBULANCE" written in reverse/lateral-inverted form on the front of ambulances?**
A: So that it appears correctly oriented (readable) in the rear-view mirror of vehicles ahead, which produces a laterally inverted image.

**Q3: Why are concave mirrors used in vehicle headlights and torches?**
A: When the bulb is placed at the focus of a concave mirror, the reflected rays emerge as a powerful parallel beam, illuminating a far area efficiently.

**Q4: Why are convex mirrors used as rear-view mirrors in vehicles?**
A: Convex mirrors always form virtual, erect, and diminished images with a wider field of view, enabling the driver to see a larger area behind.

**Q5: Why does a swimming pool appear shallower than its actual depth?**
A: Light rays from the bottom of the pool bend away from the normal as they leave the water, making the bottom appear raised.

**Q6: Why does a coin placed at the bottom of a bucket disappear when viewed from the side, but reappears when water is poured in?**
A: Empty bucket: light from the coin travels in a straight line and doesn't reach the eye. With water, the light from the coin refracts (bends) at the water-air interface, allowing it to reach the eye.

**Q7: Why is the sky blue?**
A: Air molecules scatter the shorter wavelengths (blue) of sunlight more than longer wavelengths (Rayleigh scattering).

**Q8: Why does the sun appear red at sunrise and sunset?**
A: At these times, sunlight travels a longer distance through the atmosphere, scattering away all the blue and shorter wavelengths, leaving predominantly red/orange light.

**Q9: Why do stars twinkle but planets do not (mostly)?**
A: Stars are point sources, so their light is significantly affected by atmospheric refraction. Planets are extended sources; the average position appears steady.

**Q10: Why is the sun visible 2 minutes before actual sunrise and 2 minutes after actual sunset?**
A: Atmospheric refraction bends sunlight over the horizon, allowing us to see the sun even when it is slightly below the horizon.

**Q11: Why is a rainbow formed after rain?**
A: Water droplets in the atmosphere disperse and refract sunlight, with each droplet acting as a tiny prism, producing the seven colours (VIBGYOR).

**Q12: Why does a diamond sparkle brilliantly?**
A: Diamond has a very high refractive index (2.42), so the critical angle for total internal reflection is very low. Light entering the diamond undergoes multiple TIRs, concentrating the light, and emerges from only a few faces, making it sparkle.

**Q13: Why is the power of a convex lens positive and concave lens negative?**
A: Convex lens converges light to a real focus (in front of the lens, by convention positive direction). Concave lens diverges light, with virtual focus on the same side as the object (negative direction).

**Q14: Why does an optician add lens powers algebraically?**
A: When lenses are in contact, the combined power is the algebraic sum of individual powers, simplifying prescription.

**Q15: Why does our eye become unable to focus on nearby objects in old age (presbyopia)?**
A: With age, ciliary muscles weaken and the eye lens loses flexibility, reducing the power of accommodation.

**Q16: Why is red light used in danger signals?**
A: Red light has the longest wavelength among visible colours and is scattered the least by fog, smoke, and dust, so it can be seen from a far distance.

**Q17: Why does the sun appear white at noon but red at sunrise/sunset?**
A: At noon, sunlight travels a shorter distance through the atmosphere; all colours reach our eyes, blending to appear white. At sunrise/sunset, only red wavelengths reach us.

**Q18: Why is a concave mirror used as a dentist's mirror?**
A: A concave mirror produces an enlarged, virtual, erect image of the teeth when placed close to the patient's mouth, allowing the dentist to see fine details.

**Q19: Why does the bottom of a tank filled with water appear raised?**
A: Light from the bottom bends as it leaves the water (denser medium) and enters air (rarer), making the bottom appear at a shallower depth.

**Q20: Why does the sun's disc appear flattened at sunrise and sunset?**
A: Atmospheric refraction is greater near the horizon than at the zenith, so the lower edge of the sun is refracted more than the upper edge, flattening its apparent shape.

## 5. Solved Numerical Problems

### Problem 1 (Easy — Mirror formula)
**A concave mirror has a focal length of 10 cm. Where should the object be placed to get a 2× magnified real image?**

**Solution:**
For a real image with a concave mirror, m = -2 (negative because real).
$m = -v/u = -2 \Rightarrow v = 2u$
$\frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}$
$\frac{1}{2u} + \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{10}$
$\frac{1 + 2}{2u} = \frac{1}{10} \Rightarrow \frac{3}{2u} = \frac{1}{10} \Rightarrow u = 15$ cm
**Object distance = -15 cm** (in front of mirror).

### Problem 2 (Easy — Convex mirror)
**A convex mirror used as a rear-view mirror has a radius of curvature of 3 m. A truck is 5 m away. Find the position, nature, and size of the image.**

**Solution:**
$R = +3$ m, $f = R/2 = +1.5$ m, $u = -5$ m.
$\frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{f} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{1.5} - \frac{1}{-5} = \frac{1}{1.5} + \frac{1}{5} = \frac{5 + 1.5}{7.5} = \frac{6.5}{7.5}$
$v = +7.5/6.5 = +1.15$ m
The image is 1.15 m behind the mirror (virtual, erect, diminished).
$m = -v/u = -1.15/-5 = +0.23$ → image is ~0.23× the size of the object (erect, virtual).

### Problem 3 (Medium — Concave mirror magnification)
**An object 4 cm tall is placed 25 cm in front of a concave mirror of focal length 15 cm. Find the position, size, and nature of the image.**

**Solution:**
$h = +4$ cm, $u = -25$ cm, $f = -15$ cm.
$\frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{f} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{-15} - \frac{1}{-25} = -\frac{1}{15} + \frac{1}{25}$
$= \frac{-5 + 3}{75} = -\frac{2}{75}$
$v = -75/2 = -37.5$ cm
The image is 37.5 cm in front of the mirror (real).
$m = -v/u = -(-37.5)/(-25) = -1.5$
$h' = m \times h = -1.5 \times 4 = -6$ cm → image is 6 cm tall, inverted, real.

### Problem 4 (Medium — Lens)
**A concave lens has focal length 15 cm. At what distance should the object be placed so that an image forms at 10 cm from the lens? Find the magnification.**

**Solution:**
For a concave lens, $f = -15$ cm; the image is on the same side as the object, so $v = -10$ cm.
$\frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}$
$\frac{1}{-10} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{-15}$
$-\frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{-15} - \frac{1}{-10} = -\frac{1}{15} + \frac{1}{10} = \frac{-2 + 3}{30} = \frac{1}{30}$
$u = -30$ cm → object is 30 cm in front of the lens.
$m = v/u = -10/-30 = +0.33$ → image is 0.33× size, virtual and erect.

### Problem 5 (Medium — Convex lens)
**A 2 cm tall object is placed 15 cm from a convex lens of focal length 10 cm. Find the position, size, and nature of the image.**

**Solution:**
$h = +2$ cm, $u = -15$ cm, $f = +10$ cm.
$\frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{f} + \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{10} + \frac{1}{-15} = \frac{3 - 2}{30} = \frac{1}{30}$
$v = +30$ cm → real image 30 cm on the other side.
$h' = h(v/u) = 2 \times (30/-15) = -4$ cm → 4 cm tall, inverted, real.
$m = v/u = 30/-15 = -2$ → 2× magnified (inverted).

### Problem 6 (Medium — Power of lens)
**A lens has a power of +2.5 D. What is its focal length? What type of lens is it?**

**Solution:**
$P = 1/f \Rightarrow f = 1/P = 1/2.5 = 0.4$ m = 40 cm.
Positive power → convex (converging) lens.

### Problem 7 (Hard — Refraction)
**The speed of light in a transparent medium is 2 × 10⁸ m/s. Calculate the refractive index of the medium. (c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s)**

**Solution:**
$n_m = c/v = (3 \times 10^8)/(2 \times 10^8) = 1.5$
The medium has refractive index 1.5 (similar to glass/crown glass).

### Problem 8 (Hard — Combined lens)
**Two thin lenses of powers +2.0 D and -0.5 D are placed in contact. Find the focal length of the combination.**

**Solution:**
$P_{net} = P_1 + P_2 = 2.0 + (-0.5) = +1.5$ D.
$f = 1/P_{net} = 1/1.5 = 0.667$ m ≈ 66.7 cm.
The combination is converging (positive power).
