## 1. Comprehensive Theory

### 1.1 Chemical Reactions and Equations (Ch 1)

A chemical reaction is a process in which the nature and identity of the initial substances change. Indicators of a chemical reaction: change in state, change in colour, evolution of a gas, change in temperature.

**Writing a chemical equation** uses symbols and formulae instead of words. A balanced chemical equation has the same number of atoms of each element on both sides (obeying the law of conservation of mass). Steps: draw boxes around formulae, list atoms on each side, balance by hit-and-trial, add state symbols (s), (l), (g), (aq).

**Types of chemical reactions:**

1. **Combination reaction:** Two or more reactants form a single product.
   $\text{CaO(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \rightarrow \text{Ca(OH)}_2\text{(aq)} + \text{Heat}$
   Burning of magnesium: $2\text{Mg} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{MgO}$

2. **Decomposition reaction:** Single reactant breaks into two or more products (opposite of combination). Requires heat (thermolysis), light (photolysis), or electricity (electrolysis).
   $2\text{Pb(NO}_3)_2 \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{PbO} + 4\text{NO}_2 + \text{O}_2$
   $2\text{AgCl} \xrightarrow{\text{Sunlight}} 2\text{Ag} + \text{Cl}_2$ (used in black-and-white photography)

3. **Displacement reaction:** More reactive element displaces a less reactive element from its compound.
   $\text{Fe} + \text{CuSO}_4 \rightarrow \text{FeSO}_4 + \text{Cu}$
   (Iron displaces copper because Fe is more reactive)

4. **Double displacement reaction:** Exchange of ions between two compounds.
   $\text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4 + \text{BaCl}_2 \rightarrow \text{BaSO}_4 \downarrow + 2\text{NaCl}$ (precipitation reaction)

5. **Redox reactions:** Both oxidation and reduction occur together. Oxidation = gain of oxygen OR loss of hydrogen. Reduction = loss of oxygen OR gain of hydrogen. The substance that brings about oxidation is the oxidising agent; the one that brings about reduction is the reducing agent.
   Example: $\text{CuO} + \text{H}_2 \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{Cu} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$ (CuO is reduced, H₂ is oxidised).

**Other concepts:**
- **Rancidity:** Spoilage of food by oxidation of fats/oils; prevented by adding antioxidants, refrigeration, vacuum packing, replacing air with N₂.
- **Corrosion:** Metals attacked by substances in environment; e.g., rusting of iron (formation of hydrated iron(III) oxide Fe₂O₃·xH₂O).

### 1.2 Acids, Bases and Salts (Ch 2)

**Indicators:** Substances that indicate acidic or basic nature by colour change.
- **Litmus:** Purple (natural); turns red in acid, blue in base.
- **Turmeric:** Yellow stain turns reddish-brown with soap (basic).
- **Methyl orange:** Red in acid, yellow in base.
- **Phenolphthalein:** Colourless in acid, pink in base.

**Chemical properties of acids:**
1. Acid + Metal → Salt + H₂↑
   $\text{Zn} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow \text{ZnSO}_4 + \text{H}_2$ (Test for H₂: burns with 'pop' sound)
2. Acid + Metal carbonate → Salt + CO₂ + H₂O
   $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 + 2\text{HCl} \rightarrow 2\text{NaCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2$ (CO₂ turns lime water milky)
3. Acid + Metal hydrogencarbonate → Salt + H₂O + CO₂
   $\text{NaHCO}_3 + \text{HCl} \rightarrow \text{NaCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2$
4. Acid + Metal oxide → Salt + H₂O
   $\text{CuO} + 2\text{HCl} \rightarrow \text{CuCl}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$
5. Acid + Base → Salt + Water (Neutralisation)

**What do all acids have in common?** All acids produce H⁺(aq) ions in aqueous solution. The H⁺ ion cannot exist alone, so it combines with water to form H₃O⁺ (hydronium ion).

**Chemical properties of bases:**
1. Base + Metal → Salt + H₂ (only for certain metals like Al, Zn with NaOH)
   $2\text{NaOH} + \text{Zn} \rightarrow \text{Na}_2\text{ZnO}_2 + \text{H}_2$
2. Base + Non-metal oxide → Salt + Water
   $\text{Ca(OH)}_2 + \text{CO}_2 \rightarrow \text{CaCO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O$
3. Base + Acid → Salt + Water (Neutralisation)
4. On heating, certain bases decompose (e.g., hydroxides of Cu, Al, Zn, Fe give oxides + H₂O).

**What do all bases have in common?** All bases produce OH⁻(aq) ions in aqueous solution.

**pH Scale:**
- pH = -log[H⁺]; range 0 (most acidic) to 14 (most basic); 7 is neutral.
- Acidic: pH < 7; Basic: pH > 7; Neutral: pH = 7.
- pH of solutions: HCl = 0; Lemon juice = 2.2; Vinegar = 3; Tomato = 4; Coffee = 5; Saliva (healthy) = 7.4; Blood = 7.4; Milk of magnesia = 10; NaOH = 14.
- The strength of an acid/base depends on the extent of ionisation (HCl is strong, acetic acid is weak).

**Importance of pH:**
- Acid rain (pH < 5.6) damages buildings, statues, crops.
- Soil pH affects plant growth; farmers add lime/organic matter to acidic soil.
- Our stomach produces HCl; acidity is treated with milk of magnesia (Mg(OH)₂) or antacids.
- Tooth decay begins when pH in mouth falls below 5.5; bacteria produce acids from sugar.
- Self-defense by animals: bee sting (formic acid → treat with baking soda); nettle sting (methanoic acid → treat with dock leaves).

**Salts:** Ionic compounds formed from acid + base neutralisation.
- **Common salt (NaCl):** From HCl + NaOH; source of Na, NaOH, Na₂CO₃, NaHCO₃.
- **Sodium hydroxide (NaOH):** Chlor-alkali process (electrolysis of brine, NaCl(aq)); used for soap, paper, textiles.
- **Sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O — washing soda):** Solvay process; used in glass, soap, paper, water softening.
- **Sodium hydrogencarbonate (NaHCO₃ — baking soda):** Mild, non-corrosive basic salt; used in baking (CO₂ makes cakes fluffy), antacids, fire extinguishers.
- **Plaster of Paris (CaSO₄·½H₂O):** From heating gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) at 373 K; used for casts, statues; sets to hard mass CaSO₄·2H₂O on adding water.
  $\text{CaSO}_4 \cdot 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{373K} \text{CaSO}_4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}\text{H}_2\text{O} + 1\tfrac{1}{2}\text{H}_2\text{O}$

### 1.3 Metals and Non-metals (Ch 3)

**Physical properties of metals:**
- Lustrous (shining), malleable (beaten into sheets), ductile (drawn into wires), sonorous (ringing sound), hard, high density, high melting/boiling points.
- Good conductors of heat and electricity.
- **Exceptions:** Mercury is liquid at room temperature; sodium/potassium can be cut with a knife; gallium and caesium have low melting points; iodine is lustrous but non-metal.

**Physical properties of non-metals:** Dull, non-malleable (brittle), non-ductile, poor conductors (except graphite), low melting/boiling points, non-sonorous.

**Chemical properties of metals:**
1. **Reaction with oxygen:** Metal + O₂ → Metal oxide (basic in nature, except amphoteric like Al₂O₃, ZnO)
   $4\text{Al} + 3\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$
2. **Reaction with water:** Metal + H₂O → Metal oxide/Hydroxide + H₂
   $2\text{Na} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 2\text{NaOH} + \text{H}_2$ (vigorous); $2\text{K}$ (very vigorous; catches fire); $2\text{Ca} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{Ca(OH)}_2 + \text{H}_2$ (less vigorous); $3\text{Fe} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O(steam)} \rightarrow \text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4 + 4\text{H}_2$
3. **Reaction with acids:** Metal + Dilute acid → Salt + H₂
   $\text{Mg} + 2\text{HCl} \rightarrow \text{MgCl}_2 + \text{H}_2$
4. **Reaction with salt solutions:** More reactive metal displaces less reactive metal.

**Reactivity series (most → least reactive):** K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Pb, Cu, Hg, Ag, Au.

**Extraction of metals from ores:**
- **Steps:** (i) Enrichment of ore; (ii) Conversion to oxide (calcination for carbonates, roasting for sulphides); (iii) Reduction (with C, CO, or other metals for reactive ones like Na, K, Ca, Mg — by electrolysis); (iv) Refining (electrolytic refining).
- **Thermite reaction:** $2\text{Al} + \text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 \rightarrow 2\text{Fe} + \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ + Heat (used for welding railway tracks).
- **Anodising:** Aluminium articles made anode; electrolysis with dilute H₂SO₄ makes thick Al₂O₃ layer (resists corrosion, can be dyed).
- **Alloys:** Homogeneous mixture of two or more metals (or metal + non-metal). Examples: Brass (Cu + Zn), Bronze (Cu + Sn), Solder (Pb + Sn), Duralumin (Al + Cu + Mg + Mn), Steel (Fe + C), Stainless steel (Fe + Cr + Ni).

### 1.4 Carbon and its Compounds (Ch 4)

Carbon is tetravalent (4 valence electrons); it shares electrons to form **covalent bonds** rather than ionic bonds (because it cannot lose/gain 4 electrons easily).

**Bonding:** Single bond (–), double bond (=), triple bond (≡). Covalent compounds have low melting/boiling points, are poor conductors, and have strong bonds within molecules but weak intermolecular forces.

**Allotropes of carbon:**
- **Diamond:** Hardest natural substance; sp³ hybridisation; 3D tetrahedral network; bad conductor.
- **Graphite:** Smooth, slippery, good conductor (delocalised electrons); sp² hybridisation; hexagonal layers.
- **Fullerenes:** C-60 (Buckminsterfullerene, "Bucky ball"), C-70, etc.; cage-like structures.

**Versatile nature of carbon:** Forms millions of compounds due to (i) tetravalency, (ii) catenation (self-linking into long chains), (iii) formation of multiple bonds, (iv) formation of isomers.

**Hydrocarbon families:**
- **Alkanes:** CₙH₂ₙ₊₂, single bonds (saturated) — methane CH₄, ethane C₂H₆, propane C₃H₈, butane C₄H₁₀
- **Alkenes:** CₙH₂ₙ, one double bond — ethene C₂H₄
- **Alkynes:** CₙH₂ₙ₋₂, one triple bond — ethyne C₂H₂

**Functional groups:**
- Alcohol: –OH (methanol, ethanol CH₃CH₂OH)
- Aldehyde: –CHO (ethanal CH₃CHO)
- Ketone: >C=O (propanone)
- Carboxylic acid: –COOH (acetic acid CH₃COOH)
- Haloalkane: –X (chloro-, bromo-)

**Ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH):**
- Liquid at room temperature; soluble in water; no reaction with sodium alone (but reacts with Na to form sodium ethoxide + H₂).
- Used in: alcoholic drinks, medicines (cough syrups, tincture iodine), antiseptic, solvent, fuel (with petrol).
- Harmful: damages liver, impairs judgement.

**Ethanoic acid (CH₃COOH — acetic acid):**
- 5-8% solution is vinegar; pure form is glacial acetic acid (freezes at 16.6°C).
- Reacts with: (i) metal → H₂; (ii) base → salt + water; (iii) carbonate → CO₂; (iv) alcohol (with conc. H₂SO₄ catalyst) → ester (esterification). E.g., CH₃COOH + CH₃CH₂OH → CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + H₂O.

**Saponification:** Ester + NaOH → Alcohol + Sodium salt of acid (soap). Long-chain fatty acid + NaOH → Soap (sodium stearate).
- Soap: sodium/potassium salt of long-chain fatty acid; –COO⁻Na⁺ (hydrophilic head) + long hydrocarbon chain (hydrophobic tail).
- **Cleansing action:** Soap micelles trap oil/dirt in centre (hydrophobic tails) and exterior (hydrophilic heads) allows water to wash away.
- **Detergents:** Sodium salts of long-chain benzene sulphonic acid; effective even in hard water.

## 3. Everyday Science & Applications

1. **Why does food become rancid?** Oxidation of fats and oils; prevented by antioxidants.
2. **Why are articles made of iron coated with zinc or painted?** To prevent rusting (corrosion in presence of O₂ and H₂O).
3. **Why does a baker keep baking soda in the fridge?** To neutralise acidic odours.
4. **Why does a person with acidity take milk of magnesia?** Mg(OH)₂ neutralises excess HCl in stomach.
5. **Why does tooth paste contain baking soda?** To neutralise acids in the mouth; prevent tooth decay.
6. **Why is stainless steel used in surgical instruments?** It doesn't corrode (contains Cr which forms Cr₂O₃ protective layer).
7. **Why is mercury used in thermometers?** Liquid metal with high density; uniform expansion.
8. **Why are aluminium foils used to wrap food?** Non-toxic, malleable, corrosion-resistant.
9. **Why are bronze statues greener than bronze-coloured?** Formation of basic copper carbonate (CuCO₃·Cu(OH)₂) by reaction with O₂, H₂O, CO₂ in air.
10. **Why are food cans often tin-plated or zinc-plated?** To prevent corrosion; tin and zinc are less reactive.
11. **Why does a soap solution become cloudy in hard water?** Hard water contains Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ which form insoluble Ca/Mg salts of fatty acids.
12. **Why is graphite used as a lubricant?** Its layered structure allows layers to slide over each other.
13. **Why is diamond used in cutting tools?** Hardest natural substance.
14. **Why is ethanol added to gasoline?** Increases octane number; reduces pollution.
15. **Why is vinegar used to preserve pickles?** Acetic acid inhibits bacterial growth (pH ~3).
16. **Why is baking soda used in fire extinguishers?** NaHCO₃ produces CO₂ on heating: $2\text{NaHCO}_3 \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2$ (CO₂ displaces O₂).
17. **Why is lemon juice added to prevent browning of cut fruits?** Ascorbic acid (and citric acid) inhibit oxidation by polyphenol oxidase.
18. **Why does a tarnished silver article become shiny when rubbed with toothpaste?** Toothpaste contains mild abrasives that polish the surface; the abrasive removes Ag₂S tarnish layer.
19. **Why is Plaster of Paris used in hospitals for setting broken bones?** It sets to a hard mass (gypsum) by absorbing water.
20. **Why is sodium stored in kerosene?** Sodium reacts vigorously with water/air; kerosene excludes both.

## 4. Important Facts & One-Liners

- Law of conservation of mass: mass is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.
- Redox = oxidation + reduction simultaneously.
- Rancidity: oxidative spoilage of food.
- All acids produce H⁺(aq) in water; all bases produce OH⁻(aq).
- pH scale: 0-14, with 7 neutral; below 7 acidic, above 7 basic.
- Tooth decay starts at pH < 5.5.
- Common salt (NaCl) formula; important for NaOH, Na₂CO₃, NaHCO₃ production.
- Plaster of Paris: CaSO₄·½H₂O; sets to gypsum CaSO₄·2H₂O.
- Amphoteric oxides (e.g., Al₂O₃, ZnO) react with both acids and bases.
- Reactivity series: K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au.
- Highly reactive metals (K, Na, Ca, Mg) extracted by electrolysis; middle (Zn, Fe, Sn) by reduction; least (Cu, Ag, Au) by heating or found native.
- Carbon's tetravalency + catenation → millions of compounds.
- Diamond and graphite are allotropes of carbon.
- Saturated hydrocarbons burn with a clear (non-sooty) flame; unsaturated burn with a sooty/yellow flame.
- Soap is sodium/potassium salt of long-chain fatty acid; detergent is sodium alkyl benzene sulphonate.
- Hardness of water due to Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ salts; soap forms scum; detergents don't.
- Functional group of alcohol: –OH; aldehyde: –CHO; carboxylic acid: –COOH.
- Glacial acetic acid: pure ethanoic acid (freezes at 16.6°C).

## 5. Solved Numerical / Conceptual Problems

**Problem 1 (Balancing equations):** Balance the equation: $\text{Fe} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4 + \text{H}_2$

**Solution:** $3\text{Fe} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4 + 4\text{H}_2$

**Problem 2 (HCl reaction):** What mass of H₂ is produced when 2 g of Zn reacts with excess dilute H₂SO₄? (Molar mass: Zn = 65 g/mol, H₂ = 2 g/mol)

**Solution:** $\text{Zn} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow \text{ZnSO}_4 + \text{H}_2$. 1 mol Zn → 1 mol H₂. So 2 g Zn = 2/65 mol → 2/65 mol H₂ = 4/65 ≈ 0.062 g H₂.

**Problem 3 (pH concept):** Solution A has pH 3; solution B has pH 9. Which is more acidic? What is the ratio of H⁺ ion concentration?

**Solution:** Solution A is more acidic. [H⁺] ratio = $10^{(9-3)} = 10^6: 1$ (B has million times fewer H⁺).

**Problem 4 (Reactivity):** Why does copper not react with dilute HCl but zinc does?

**Solution:** Copper is below hydrogen in the reactivity series; cannot displace H₂ from acids. Zinc is above hydrogen, can displace.

**Problem 5 (Alkane formula):** What is the formula of the alkane with 5 carbon atoms?

**Solution:** CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ with n=5: C₅H₁₂ (pentane).

**Problem 6 (Saponification):** Write equation for saponification of glyceryl oleate (C₁₇H₃₃COO)₃C₃H₅.

**Solution:** $(\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{33}\text{COO})_3\text{C}_3\text{H}_5 + 3\text{NaOH} \rightarrow 3\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{33}\text{COONa} + \text{C}_3\text{H}_5(\text{OH})_3$. (Soap: sodium oleate; Glycerol: by-product.)

**Problem 7 (Esterification):** What is the ester formed when acetic acid reacts with ethanol?

**Solution:** $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{OH} \xrightarrow{\text{conc. H}_2\text{SO}_4} \text{CH}_3\text{COOCH}_2\text{CH}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$ (Ethyl ethanoate, sweet smell).
