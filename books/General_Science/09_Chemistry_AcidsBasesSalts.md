## 1. Comprehensive Theory

### 1.1 Indicators

Indicators are substances that indicate the presence of an acid or a base by a change in colour (or odour).

**Types of indicators:**

1. **Natural indicators:**
   - **Litmus** (extracted from lichen): Purple dye. Acids turn blue litmus red; bases turn red litmus blue.
   - **Turmeric:** Stain turns reddish-brown with bases, yellow with acids.
   - **Red cabbage juice:** Red in acidic, green in basic solutions.
   - **Petunia, Geranium, Hydrangea petals:** Various colour changes.

2. **Synthetic indicators:**
   - **Phenolphthalein:** Colourless in acid, pink in base.
   - **Methyl orange:** Red in acid, yellow in base.

3. **Olfactory indicators** (change in odour):
   - **Onion:** Loses smell with base, retains smell with acid.
   - **Vanilla:** Smell lost with base.
   - **Clove oil:** Smell lost with base.

### 1.2 Properties of Acids

**Acids** are sour in taste, change blue litmus to red, and have pH < 7.

**Common acids:**
- **Hydrochloric acid (HCl)** — in gastric juice.
- **Sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄)** — in car batteries, fertilisers.
- **Nitric acid (HNO₃)** — in fertilisers, explosives.
- **Acetic acid (CH₃COOH)** — in vinegar.
- **Citric acid (C₆H₈O₇)** — in citrus fruits.
- **Ascorbic acid (Vitamin C)** — in amla, lemon, orange.
- **Lactic acid** — in sour milk, curd.
- **Tartaric acid** — in tamarind, grapes.
- **Formic acid (HCOOH)** — in ant, bee stings.
- **Carbonic acid (H₂CO₃)** — in soft drinks.

**Chemical properties of acids:**

1. **Reaction with metals:** Acid + Metal → Salt + Hydrogen gas:
   - Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂↑
   - 2NaOH + Zn → Na₂ZnO₂ + H₂↑ (for Na, K, Ca — react with bases also)

2. **Reaction with metal carbonates and bicarbonates:** Acid + Carbonate → Salt + Water + CO₂:
   - Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂↑
   - NaHCO₃ + HCl → NaCl + H₂O + CO₂↑
   - The CO₂ turns lime water milky: Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃↓ + H₂O (white precipitate)
   - On passing excess CO₂: CaCO₃ + H₂O + CO₂ → Ca(HCO₃)₂ (soluble)

3. **Reaction with metal oxides (bases):** Acid + Metal oxide → Salt + Water:
   - CuO + 2HCl → CuCl₂ + H₂O (blue-green solution)

4. **Reaction with bases (neutralization):** Acid + Base → Salt + Water:
   - NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O

5. **Aqueous solutions of acids conduct electricity** (acids are electrolytes).

### 1.3 Properties of Bases

**Bases** are bitter in taste, change red litmus to blue, and have pH > 7.

**Alkalis** are water-soluble bases (e.g., NaOH, KOH).

**Common bases:**
- **Sodium hydroxide (NaOH)** — in soap, drain cleaners.
- **Potassium hydroxide (KOH)** — in soap.
- **Calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂)** — in lime water.
- **Magnesium hydroxide (Mg(OH)₂)** — in milk of magnesia (antacid).
- **Ammonium hydroxide (NH₄OH)** — in window cleaners.

**Chemical properties of bases:**

1. **Reaction with metals:** Some metals (Zn, Al) react with strong bases (NaOH, KOH) to produce hydrogen:
   - 2NaOH + Zn → Na₂ZnO₂ + H₂↑

2. **Reaction with non-metallic oxides:** Base + Non-metal oxide → Salt + Water:
   - 2NaOH + CO₂ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O

3. **Reaction with acids (neutralization):** Base + Acid → Salt + Water.

4. **Aqueous solutions of bases conduct electricity.**

5. **Bases change the colour of indicators:** Red litmus → blue; turmeric → reddish-brown; phenolphthalein → pink.

### 1.4 pH Scale

The **pH scale** is a measure of the hydrogen ion concentration in a solution. pH = -log[H⁺] [General Studies Reference, p. 45-46].

- pH = 0 → strongly acidic
- pH = 7 → neutral (pure water)
- pH = 14 → strongly basic (alkaline)

**Importance of pH in everyday life:**

- **Digestive system:** Gastric juice (HCl) has pH ~2; useful for digestion. Excess acid → acidity → antacids (Milk of Magnesia, Mg(OH)₂) neutralize it.
- **Soil pH:** Plants need a specific pH range. pH < 7 (acidic) or pH > 7 (alkaline) affects plant growth. Soil pH is maintained by adding organic matter, lime (for acidic soil), or gypsum.
- **Tooth decay:** Bacteria in the mouth produce acids by breaking down sugar → lowers pH below 5.5 → tooth enamel corrodes. Toothpaste (basic) neutralizes the acid.
- **Self-defence by animals:** Bee sting (formic acid) → pain; treated with baking soda (basic). Nettle plant injects methanoic acid → stinging pain.
- **Acid rain:** When pH of rain water < 5.6 → harmful for buildings, statues, crops, aquatic life.

### 1.5 Neutralization Reaction

The reaction between an acid and a base to give a salt and water is called a **neutralization reaction**:

$$\text{Acid} + \text{Base} \rightarrow \text{Salt} + \text{Water}$$

$$\text{NaOH}(aq) + \text{HCl}(aq) \rightarrow \text{NaCl}(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$

Heat is evolved in a neutralization reaction (exothermic).

### 1.6 Salts

A **salt** is an ionic compound formed from the cation of a base and the anion of an acid. Common examples: NaCl, KCl, CaSO₄, Na₂CO₃, NaHCO₃.

**Family of salts:**

- **Sodium salts:** Sodium chloride (NaCl), sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃), sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO₃), sodium sulphate (Na₂SO₄).
- **Potassium salts:** KCl, KNO₃, K₂SO₄.
- **Calcium salts:** CaCO₃, CaSO₄, CaCl₂.
- **Ammonium salts:** NH₄Cl, (NH₄)₂SO₄, NH₄NO₃.

**pH of salt solutions:**
- Salt of strong acid + strong base → neutral (pH = 7): e.g., NaCl, KCl.
- Salt of strong acid + weak base → acidic (pH < 7): e.g., NH₄Cl.
- Salt of weak acid + strong base → basic (pH > 7): e.g., Na₂CO₃, NaHCO₃.

### 1.7 Chemicals from Common Salt (NaCl)

Sodium chloride (common salt) is the source of several important chemicals:

#### (a) Sodium Hydroxide (NaOH) — Caustic Soda
Produced by the **Chlor-alkali process** (electrolysis of brine — concentrated NaCl solution):

$$2NaCl(aq) + 2H_2O(l) \xrightarrow{\text{electrolysis}} 2NaOH(aq) + Cl_2(g) + H_2(g)$$

**Uses:** Manufacture of soap, detergents, paper, textiles, drain cleaners.

#### (b) Bleaching Powder (CaOCl₂) — Calcium Oxychloride
Produced by passing chlorine gas over dry slaked lime:

$$Ca(OH)_2 + Cl_2 \rightarrow CaOCl_2 + H_2O$$

**Chemical name:** Calcium oxychloride. **Uses:** For bleaching clothes, disinfecting drinking water, in textile and paper industries.

#### (c) Baking Soda (NaHCO₃) — Sodium Hydrogencarbonate
**Preparation:**
$$NaCl + H_2O + CO_2 + NH_3 \rightarrow NH_4Cl + NaHCO_3$$

**Properties:**
- Mild, non-corrosive basic salt.
- On heating, decomposes to give sodium carbonate and CO₂:
  $2NaHCO_3 \xrightarrow{\text{heat}} Na_2CO_3 + H_2O + CO_2$

**Uses:**
- **Ingredient in antacids** (relieves acidity in the stomach).
- **Ingredient in baking powder** (mix of NaHCO₃ + tartaric acid; produces CO₂ that makes cakes fluffy).
- **Soda-acid fire extinguisher** (H₂SO₄ + NaHCO₃ → CO₂ + water + salt; CO₂ extinguishes fire).
- Used in making effervescent drinks.

#### (d) Washing Soda (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O) — Sodium Carbonate Decahydrate
**Preparation:** Recrystallisation of sodium carbonate with water:
$$Na_2CO_3 + 10H_2O \rightarrow Na_2CO_3 \cdot 10H_2O$$

**Properties:**
- Transparent crystalline solid.
- Contains 10 molecules of water of crystallisation (hence decahydrate).
- Effloresces (loses water of crystallisation) on exposure to dry air, becoming a white powder.

**Uses:**
- **Cleaning agent** (household cleaning, washing clothes).
- **Water softening** (precipitates calcium and magnesium ions).
- **Manufacture of glass, soap, borax, caustic soda.**
- Removing permanent hardness of water.

#### (e) Plaster of Paris (CaSO₄·½H₂O) — Calcium Sulphate Hemihydrate
**Preparation:** Heating gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) at 373 K:
$$2(CaSO_4 \cdot 2H_2O) \xrightarrow{373K} 2(CaSO_4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}H_2O) + 3H_2O$$
$$\text{Gypsum} \rightarrow \text{Plaster of Paris} + \text{Water}$$

**Properties:**
- White powder.
- **Setting:** When mixed with water, it sets into a hard mass (gypsum) in about 5-15 minutes:
  $CaSO_4 \cdot \frac{1}{2}H_2O + 1\frac{1}{2}H_2O \rightarrow CaSO_4 \cdot 2H_2O$

**Uses:**
- Making **plaster casts** for fractured bones.
- Making **decorative materials, statues, false ceilings**.
- Making **chalk** for writing on blackboards.
- In **dentistry** for making moulds.

**Important:** The temperature of preparation should not exceed 373 K; above this, Plaster of Paris loses all its water and becomes dead burnt plaster (anhydrous CaSO₄), which does not set with water.

### 1.8 Water of Crystallisation

Many salts contain fixed number of water molecules as part of their crystal structure. These water molecules are called **water of crystallisation**.

| Salt | Formula | Water of crystallisation |
|------|---------|--------------------------|
| Washing soda | Na₂CO₃·10H₂O | 10 |
| Gypsum | CaSO₄·2H₂O | 2 |
| Plaster of Paris | CaSO₄·½H₂O | ½ |
| Blue vitriol (CuSO₄·5H₂O) | — | 5 |
| Green vitriol (FeSO₄·7H₂O) | — | 7 |
| White vitriol (ZnSO₄·7H₂O) | — | 7 |
| Epsom salt (MgSO₄·7H₂O) | — | 7 |

A salt that contains water of crystallisation is called a **hydrated salt**. A salt without water of crystallisation is **anhydrous**.

When a hydrated salt is heated, it loses its water of crystallisation and becomes anhydrous. For example, blue copper sulphate (CuSO₄·5H₂O) on heating becomes anhydrous white copper sulphate (CuSO₄) and water.

## 3. Everyday Science & Applications

**Q1: Why does a bee sting cause pain, and how is it treated?**
A: Bee sting injects **formic acid (methanoic acid)** into the skin, causing pain. It is treated by applying **baking soda (NaHCO₃)**, a mild base that neutralizes the acid.

**Q2: Why is a sting from a nettle plant painful? How is it treated?**
A: Nettle plant injects **methanoic acid** into the skin. Treatment: rubbing with the leaf of the **dock plant** (which contains a basic juice) neutralizes the acid.

**Q3: Why is the pH of the stomach important?**
A: Gastric juice contains HCl (pH ~2), which is essential for digestion of food and kills harmful bacteria. Excess acid causes acidity (indigestion), treated with antacids (Milk of Magnesia).

**Q4: Why does a person with acidity take a mild base?**
A: The excess acid in the stomach is neutralized by the mild base (antacid), relieving the discomfort.

**Q5: Why does tooth decay occur?**
A: Bacteria in the mouth break down sugar and produce acids → pH of mouth falls below 5.5 → tooth enamel (mostly calcium phosphate) corrodes → tooth decay. Toothpaste (basic) neutralizes the acid.

**Q6: Why is acid rain harmful?**
A: When rain water pH falls below 5.6, it corrodes buildings, statues, monuments (especially marble and metals), affects crops, makes soil acidic, and harms aquatic life.

**Q7: Why are basic salts (e.g., Mg(OH)₂) used in antacids?**
A: To neutralize excess acid in the stomach without harming the body.

**Q8: Why is soil pH important for plant growth?**
A: Plants need a specific pH range. Highly acidic or alkaline soil affects nutrient availability and plant growth. Soil pH is adjusted by adding lime (for acidic soil) or organic matter.

**Q9: Why is baking powder used in baking?**
A: Baking powder contains NaHCO₃ and tartaric acid. When moistened and heated, NaHCO₃ reacts with the acid to release CO₂, which makes the cake soft and fluffy.

**Q10: Why is Plaster of Paris used for setting fractured bones?**
A: When POP is mixed with water, it sets into a hard mass in 5-15 minutes, providing a rigid support for the bone to heal.

**Q11: Why does a turmeric stain on a cloth turn red when washed with soap?**
A: Soap is basic; turmeric (yellow) turns reddish-brown with bases. Water alone cannot remove the colour because the base in soap is required.

**Q12: Why is NaOH stored in airtight containers?**
A: NaOH is highly hygroscopic (absorbs moisture from air) and reacts with CO₂ to form Na₂CO₃. Storing in airtight containers prevents both.

**Q13: Why is washing soda used for softening water?**
A: Washing soda (Na₂CO₃) precipitates calcium and magnesium ions (which cause hardness) as insoluble carbonates, thereby softening the water.

**Q14: Why does a soda-acid fire extinguisher work?**
A: H₂SO₄ + NaHCO₃ → CO₂ + water + Na₂SO₄. The CO₂ gas is heavier than air and forms a blanket over the fire, cutting off the oxygen supply and extinguishing it.

**Q15: Why is HCl produced in the stomach even though it is a strong acid?**
A: HCl activates digestive enzymes (pepsin), kills harmful bacteria, and helps in digestion. The stomach lining is protected from HCl by a layer of mucus.

**Q16: Why is dry HCl gas not acidic?**
A: Acidity is shown only when HCl is dissolved in water. Dry HCl is a covalent molecular gas; H⁺ ions are not produced until it is dissolved in water. [Higher class concept]

**Q17: Why do sour substances taste sour?**
A: All sour substances contain acids. The H⁺ ions produced by acids in solution interact with taste buds, giving a sour taste.

**Q18: Why does a strip of copper turn blue-green when dipped in HCl?**
A: Cu + 2HCl → CuCl₂ + H₂. CuCl₂ is blue-green in solution.

**Q19: Why is rainwater slightly acidic?**
A: CO₂ from the atmosphere dissolves in rainwater to form carbonic acid (H₂CO₃), giving it a pH of about 5.6 (slightly acidic).

**Q20: Why is white vitriol used in medicine?**
A: Zinc sulphate (ZnSO₄·7H₂O) is used in eye lotions and as an emetic (to induce vomiting in case of poisoning).

## 5. Solved Numerical Problems

### Problem 1 (Easy — pH)
**The pH of a solution is 4. Is it acidic, basic, or neutral? What is its H⁺ ion concentration?**

**Solution:**
pH = 4 < 7 → Acidic.
[H⁺] = 10⁻⁴ M = $1 \times 10^{-4}$ mol/L.

### Problem 2 (Easy — Acid + Metal)
**Write a balanced equation for the reaction of zinc with dilute sulphuric acid.**

**Solution:**
Zn(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂(g)↑

### Problem 3 (Medium — Acid + Carbonate)
**A student added dilute HCl to a white solid. A colourless gas was evolved which turned lime water milky. Identify the solid.**

**Solution:**
White solid + HCl → Colourless gas (turns lime water milky).
The gas is CO₂ (turns lime water milky by forming CaCO₃).
The solid is likely a metal carbonate or hydrogencarbonate (e.g., Na₂CO₃, NaHCO₃, CaCO₃).
Test reaction: Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂↑

### Problem 4 (Medium — Antacid reaction)
**Why is Milk of Magnesia [Mg(OH)₂] used as an antacid?**

**Solution:**
Excess HCl in the stomach causes acidity. Mg(OH)₂ is a mild base that neutralizes the acid:
Mg(OH)₂ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + 2H₂O

### Problem 5 (Medium — Plaster of Paris)
**A doctor uses Plaster of Paris to set a fractured bone. Write the chemical equation for the setting process.**

**Solution:**
$CaSO_4 \cdot \frac{1}{2}H_2O + 1\frac{1}{2}H_2O \rightarrow CaSO_4 \cdot 2H_2O$ (hard mass / gypsum)

### Problem 6 (Medium — Baking soda)
**A baker uses baking powder to make a cake fluffy. What is the role of tartaric acid in baking powder?**

**Solution:**
Baking powder = NaHCO₃ + tartaric acid.
When moistened, NaHCO₃ reacts with tartaric acid to release CO₂:
$NaHCO_3 + H^+ \rightarrow Na^+ + H_2O + CO_2\uparrow$
The CO₂ bubbles make the cake soft and fluffy.

### Problem 7 (Medium — Chlor-alkali process)
**Write the equation for the electrolysis of brine solution. Name the products formed at the cathode and anode.**

**Solution:**
$2NaCl(aq) + 2H_2O(l) \rightarrow 2NaOH(aq) + Cl_2(g) + H_2(g)$

- **Cathode:** H₂ gas (along with NaOH solution).
- **Anode:** Cl₂ gas.

### Problem 8 (Hard — pH calculations)
**A solution has a hydrogen ion concentration of $1 \times 10^{-3}$ M. What is its pH? Is it acidic or basic?**

**Solution:**
pH = -log[H⁺] = -log(1 × 10⁻³) = 3.
pH = 3 < 7 → Acidic.
