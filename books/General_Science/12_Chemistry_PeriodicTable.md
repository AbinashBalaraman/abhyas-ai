## 1. Comprehensive Theory

### 1.1 Why Classify Elements?

With over 118 elements known (and many more to discover), it is impossible to study the properties of each element individually. **Classification** helps us to study groups of elements with similar properties together. Early attempts at classification include:

- **Prout's hypothesis (1815):** All elements are made of hydrogen.
- **Dobereiner's Triads (1829):** Group of three elements with similar properties and atomic weights in arithmetic progression.
  - Example: Li (7), Na (23), K (39) — atomic weights 7, 23, 39.
  - Limitation: Only a few triads could be formed.
- **Newlands' Law of Octaves (1866):** When elements are arranged in increasing order of atomic weight, every 8th element has properties similar to the 1st.
  - Limitation: Worked well for lighter elements only; didn't work for heavier elements; no place for noble gases and newly discovered elements.

### 1.2 Mendeleev's Periodic Table (1869)

**Dmitri Mendeleev** (Russian chemist) arranged elements in order of increasing atomic weight and grouped them by similar properties. He left gaps for undiscovered elements and predicted their properties:

**Achievements of Mendeleev's table:**
- Systematic study of elements.
- Predicted the existence and properties of yet-undiscovered elements (e.g., eka-aluminium → gallium, eka-silicon → germanium).
- Corrected the atomic weights of some elements (e.g., beryllium, indium).

**Limitations of Mendeleev's table:**
- Position of hydrogen was uncertain.
- Isotopes had no separate place.
- No place for noble gases (discovered later).
- Some elements with similar properties were placed in different groups (e.g., Cu and Ag with alkali metals in some sense).
- The basis of classification (atomic weight) had anomalies (e.g., Co-Ni, Ar-K — heavier element placed before lighter).

### 1.3 Modern Periodic Law

**Moseley's discovery (1913):** The physical and chemical properties of elements are **periodic functions of their atomic numbers** (not atomic weights).

This solved the anomalies in Mendeleev's table (Co, Ni; Ar, K).

### 1.4 Modern Periodic Table

The **Modern Periodic Table** has:
- 18 **vertical columns** called **groups** (numbered 1 to 18).
- 7 **horizontal rows** called **periods** (numbered 1 to 7).
- **Elements arranged in order of increasing atomic number.**

**Total elements:** 118 (as of recent counts). Of these, 94 occur naturally; the rest are synthetic.

### 1.5 Classification in the Modern Periodic Table

The modern periodic table is divided into four blocks [General Studies Reference, p. 81-82]:

| Block | Groups | Elements | Valence orbital | Type |
|-------|--------|----------|------------------|------|
| **s-block** | 1, 2 | Li to Ra | ns¹⁻² | Metals (H is non-metal) |
| **p-block** | 13 to 18 | B to Rn | ns²np¹⁻⁶ | Metals, non-metals, metalloids |
| **d-block** | 3 to 12 | Sc to Cn | (n-1)d¹⁻¹⁰ns¹⁻² | Transition metals |
| **f-block** | Lanthanoids, Actinoids | Ce to Lu, Th to Lr | (n-2)f¹⁻¹⁴ | Inner transition metals |

### 1.6 Periods

There are 7 periods:

| Period | Number of elements | Description |
|--------|-------------------|-------------|
| 1 | 2 | H (1), He (2) — very short period |
| 2 | 8 | Li to Ne — short period |
| 3 | 8 | Na to Ar — short period |
| 4 | 18 | K to Kr — long period |
| 5 | 18 | Rb to Xe — long period |
| 6 | 32 | Cs to Rn — longest period (includes lanthanoids) |
| 7 | 32 (incomplete) | Fr to Og — incomplete period (includes actinoids) |

### 1.7 Groups

There are 18 groups in the modern periodic table:

| Group | Common name | Examples |
|-------|-------------|----------|
| 1 | Alkali metals | Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr |
| 2 | Alkaline earth metals | Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra |
| 3-12 | Transition metals | Sc, Ti, V,..., Zn |
| 13 | Boron family | B, Al, Ga, In, Tl |
| 14 | Carbon family | C, Si, Ge, Sn, Pb |
| 15 | Nitrogen family | N, P, As, Sb, Bi |
| 16 | Oxygen family (Chalcogens) | O, S, Se, Te, Po |
| 17 | Halogens | F, Cl, Br, I, At |
| 18 | Noble gases | He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn |

### 1.8 Trends in the Modern Periodic Table

#### (a) Atomic Radius
- **Across a period (left to right):** Atomic radius **decreases** (due to increased nuclear charge, electrons pulled closer).
- **Down a group (top to bottom):** Atomic radius **increases** (new shells are added, increasing size).

#### (b) Ionisation Enthalpy (Ionisation Energy)
- **Across a period:** Ionisation enthalpy **increases** (atoms are smaller, hold electrons more tightly).
- **Down a group:** Ionisation enthalpy **decreases** (outer electron is farther from nucleus, easier to remove).

#### (c) Electronegativity
- **Across a period:** Electronegativity **increases**.
- **Down a group:** Electronegativity **decreases**.
- **Most electronegative element:** Fluorine (4.0 on Pauling scale).
- **Most electropositive element:** Caesium (or Francium).

#### (d) Metallic Character
- **Across a period (left to right):** Metallic character **decreases**; non-metallic character **increases**.
- **Down a group:** Metallic character **increases**; non-metallic character **decreases**.

#### (e) Valency
- **Across a period:** Valency first increases (1, 2, 3, 4) then decreases (towards 0 for noble gases).
- **Down a group:** Valency is the same (because of the same number of valence electrons).
- Group 1 elements have valency 1; Group 2 have valency 2; Group 13 have valency 3 (some +1); Group 14 have valency 4; Group 15 have valency 3; Group 16 have valency 2; Group 17 have valency 1; Group 18 (noble gases) have valency 0.

#### (f) Melting and Boiling Points
- **Metals:** Generally high, with a maximum at the centre of the period (transition metals).
- **Non-metals:** Generally low.
- **Noble gases:** Very low (exist as gases at room temperature).

#### (g) Nature of Oxides
- **Metals form basic oxides** (e.g., Na₂O, CaO, MgO).
- **Non-metals form acidic oxides** (e.g., CO₂, SO₂, NO₂).
- **Metalloids form amphoteric oxides** (e.g., Al₂O₃, ZnO).

### 1.9 Important Properties of Some Groups

#### Alkali Metals (Group 1)
- Soft, silvery-white, low melting points.
- Highly reactive; react with water to form strong bases and H₂.
- Stored in kerosene.
- Valency: 1 (form M⁺ ions).
- Form ionic compounds.
- Strong reducing agents.
- Flame colours: Li (crimson red), Na (yellow), K (violet), Rb (red), Cs (blue).

#### Alkaline Earth Metals (Group 2)
- Less reactive than alkali metals; harder; higher melting points.
- React with water (slowly) to form hydroxides.
- Valency: 2 (form M²⁺ ions).
- Form ionic compounds.

#### Halogens (Group 17)
- Highly reactive non-metals.
- Exist as diatomic molecules (F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂).
- F₂ (pale yellow gas), Cl₂ (greenish-yellow gas), Br₂ (red-brown liquid), I₂ (violet solid).
- Valency: 1 (form X⁻ ions).
- Strong oxidising agents.

#### Noble Gases (Group 18)
- Colourless, odourless gases.
- Inert (unreactive) under normal conditions due to stable octet.
- Used in balloons (He), lighting (Ne), welding (Ar).

### 1.10 Discovery of Noble Gases

The discovery of noble gases (He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn) was a major breakthrough that led to the addition of an entire new group to the periodic table. They were placed in Group 18 because they are inert and have completely filled valence shells.

### 1.11 Limitations of Modern Periodic Table

Despite its many advantages, the modern periodic table has some limitations [General Studies Reference, p. 82-84]:
- Position of hydrogen is not fixed (resembles alkali metals in some properties, halogens in others).
- Lanthanoids and actinoids are placed separately (not in the main body).
- Heavier elements (post-actinoids) cannot be properly predicted.

## 3. Everyday Science & Applications

**Q1: Why are alkali metals stored in kerosene?**
A: Alkali metals are highly reactive; they react vigorously with air, moisture, and CO₂. Kerosene prevents contact with air and water.

**Q2: Why is fluorine the most electronegative element?**
A: Fluorine has the smallest atomic radius in its period (Period 2) and a strong nuclear charge, so it attracts electrons most strongly.

**Q3: Why does metallic character increase down a group?**
A: Atomic size increases; the outermost electron is farther from the nucleus, easier to lose → more metallic.

**Q4: Why are halogens coloured?**
A: Halogens absorb visible light for electron excitation, giving each a characteristic colour: F₂ (pale yellow), Cl₂ (greenish-yellow), Br₂ (red-brown), I₂ (violet).

**Q5: Why are noble gases inert?**
A: They have a fully filled valence shell (octet for He, duet for H), so they are stable and unreactive under normal conditions.

**Q6: Why is sodium kept in kerosene but not in water?**
A: Sodium reacts violently with water, producing NaOH and H₂ (with a lot of heat, even fire). Kerosene is inert to sodium.

**Q7: Why does the size of atoms decrease across a period?**
A: The number of protons increases, pulling electrons in the same shell closer, reducing the atomic radius.

**Q8: Why are elements of Group 18 called noble gases?**
A: They are inert (do not react) under normal conditions, like noble metals (gold, silver, platinum) that resist corrosion. Hence, they are called "noble."

**Q9: Why are transition metals used as catalysts?**
A: They have variable valencies, d-orbitals for forming intermediate complexes, and high surface area in finely divided form.

**Q10: Why does sodium have a +1 charge and magnesium +2?**
A: Sodium has 1 valence electron, lost to form Na⁺. Magnesium has 2 valence electrons, lost to form Mg²⁺.

**Q11: Why are halogens good oxidising agents?**
A: They have a strong tendency to gain one electron to achieve a noble gas configuration.

**Q12: Why is the position of hydrogen unusual?**
A: Hydrogen has 1 valence electron, like Group 1; but it is a non-metal, like Group 17. It also exists as H⁺ (like alkali metals) and H⁻ (like halogens). So its position is debated.

**Q13: Why do elements in the same group have similar chemical properties?**
A: They have the same number of valence electrons, so they show similar chemical behaviour.

**Q14: Why does ionisation energy increase across a period?**
A: Nuclear charge increases, atomic size decreases, so the outermost electron is held more tightly, requiring more energy to remove.

**Q15: Why are alkaline earth metals less reactive than alkali metals?**
A: Alkaline earth metals have 2 valence electrons, smaller size, and higher ionisation energy, so they are less reactive.

**Q16: Why is chlorine a gas, bromine a liquid, and iodine a solid at room temperature?**
A: As molecular size and mass increase, the intermolecular van der Waals forces increase; hence, the physical state becomes denser (gas → liquid → solid).

**Q17: Why is helium used in balloons?**
A: Helium is non-flammable (safer than hydrogen) and lighter than air.

**Q18: Why is neon used in advertising signs?**
A: Neon emits a characteristic reddish-orange glow when an electric current passes through it.

**Q19: Why are Group 17 elements called halogens?**
A: "Halogen" means "salt-former" in Greek. They readily react with metals to form salts (e.g., NaCl, KBr).

**Q20: Why is fluorine placed in Group 17, not Group 1?**
A: Despite having 1 electron in its valence shell like hydrogen, fluorine is a non-metal with a strong tendency to gain 1 electron (not lose it). So it belongs to Group 17 (halogens), not Group 1.

## 5. Solved Numerical/Conceptual Problems

### Problem 1 (Easy — Period and group)
**An element has atomic number 16. State its group, period, and valency.**

**Solution:**
Z = 16: Electronic configuration 2, 8, 6.
- Valence electrons = 6 → Group 16 (Chalcogens).
- Number of shells = 3 → Period 3.
- Valency = 8 - 6 = 2.
The element is **sulphur (S)**, a non-metal.

### Problem 2 (Easy — Valency)
**An element X has 12 protons. Predict its group, period, and nature (metal/non-metal).**

**Solution:**
Z = 12: Configuration 2, 8, 2.
- Valence electrons = 2 → Group 2 (alkaline earth metals).
- Shells = 3 → Period 3.
The element is **magnesium (Mg)**, a metal, valency 2.

### Problem 3 (Medium — Trend)
**Among the elements Li, Na, K, and Rb, which is the most reactive metal? Why?**

**Solution:**
Most reactive = **Rb (Rubidium)**. Reactivity of alkali metals increases down the group because atomic size increases, the valence electron is more easily lost.

### Problem 4 (Medium — Trend)
**Among the elements F, Cl, Br, I, which is the most reactive non-metal? Why?**

**Solution:**
Most reactive non-metal = **F (Fluorine)**. Reactivity of halogens decreases down the group because atomic size increases, attraction for electrons decreases.

### Problem 5 (Medium — Trend)
**Why is the atomic radius of Na larger than that of Li?**

**Solution:**
Both are in Group 1. Na is below Li in the group. Going down a group, new shells are added, so atomic radius increases.

### Problem 6 (Medium — Nature of oxide)
**An element X forms an oxide X₂O which is highly basic. Predict the group of X.**

**Solution:**
The valency of X is 1, so X is in **Group 1** (alkali metals). The basic nature of the oxide confirms that X is a metal.

### Problem 7 (Hard — Periodic table)
**An element belongs to Period 3 and Group 17. Predict its formula with hydrogen and its valency.**

**Solution:**
Period 3, Group 17 → Element is **Cl (chlorine)**.
Valency = 1.
Formula with H: HCl.

### Problem 8 (Hard — Ionisation energy)
**Why is the ionisation energy of noble gases very high?**

**Solution:**
Noble gases have a fully filled valence shell (octet for all but He), making them very stable. Removing an electron from a stable configuration requires a very high amount of energy.
