## 1. Comprehensive Theory

### 1.1 Magnets and Magnetic Poles

A **magnet** is a material that attracts iron, cobalt, nickel, and steel. Every magnet has two poles: a **north pole (N)** and a **south pole (S)**.

**Properties of magnetic poles:**
- **Like poles repel** each other.
- **Unlike poles attract** each other.
- A compass needle is a small bar magnet; the end pointing north is the north pole, and the end pointing south is the south pole.
- Magnetic poles always exist in pairs — magnetic monopoles do not exist.

### 1.2 Magnetic Field and Field Lines

The region surrounding a magnet in which the force of the magnet can be detected is said to have a **magnetic field**. The lines along which iron filings align themselves represent **magnetic field lines**.

**Properties of magnetic field lines:**

1. Magnetic field is a quantity that has both **direction and magnitude**.
2. The direction is taken to be the direction in which a north pole of the compass needle moves inside it.
3. By convention, field lines **emerge from the north pole** and **merge at the south pole**.
4. **Inside the magnet**, the direction of field lines is from its **south pole to its north pole** — so magnetic field lines are **closed curves**.
5. The relative strength of the magnetic field is shown by the **degree of closeness** of field lines — closer lines indicate a stronger field.
6. **No two field lines are found to cross** each other (if they did, the compass needle would point in two directions at the intersection, which is impossible).

### 1.3 Oersted's Discovery — Magnetism from Electricity

In 1820, **Hans Christian Oersted (1777–1851)**, one of the leading scientists of the 19th century, accidentally discovered that a compass needle got deflected when an electric current passed through a metallic wire placed nearby. This showed that **electricity and magnetism are related phenomena** — a current-carrying wire behaves like a magnet.

The unit of magnetic field strength is named **oersted** in his honor. His research later created technologies such as radio, television, and fiber optics.

### 1.4 Magnetic Field due to a Straight Current-Carrying Conductor

When current flows through a straight conductor, the magnetic field lines around it consist of a series of **concentric circles** whose direction is given by the **right-hand rule**.

**Observations from NCERT Activity 12.4/12.5:**
- If the current flows from north to south, the north pole of the compass moves **east**.
- If the current is reversed, the needle moves **west**.
- If the current is increased, the deflection also increases (magnetic field magnitude increases).
- If the compass is moved away from the wire, deflection decreases (field decreases with distance).

**Pattern:** Concentric circles, with the magnetic field stronger close to the wire and weakening outward.

### 1.5 Right-Hand Thumb Rule (Maxwell's Corkscrew Rule)

A convenient way to find the direction of the magnetic field associated with a current-carrying straight conductor:

> Imagine that you are holding a current-carrying straight conductor in your **right hand** such that the **thumb points towards the direction of current**. Then your **fingers will wrap around the conductor** in the direction of the field lines of the magnetic field.

**Alternative — Maxwell's Corkscrew Rule:** If we consider ourselves driving a corkscrew in the direction of the current, the direction of rotation of the corkscrew is the direction of the magnetic field.

**Example:** A current through a horizontal power line flows in east to west direction. At a point directly below it, the magnetic field turns **clockwise** in a plane perpendicular to the wire (viewed from the east end) and **anti-clockwise** (viewed from the west end).

### 1.6 Magnetic Field due to a Current in a Circular Loop

For a current-carrying circular loop:
- The magnetic field lines are concentric circles around the wire; at the centre of the loop, they appear as **straight lines**.
- The field magnitude is greater at the centre.
- Every section of the wire contributes to the magnetic field lines in the same direction within the loop (by right-hand rule).
- For **n** turns, the field is **n times** that of a single turn.

### 1.7 Solenoid and Electromagnet

A **solenoid** is a coil of many circular turns of insulated copper wire wrapped closely in the shape of a cylinder.

**Properties of a current-carrying solenoid:**
- Pattern of field lines similar to a **bar magnet**.
- One end behaves as the magnetic **north pole**, the other as the **south pole**.
- The field lines inside the solenoid are parallel straight lines → **uniform magnetic field** inside.
- The field is the same at all points inside the solenoid.

**Electromagnet:** A strong magnetic field produced inside a solenoid can magnetise a piece of magnetic material, like soft iron, when placed inside the coil. The magnet so formed is called an **electromagnet**.

### 1.8 Force on a Current-Carrying Conductor in a Magnetic Field

A current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field experiences a **force**.

**Key observations from NCERT Activity 12.7:**
- A current-carrying aluminium rod, placed in the magnetic field of a horse-shoe magnet, is **displaced**.
- When the direction of current is reversed, the direction of displacement is **reversed**.
- When the direction of the magnetic field is reversed (poles interchanged), the direction of force is also reversed.
- The displacement is **largest** when the direction of current is at right angles to the direction of the magnetic field.
- The force is **perpendicular** to both the current direction and the magnetic field direction.

### 1.9 Fleming's Left-Hand Rule

For finding the direction of force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field:

> Stretch the **thumb, forefinger, and middle finger** of your **left hand** such that they are **mutually perpendicular**. If the **first finger points in the direction of magnetic field** and the **second finger in the direction of current**, then the **thumb will point in the direction of motion or the force** acting on the conductor.

**Devices that use this principle:** Electric motor, electric generator, loudspeakers, microphones, measuring instruments.

### 1.10 Electric Motor

An **electric motor** is a device that converts electrical energy into mechanical energy. It works on the principle that a current-carrying coil placed in a magnetic field experiences a force.

**Principle:** When a current-carrying coil is placed in a uniform magnetic field, it experiences a torque that rotates it. The direction of rotation is given by Fleming's left-hand rule.

**Working:** A rectangular coil (armature) is placed between the poles of a magnet. Current is supplied through brushes and a split-ring commutator. The direction of current reverses every half rotation, ensuring continuous rotation in the same direction.

**Uses:** Electric fans, washing machines, mixers, blenders, water pumps, electric vehicles.

### 1.11 Electromagnetic Induction

**Electromagnetic induction** is the phenomenon of generating an induced current (or emf) in a coil due to a changing magnetic field.

**Faraday's Laws of Electromagnetic Induction:**

1. **First law:** Whenever the magnetic flux linked with a coil changes, an emf is induced in the coil. The induced emf lasts as long as the change in magnetic flux continues.
2. **Second law:** The magnitude of the induced emf is directly proportional to the rate of change of magnetic flux through the coil.

$$\text{Induced emf} \, \varepsilon = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$$

The negative sign indicates that the induced emf opposes the change in flux (Lenz's law).

**Methods to change magnetic flux:**
- Moving a magnet towards or away from a coil.
- Moving a coil towards or away from a magnet.
- Rotating a coil in a magnetic field.
- Changing the current in a nearby coil.

### 1.12 Electric Generator

An **electric generator** converts mechanical energy into electrical energy, working on the principle of electromagnetic induction.

**Two types:**
- **AC generator (Alternator):** Produces alternating current. Uses slip rings.
- **DC generator (Dynamo):** Produces direct current. Uses split-ring commutator.

**Working:** A rectangular coil is rotated in a uniform magnetic field. The angle between the magnetic field and the area vector changes continuously, changing the magnetic flux. By Faraday's law, an emf is induced.

**Uses of generators:** Power plants (thermal, hydro, nuclear), backup power systems, portable generators.

### 1.13 Domestic Electric Circuits

In our homes, we receive AC electric power of **220 V** with a frequency of **50 Hz**. The main wires:

- **Live wire (positive):** Red insulation cover; potential 220 V relative to earth.
- **Neutral wire (negative):** Black insulation.
- **Earth wire:** Green insulation, connected to a metal plate deep in the earth.

**Two circuit ratings:**
- **15 A** for high-power appliances (geysers, air coolers).
- **5 A** for bulbs, fans.

**In each circuit, different appliances are connected across the live and neutral wires in parallel, with separate ON/OFF switches for each**.

### 1.14 Electric Fuse — Safety Device

An **electric fuse** is an important safety component that prevents damage to appliances and circuits due to **overloading** or **short-circuiting**.

**Construction:** A piece of wire made of a metal or alloy of appropriate melting point (aluminium, copper, iron, lead), encased in a cartridge of porcelain or similar material with metal ends.

**Working:** If a current larger than the specified value flows, the temperature of the fuse wire increases (due to Joule heating), the wire melts, and the circuit breaks.

**Fuse ratings:** 1 A, 2 A, 3 A, 5 A, 10 A, etc.

**For an electric iron consuming 1 kW at 220 V**, current = 1000/220 = 4.54 A → use a 5 A fuse.

**Overloading** occurs when the live wire and neutral wire come into direct contact (damaged insulation, appliance fault), or when too many appliances are connected to a single socket, or due to a hike in supply voltage.

### 1.15 Earth Wire

The **earth wire** has insulation of green colour and is usually connected to a metal plate deep in the earth near the house.

**Function:** Used as a safety measure, especially for appliances with metallic bodies (electric press, toaster, table fan, refrigerator). The metallic body is connected to the earth wire, which provides a low-resistance conducting path for the current. Any leakage of current to the metallic body keeps its potential to that of the earth, and the user may not get a severe electric shock.

### 1.16 Magnetism in Medicine (MRI)

Electric currents in the body produce magnetic fields. The heart and brain produce the most significant magnetic fields in the body.

**Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** A technique that uses the magnetic field inside the body to obtain images of different body parts, useful for medical diagnosis.

## 3. Everyday Science & Applications

**Q1: Why does a compass needle get deflected when brought near a bar magnet?**
A: A bar magnet has a magnetic field around it. The compass needle is itself a small magnet and experiences a force in this field, causing deflection.

**Q2: Why are magnetic field lines closed curves?**
A: Because inside the magnet, the field lines go from south to north pole, while outside they go from north to south pole — together forming closed loops.

**Q3: Why do two magnetic field lines never intersect?**
A: If they intersected, the compass needle at the intersection would point in two directions, which is impossible.

**Q4: Why does a current-carrying wire behave like a magnet?**
A: A current produces a magnetic field around it (Oersted's discovery). This field can attract or repel other magnets, like a regular magnet.

**Q5: Why is the field inside a solenoid uniform?**
A: The field lines inside the solenoid are parallel straight lines, indicating the field is the same at all points inside.

**Q6: Why is an electromagnet preferred over a permanent magnet in many applications?**
A: An electromagnet can be turned on/off and its strength can be changed by varying the current.

**Q7: Why does a current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field experience a force?**
A: The magnetic field of the current interacts with the external magnetic field, producing a force on the conductor. The direction of the force is given by Fleming's left-hand rule.

**Q8: Why is the force on a conductor maximum when the current is perpendicular to the magnetic field?**
A: The force F = BIL sin θ. When θ = 90°, sin θ = 1 (maximum), so F is maximum. When θ = 0° (parallel), F = 0.

**Q9: Why does an electric motor use a split-ring commutator?**
A: To reverse the direction of current every half rotation, ensuring continuous rotation in the same direction.

**Q10: Why does a generator produce alternating current?**
A: As the coil rotates, the angle between the magnetic field and area vector changes, so the magnetic flux linked with the coil changes, inducing an emf that changes direction every half rotation.

**Q11: Why is the earth wire connected to a metal plate deep in the ground?**
A: The earth provides a large reservoir of charge; connecting the earth wire to a metal plate buried in moist soil ensures a low-resistance path to earth.

**Q12: Why is a fuse wire made of metal with low melting point?**
A: So that it melts and breaks the circuit when current exceeds the safe limit, protecting appliances from damage.

**Q13: Why is MRI safer than X-rays for repeated imaging?**
A: MRI uses magnetic fields, not ionising radiation, so it does not damage tissues even with repeated scans.

**Q14: Why is the field of a bar magnet stronger at the poles?**
A: Magnetic field lines are closer (more crowded) near the poles → stronger field.

**Q15: Why is the magnetic field of a solenoid similar to a bar magnet?**
A: Both have north and south poles, with field lines emerging from the north and merging at the south. The field inside is uniform and parallel.

**Q16: Why does the magnetic field of a current-carrying straight wire form concentric circles?**
A: Every point on a circle around the wire is at the same distance, and the field magnitude depends only on the current and distance. By symmetry, the field lines form circles.

**Q17: Why is the maximum displacement in NCERT Activity 12.7 observed when the rod is perpendicular to the magnetic field?**
A: Force is F = BIL sin θ. Maximum when sin θ = 1, i.e., θ = 90° (perpendicular).

**Q18: Why does an electron beam deflect in a magnetic field?**
A: A moving electron constitutes a current. This current in a magnetic field experiences a force (Fleming's left-hand rule), causing deflection.

**Q19: Why are electric bells made using electromagnets?**
A: Electromagnets can be switched on/off rapidly by controlling the current, making them ideal for vibrating the bell striker.

**Q20: Why is the unit of magnetic field named after Oersted?**
A: He was the first to demonstrate the connection between electricity and magnetism (compass needle deflection due to current).

## 5. Solved Conceptual Problems

### Problem 1 (Easy — Right-Hand Rule)
**A current flows vertically downward through a straight wire. What is the direction of the magnetic field at a point to the right of the wire?**

**Solution:**
Applying the right-hand thumb rule: point the thumb downward (direction of current). The fingers wrap around the wire, with the field to the right of the wire pointing **into the page** (away from the reader).

### Problem 2 (Easy — Right-Hand Rule)
**A current through a horizontal power line flows in east to west direction. What is the direction of magnetic field at a point directly below it?**

**Solution:**
Apply the right-hand thumb rule. The current is east to west. The magnetic field at any point below the wire turns **clockwise** in a plane perpendicular to the wire, when viewed from the east end (and anti-clockwise, when viewed from the west end).

### Problem 3 (Medium — Fleming's Left-Hand Rule)
**An electron enters a magnetic field at right angles to it, moving from left to right. The magnetic field is into the page. What is the direction of force on the electron?**

**Solution:**
The direction of conventional current is taken **opposite** to the direction of motion of electrons. So the current is from **right to left**. By Fleming's left-hand rule:
- First finger (B): into the page
- Second finger (I): right to left
- Thumb (F): **downward**

Alternatively: the force is perpendicular to both. The answer is **into the page** (option d).

### Problem 4 (Medium — Direction of magnetic field)
**A positively charged particle (alpha-particle) projected towards west is deflected towards north by a magnetic field. The direction of magnetic field is:**

**Solution:**
Current direction is the direction of motion of positive charge, i.e., **west**.
Force is towards **north**.
Apply Fleming's left-hand rule: with second finger (current) pointing west and thumb (force) pointing north, the first finger (field) points **upward**.

So the answer is (d) upward.

### Problem 5 (Medium — Solenoid)
**The magnetic field inside a long straight solenoid carrying current is:**
(a) zero
(b) decreases as we move towards its end
(c) increases as we move towards its end
(d) the same at all points

**Solution:**
**(d) the same at all points** — the field lines inside a solenoid are parallel straight lines, indicating a uniform field.

### Problem 6 (Medium — Force on conductor)
**A current-carrying conductor is placed in a magnetic field. When is the force experienced by it largest?**

**Solution:**
The force is largest when the direction of current is **at right angles** (perpendicular) to the direction of the magnetic field.

### Problem 7 (Medium — Circuit diagram)
**An electric oven of 2 kW power rating is operated in a domestic electric circuit (220 V) that has a current rating of 5 A. What result do you expect?**

**Solution:**
Current drawn = P/V = 2000/220 = 9.09 A. This is much greater than the circuit rating of 5 A, so the fuse will blow, cutting off the power supply.

### Problem 8 (Hard — Electromagnetic induction)
**A magnet is moved towards a coil. What happens to the induced current in the coil?**

**Solution:**
By Faraday's law, the changing magnetic flux through the coil induces an EMF, which drives a current. By Lenz's law, the induced current creates a magnetic field that opposes the change — so the induced current flows in a direction such that the coil's face near the approaching magnet becomes a north pole, repelling the magnet. The induced current exists as long as the magnet is moving.

[Reference — electromagnetic induction]
