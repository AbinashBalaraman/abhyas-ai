## 1. Comprehensive Theory

### 1.1 Reproduction — Overview

**Reproduction** is the process by which living organisms produce offspring of their own kind, ensuring the continuity of the species.

**Two main types:**

- **Asexual reproduction:** Involves a single parent; offspring are genetically identical (clones).
- **Sexual reproduction:** Involves two parents; offspring are genetically varied.

### 1.2 Asexual Reproduction

A single parent produces offspring without the fusion of gametes. Methods:

1. **Binary fission:** Parent divides into two equal daughters. Examples: Amoeba, Paramecium, bacteria.
2. **Multiple fission:** Parent divides into many daughters. Example: Plasmodium (malaria parasite).
3. **Budding:** Outgrowth (bud) from parent, which detaches and grows. Examples: Hydra, yeast.
4. **Fragmentation:** Body breaks into fragments, each growing into a new individual. Example: Spirogyra (algae), planaria.
5. **Spore formation:** Spores are produced and germinate into new individuals. Examples: fungi, ferns, mosses.
6. **Vegetative propagation:** New plants from vegetative parts (stem, root, leaf). Examples: potato (tuber), ginger (rhizome), Bryophyllum (leaf buds).
7. **Tissue culture:** Growing plants from a small piece of tissue in a nutrient medium (artificial asexual reproduction).

**Advantages of asexual reproduction:** Faster, no need for a mate, can rapidly colonize favourable environments.

**Disadvantages:** No genetic variation; less adaptability to changing environments.

### 1.3 Sexual Reproduction

Involves the **fusion of male and female gametes** (fertilization) to form a **zygote**, which develops into a new individual.

**Advantages:** Genetic variation; better adaptability to environmental changes.

**Key events:**
1. **Gametogenesis:** Formation of gametes (sperm in males, ova/eggs in females).
2. **Fertilization:** Fusion of male and female gametes → zygote.
3. **Zygote formation and development:** Zygote divides repeatedly → embryo → foetus → baby.

**Two types of fertilization:**
- **External fertilization:** Gametes fuse outside the body (in water). Examples: fish, amphibians.
- **Internal fertilization:** Gametes fuse inside the female body. Examples: reptiles, birds, mammals.

### 1.4 Reproduction in Flowering Plants (Sexual)

A typical flower has 4 whorls:

- **Sepals (calyx):** Green, protective.
- **Petals (corolla):** Coloured, attract pollinators.
- **Stamens (androecium):** Male part; consists of anther (contains pollen grains) and filament.
- **Pistil/carpel (gynoecium):** Female part; consists of stigma, style, and ovary (contains ovules).

**Pollen grain:** Male gametophyte; contains male gametes.
**Ovule:** Contains the egg cell (female gamete).

**Pollination:** The transfer of pollen grains from the anther to the stigma.

**Types of pollination:**
- **Self-pollination:** Pollen from anther lands on stigma of the same flower or another flower of the same plant.
- **Cross-pollination:** Pollen from one plant lands on the stigma of a flower of another plant (of the same species).

**Agents of pollination:** Wind (anemophily), insects (entomophily), birds (ornithophily), water (hydrophily), bats (chiropterophily).

**Fertilization (in plants):**
1. Pollen grain lands on stigma.
2. Pollen tube grows down through the style to the ovary.
3. Male gamete travels through the pollen tube to the ovule.
4. Male gamete fuses with the egg cell → zygote.
5. Zygote develops into embryo; ovule develops into seed; ovary develops into fruit.

### 1.5 Human Reproductive System

#### Male Reproductive System

| Organ | Function |
|-------|----------|
| **Testes (2)** | Produce sperms and male hormone (testosterone) |
| **Scrotum** | Sac that holds the testes outside the body (for lower temperature) |
| **Epididymis** | Stores sperms and helps them mature |
| **Vas deferens** | Carries sperms from epididymis to the urethra |
| **Seminal vesicles, prostate, Cowper's glands** | Produce seminal fluid (which mixes with sperms to form semen) |
| **Urethra** | Common passage for urine and semen |
| **Penis** | Copulatory organ; delivers semen into the vagina |

#### Female Reproductive System

| Organ | Function |
|-------|----------|
| **Ovaries (2)** | Produce eggs (ova) and female hormones (oestrogen, progesterone) |
| **Fallopian tubes (oviducts, 2)** | Carry the egg from ovary to uterus; site of fertilization |
| **Uterus (womb)** | Pear-shaped muscular organ; site of embryo development |
| **Vagina** | Receives the penis during intercourse; birth canal |

### 1.6 Menstrual Cycle and Puberty

**Puberty:** Age at which reproductive organs become functional and secondary sexual characteristics develop (~10-14 years).

**Menstrual cycle** (approx. 28 days):

1. **Menstrual phase (1-5 days):** The uterus lining breaks down; bleeding occurs.
2. **Follicular phase (6-13 days):** Follicle develops in the ovary; uterine lining thickens.
3. **Ovulation (~14th day):** Release of the egg from the ovary.
4. **Luteal phase (15-28 days):** Empty follicle becomes corpus luteum; produces progesterone.

If fertilization does not occur, the cycle repeats with menstruation. If fertilization occurs, the embryo implants in the uterus and the cycle stops (pregnancy).

**Menopause:** Permanent cessation of menstruation (~45-50 years).

### 1.7 Fertilization, Implantation, Pregnancy, Birth

- **Fertilization:** Sperm meets the egg in the fallopian tube. The zygote is formed.
- **Implantation:** The zygote divides to form a ball of cells (blastocyst) that implants in the uterine wall.
- **Pregnancy:** Embryo develops in the uterus (~9 months) into a foetus. Placenta provides oxygen and nutrients; umbilical cord connects the foetus to the placenta.
- **Parturition (birth):** Strong uterine contractions expel the baby through the vagina.
- **Lactation:** Mammary glands produce milk to feed the newborn.

### 1.8 Reproductive Health

**Birth control (contraception) methods:**

1. **Barrier methods:** Condoms, diaphragms — prevent sperm from reaching the egg.
2. **Hormonal methods:** Pills, injections, patches — prevent ovulation.
3. **Intrauterine devices (IUDs):** Copper-T placed in the uterus.
4. **Surgical methods:** Vasectomy (cutting vas deferens in males) and tubectomy (cutting fallopian tubes in females).
5. **Natural methods:** Withdrawal, calendar method (rhythm method).

**Sexually Transmitted Diseases (STDs):** Spread by sexual contact. Examples: AIDS (HIV), syphilis, gonorrhoea.

### 1.9 Heredity

**Heredity** is the passing of characteristics (traits) from parents to offspring. The science of heredity is called **genetics**.

**Mendel's Experiments**:
- **Gregor Mendel** (1822-1884) is the father of genetics.
- He worked on **pea plants** (Pisum sativum) because they have many contrasting traits, short life cycle, and clear-cut characteristics.

**Mendel's Monohybrid Cross** (one trait, e.g., plant height):
- Tall (TT) × Dwarf (tt) → All Tall (Tt) in F1 generation.
- F1 × F1 (Tt × Tt) → Tall (TT) : Tall (Tt) : Tall (Tt) : Dwarf (tt) = 3 : 1 in F2 generation.

### 1.10 Mendel's Laws

#### Law of Dominance
- Each trait is controlled by a pair of **alleles** (forms of a gene).
- One allele is **dominant** (expresses itself) and the other is **recessive** (suppressed in presence of dominant).
- In a heterozygote (Tt), the dominant allele (T) expresses the trait.

#### Law of Segregation
- Each organism has two alleles for each trait.
- During gamete formation, the alleles separate (segregate), so each gamete receives only one allele.
- During fertilization, the offspring receives one allele from each parent, restoring the pair.

### 1.11 Genetic Terms

- **Genotype:** The genetic makeup of an organism (e.g., Tt, TT, tt).
- **Phenotype:** The observable characteristic (e.g., tall, dwarf).
- **Homozygous:** Two identical alleles (TT or tt).
- **Heterozygous:** Two different alleles (Tt).
- **Dominant allele:** Expresses in both homozygous and heterozygous conditions.
- **Recessive allele:** Expresses only in homozygous condition.
- **F1 generation:** First filial generation (offspring of the initial cross).
- **F2 generation:** Offspring of the F1 cross.

### 1.12 Dihybrid Cross

A dihybrid cross involves two traits. Mendel crossed pea plants differing in two traits: seed shape (round vs wrinkled) and seed colour (yellow vs green).

- Parental (P): Round Yellow (RRYY) × Wrinkled Green (rryy)
- F1: All Round Yellow (RrYy)
- F2: 9 Round Yellow : 3 Round Green : 3 Wrinkled Yellow : 1 Wrinkled Green (9:3:3:1)

#### Law of Independent Assortment
"Independent pairs of genes assort independently of one another during gamete formation." This means the inheritance of one trait (e.g., seed shape) is independent of the inheritance of another trait (e.g., seed colour).

### 1.13 Sex Determination

In humans, sex is determined by **sex chromosomes**:
- **Male:** XY
- **Female:** XX

A mother (XX) produces eggs with X only. A father (XY) produces sperms with X or Y. If a Y-sperm fertilizes the egg (X) → XY (boy). If an X-sperm fertilizes the egg (X) → XX (girl). So the **father** determines the sex of the child.

**Sex ratio:** Approximately 50:50 (since there are equal chances of X or Y sperm fertilizing the egg).

### 1.14 Evolution

**Evolution** is the gradual change in the inherited characteristics of species over generations.

**Theories of evolution:**

#### (a) Lamarck's Theory of Inheritance of Acquired Characters (1809)
- Organisms develop new characteristics during their lifetime in response to changes in the environment.
- These acquired traits are passed to offspring.
- Example: Giraffes' long necks from stretching to reach high leaves.
- **Limitation:** Most acquired traits are not inherited.

#### (b) Darwin's Theory of Natural Selection (1859)
- All species produce more offspring than can survive.
- There is **variation** within populations.
- Variations that help survival (favourable variations) are passed to offspring (**natural selection**).
- Over time, this leads to the formation of new species.
- Example: Long-necked giraffes survived because they could reach high leaves.

### 1.15 Evidences of Evolution
- **Homologous organs:** Same basic structure but different functions (e.g., forelimbs of human, whale, bat, cheetah). Indicate common ancestry.
- **Analogous organs:** Different basic structure but similar function (e.g., wings of butterfly and bird). Indicate convergent evolution.
- **Fossils:** Remains of ancient organisms preserved in rocks.
- **Embryology:** Similarities in embryos of different vertebrates.

### 1.16 Human Evolution
- Humans belong to the family **Hominidae** (great apes).
- Common ancestors: Dryopithecus, Ramapithecus, Australopithecus, Homo erectus, Homo neanderthalensis, Homo sapiens.
- Modern humans (Homo sapiens) appeared about 200,000 years ago.

## 3. Everyday Science & Applications

**Q1: Why are some children born with different coloured eyes than their parents?**
A: Eye colour is determined by multiple genes, with possible dominant and recessive combinations. Each parent contributes one allele, and the offspring's phenotype depends on the combination.

**Q2: Why is a pea plant chosen by Mendel for genetic experiments?**
A: Pea plants have many contrasting traits, a short life cycle, clear-cut characteristics, and can be easily cross-pollinated.

**Q3: Why does the testis lie outside the abdominal cavity?**
A: Sperms require a temperature lower than body temperature (37°C) for production. The scrotum maintains the testes at ~35°C.

**Q4: Why is the urethra in males called the common passage?**
A: It carries both urine (from the bladder) and semen (from the reproductive system).

**Q5: Why does the menstrual cycle stop during pregnancy?**
A: Hormones (especially progesterone) from the corpus luteum and placenta maintain the uterine lining, preventing menstruation.

**Q6: Why are vasectomy and tubectomy considered permanent methods of birth control?**
A: They cut the vas deferens or fallopian tubes, preventing the sperm or egg from reaching the partner's gamete. They are difficult to reverse.

**Q7: Why is the father responsible for the sex of a child?**
A: The mother has only X chromosomes (XX), so her egg always carries X. The father has X and Y, so his sperm may carry X or Y. The combination of X or Y from the father determines the sex.

**Q8: Why are the forelimbs of a whale, a bat, a cheetah, and a human considered homologous?**
A: They have the same basic structure (humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, metacarpals, phalanges) but different functions. This indicates common ancestry.

**Q9: Why are the wings of a butterfly and a bird considered analogous?**
A: They have different basic structures (insect wing vs bird wing) but serve the same function (flight). They evolved independently (convergent evolution).

**Q10: Why are fossils considered important evidence of evolution?**
A: Fossils preserve the remains of ancient organisms, showing how species have changed over time and providing a record of evolution.

**Q11: Why is a test cross performed?**
A: To determine whether an organism with a dominant phenotype is homozygous (TT) or heterozygous (Tt). Cross with a homozygous recessive (tt); if any offspring show the recessive trait, the parent is heterozygous.

**Q12: Why are sons often shorter than fathers?**
A: Height is influenced by many genes (polygenic inheritance) and also by nutrition and environment. Not all traits of parents are inherited.

**Q13: Why is grafting a form of asexual reproduction?**
A: In grafting, a part of one plant (scion) is joined with another plant (stock). The resulting plant is genetically identical to the scion, not involving gametes.

**Q14: Why is variation important for evolution?**
A: Without variation, there would be no different traits for natural selection to act upon, and evolution would not occur.

**Q15: Why do we age and die?**
A: Ageing is a result of accumulated cellular damage and programmed changes. Death is the end of life, allowing for the next generation.

**Q16: Why is breast milk recommended for newborns?**
A: Breast milk contains antibodies, nutrients, and hormones in the right balance for the baby's growth and immunity.

**Q17: Why are identical twins so similar in appearance?**
A: Identical twins develop from a single zygote that splits into two embryos. They have the same DNA, so they are genetically identical.

**Q18: Why is genetic diversity important for a species?**
A: Genetic diversity helps a species adapt to environmental changes, resist diseases, and survive catastrophic events.

**Q19: Why did Darwin's finches evolve different beaks?**
A: Different finch populations on different Galápagos islands had access to different food sources. Natural selection favoured beaks suited to each food, leading to the evolution of different beak shapes.

**Q20: Why are embryos of different vertebrates similar?**
A: Vertebrates share a common ancestor, and their embryos develop through similar stages in the early phase. As they develop, they differentiate into their specific forms.
