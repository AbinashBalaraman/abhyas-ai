# Monetary Policy & Policy Rates

## 1. Conceptual Deep Dive & Historical Background

### 1.1 What is Monetary Policy?

Monetary policy is the macroeconomic policy laid down by the central bank (RBI in India) to manage the money supply and interest rates to achieve macroeconomic objectives like price stability, economic growth, employment, and financial stability. In India, the primary objective is **maintaining price stability while keeping in mind growth** — explicit in the RBI Act 1934 as amended in 2016.

The monetary policy transmission mechanism works as follows:
- RBI changes policy rate (Repo Rate)
- Banks adjust their lending/deposit rates
- Affects borrowing/investment decisions
- Influences aggregate demand
- Impacts inflation and growth

### 1.2 Evolution of Monetary Policy in India

**Phase 1 (1935-1949): Pre-Nationalisation Era**
- RBI established 1 April 1935 under RBI Act 1934
- Initial focus on currency management and exchange rate stability

**Phase 2 (1950-1980s): Plan Era**
- Five Year Plans dictated credit allocation
- Multiple objectives: growth, employment, sectoral priorities
- Direct controls on interest rates (administered rates)

**Phase 3 (1985-1997): Liberalisation Begins**
- 1985: Vaghul Working Group — phased deregulation of interest rates
- 1988: First reforms under Narasimham Committee (Phase I)
- 1992-97: Major reforms; introduction of Auctions for G-Secs; LAF (Liquidity Adjustment Facility)

**Phase 4 (1998-2016): Modern Monetary Policy**
- 1998: Narasimham Committee II — capital adequacy, NPAs
- 1999: OMO (Open Market Operations) framework
- 2004: Quarterly Monetary Policy Review (quarterly → bi-monthly)
- **2014**: Dr. Raghuram Rajan introduced Monetary Policy Framework Agreement with Government (signed 2015)
- **2016**: RBI Act amendment → Statutory MPC

**Phase 5 (2016-Present): Statutory MPC Era**
- Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) constituted 29 September 2016
- Section 45ZA, 45ZB of RBI Act 1934
- Flexible Inflation Targeting (FIT) introduced

### 1.3 Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) — Composition & Function

**Statutory Basis**: RBI Act 1934 amended in May 2016

**Composition (6 members)**:
1. RBI Governor (Chairperson, ex officio)
2. RBI Deputy Governor in charge of Monetary Policy

### 1.4 Inflation Targeting Framework

**Current Target**: **4% CPI inflation**

**Tolerance Band**: **2% (lower) to 6% (upper)**

**Latest Review**: 25 March 2026 — Central Government retained the target for **April 1, 2026 to March 31, 2031**

**Operating Target**: Weighted Average Call Rate (WACR) aligned with policy repo rate

**Failure Trigger**: If average inflation is:
- Above 6% for any 3 consecutive quarters, OR
- Below 2% for any 3 consecutive quarters
Then RBI must submit a report to Government explaining reasons and remedial actions

## 2. Regulatory Framework: Policy Rates

### 2.1 Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF) Corridor

The LAF corridor has three key rates:

```
    ┌──────────────────────────────────────────┐
    │                                          │
    │   MSF Rate (Ceiling)  ◄── 25 bps above ──┤
    │                                          │
    │              REPO RATE (Mid-point)        │
    │              (Policy Rate)                │
    │                                          │
    │   SDF Rate (Floor)    ◄── 25 bps below ──┤
    │                                          │
    └──────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Corridor width**: 50 bps (25 bps above and below repo)

### 2.3 Definitions

| Rate | Definition | Position |
|---|---|---|
| **Repo Rate** | Rate at which RBI provides liquidity under LAF against collateral of govt/approved securities | Mid-point of corridor (policy rate) |
| **SDF (Standing Deposit Facility) Rate** | Rate at which RBI accepts uncollateralised overnight deposits from LAF participants | **Floor of corridor** (25 bps below Repo) |
| **MSF (Marginal Standing Facility) Rate** | Penal rate at which banks can borrow overnight by dipping into SLR portfolio up to 2% | **Ceiling of corridor** (25 bps above Repo) |
| **Bank Rate** | Rate at which RBI is ready to buy/rediscount bills; aligned with MSF rate under Sec 49 of RBI Act 1934 | Same as MSF |
| **Reverse Repo Rate** | Rate at which RBI absorbs liquidity from banks against collateral of govt securities | After SDF (April 2022), at RBI discretion |
| **CRR (Cash Reserve Ratio)** | % of Net Demand & Time Liabilities (NDTL) banks must maintain with RBI as cash | Statutory |
| **SLR (Statutory Liquidity Ratio)** | % of demand & time liabilities to be maintained in govt securities, cash, gold | Statutory |

### 2.4 CRR — Cash Reserve Ratio

**Statutory Basis**: Section 42(1) of RBI Act, 1934

**Definition**: Banks must maintain a specified percentage of their Net Demand and Time Liabilities (NDTL) as cash balance with RBI.

**Calculation**:
- NDTL = Demand Liabilities + Time Liabilities
- Demand Liabilities: Current accounts, demand drafts, etc.
- Time Liabilities: Fixed deposits, savings accounts

**Impact of CRR Change**:
- ↑CRR → More locked-up liquidity → Less lendable funds → Higher interest rates → Lower inflation
- ↓CRR → More lendable funds → Lower interest rates → Higher growth, risk of inflation

**Current Value**: ⚠️ VERIFY current CRR rate as of June 2026

**Historical Range**: CRR has varied from 3% to 15% historically; current rates typically 4-6%.

### 2.5 SLR — Statutory Liquidity Ratio

**Statutory Basis**: Section 24 of Banking Regulation Act, 1949

**Definition**: Banks must maintain a specified percentage of their Net Demand and Time Liabilities in:
1. Government securities (G-Secs)
2. Cash
3. Gold

**Forms of SLR Maintenance**:
- Gold (with RBI)
- Government of India securities
- State Government securities (SDL)
- Other approved securities

**Current Value**: ⚠️ VERIFY current SLR rate

### 2.6 MSF — Marginal Standing Facility

- Available to banks facing severe liquidity shortage
- Borrowings under MSF are charged at the MSF rate
- Banks can dip into their SLR portfolio up to 2% below the prescribed SLR
- Penal rate — higher than repo rate
- Available for overnight borrowing

### 2.7 Bank Rate

- Section 49 of RBI Act, 1934
- Rate at which RBI is prepared to buy or rediscount bills of exchange or other commercial paper
- Aligned with MSF rate since 2015
- Not actively used as a policy tool currently; signals RBI's policy stance

### 2.8 Open Market Operations (OMO)

**OMO** = Outright purchase/sale of government securities for durable liquidity injection/absorption

**Types**:
- **Outright OMO**: Direct purchase/sale of securities (no repurchase)
- **Repo OMO**: With agreement to reverse transaction
- **MSF**: Penal-rate overnight borrowing
- **SDF**: Uncollateralised overnight deposit facility

## 3. Transmission Mechanism

### 3.1 How Monetary Policy Affects the Economy

```
RBI changes Repo Rate
        ↓
Call Money Rate moves (overnight rate)
        ↓
Short-term MCLR/Save rates adjust
        ↓
Long-term lending rates adjust
        ↓
Borrowing cost changes → Demand changes
        ↓
Aggregate Demand changes
        ↓
Inflation/Growth affected
```

### 3.2 External Benchmark Lending Rate

- Effective 1 April 2019, RBI mandated that all new floating-rate personal, retail, and MSME loans be linked to external benchmarks (Repo Rate, T-Bill yield, or any other market benchmark)
- Improves monetary policy transmission
- Replaced older internal benchmarks (MCLR, Base Rate)

**Common External Benchmarks**:
- RBI Repo Rate (most common)
- 91-day T-Bill yield
- 182-day T-Bill yield
- Any other benchmark published by FBIL

## 4. Open Market & Other Operations

### 4.1 Open Market Operations (OMO)
- Outright purchase/sale of G-Secs for durable liquidity
- Used to manage liquidity beyond LAF

### 4.2 Market Stabilisation Scheme (MSS)
- Used to manage liquidity arising from large forex inflows
- Treasury Bills issued for sterilisation

### 4.3 Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR)
- Basel III requirement
- High Quality Liquid Assets (HQLA) ≥ 30-day net cash outflows
- LCR = HQLA / Net cash outflows over 30 days
- Minimum: 100%

### 4.4 Marginal Standing Facility (MSF) Usage Pattern
- Banks use MSF in tight liquidity conditions
- Penalty rate (higher than repo) discourages overuse

### 4.5 Standing Deposit Facility (SDF)
- Replaced fixed reverse repo rate in April 2022
- Allows banks to park excess liquidity with RBI at floor rate
- Uncollateralised (unlike reverse repo)
- Market-determined rate

### B. Descriptive Questions (3)

**Q16.** Discuss the composition, role, and significance of the Monetary Policy Committee in India's inflation targeting framework.

**Model Answer**: The MPC was constituted on 29 September 2016 under Sections 45ZA and 45ZB of the RBI Act, 1934 (inserted by Finance Act 2016). It comprises 6 members: RBI Governor (Chairperson), RBI Deputy Governor in charge of monetary policy, one RBI officer nominated by Central Board, and three external members appointed by Central Government (each for a four-year term).

Role: MPC decides the policy repo rate required to maintain inflation at 4% (with 2-6% tolerance band). MPC meets at least 4 times a year (typically 6), and publishes minutes 14 days after each meeting. Decisions are by majority vote; Governor has casting vote.

Significance: Brought transparency and accountability to monetary policy. Made RBI's framework more institutional than discretionary. Aligned India with global central bank practices. Quorum requirement of 4 ensures broad consensus.

**Q17.** Explain the LAF corridor mechanism. How does SDF differ from Reverse Repo?

**Model Answer**: The Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF) is RBI's main tool for managing day-to-day liquidity. The LAF corridor consists of three rates:
- **MSF Rate** (ceiling) — penal rate for emergency overnight borrowing
- **Repo Rate** (mid-point) — policy rate for short-term liquidity
- **SDF Rate** (floor) — uncollateralised overnight deposit acceptance

Corridor width: 50 bps (25 bps above/below repo).

SDF vs Reverse Repo:
- Reverse Repo: Collateralised; banks park excess liquidity against G-Secs; rate set at RBI discretion (currently 3.35%)
- SDF: Uncollateralised; introduced April 2022; floor of corridor (25 bps below repo); replaced fixed reverse repo as floor
- Reverse Repo remains a tool but SDF is the operative floor rate

**Q18.** What is the difference between Repo Rate and MSF Rate? When does a bank use MSF?

**Model Answer**:
- **Repo Rate**: Policy rate at which RBI provides liquidity against G-Sec collateral under LAF. Mid-point of corridor.
- **MSF Rate**: Penal rate (25 bps above repo) for emergency overnight borrowing. Banks can dip into SLR portfolio up to 2% below the prescribed SLR.

A bank uses MSF when:
1. Faced with severe liquidity shortage at end-of-day
2. Has exhausted normal repo borrowing
3. Willing to dip into SLR portfolio (incurs penalty for breaking SLR temporarily)
4. Cannot find liquidity in inter-bank market

MSF acts as a safety valve — penal rate discourages routine use, but ensures no bank fails due to overnight liquidity crunch.
