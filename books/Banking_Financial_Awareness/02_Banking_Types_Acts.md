# Banking Types & Banking Regulation Acts

## 1. Conceptual Deep Dive & Historical Background

### 1.1 Evolution of Banking in India

The Indian banking system has evolved over centuries from indigenous bankers (shroffs, seths, sahukars) during the Mughal era, through the Presidency Banks era (Bank of Calcutta/Bengal 1806, Bank of Bombay 1840, Bank of Madras 1843), to the modern scheduled commercial banking system. The three Presidency Banks were amalgamated in 1921 to form the **Imperial Bank of India**, which was later nationalised in 1955 and renamed the **State Bank of India (SBI)** on 1 July 1955 .

The most transformational phase began with the **nationalisation of 14 major commercial banks** on **19 July 1969** (under the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Ordinance, 1969) by PM Indira Gandhi, with deposits exceeding ₹50 crore. A second round of nationalisation followed on **15 April 1980**, bringing 6 more banks under government control. This expanded public sector presence to dominate ~91% of banking .

The next major structural reform came with the **Narasimham Committee recommendations** (1991 and 1998) which liberalised banking, introduced prudential norms, asset classification, and paved the way for new private sector banks. The **2015–2020 mega merger** reduced 27 Public Sector Banks (PSBs) to **12 PSBs** (effective 1 April 2020), creating globally-sized banks through amalgamation.

### 1.2 Constitutional & Statutory Basis

Banking in India is governed by a complex web of statutes:

- **Article 39(b)** of the Indian Constitution directs the State to ensure that the ownership and control of material resources are so distributed as to subserve the common good.
- **Entry 45, List I (Union List)** of the Seventh Schedule gives Parliament exclusive power to legislate on "Banking" — meaning the Union Government (RBI) controls banking policy.
- **Entry 43, List I** covers "Incorporation, regulation and winding up of corporations" including banking companies.
- **Entry 28, List III (Concurrent List)** covers money lending by individuals.

The principal statutes regulating banking activity are the **Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934** (RBI's parent Act), the **Banking Regulation Act, 1949** (operational regulation of banks), and sector-specific Acts like **SARFAESI Act 2002**, **NI Act 1881**, **IBC 2016**, and **PMLA 2002**.

### 1.3 Classification of Banks in India

Banks in India are classified along multiple dimensions:

#### A. Scheduled vs Non-Scheduled Banks
- **Scheduled Banks**: Banks listed in the **Second Schedule of the RBI Act, 1934** under Section 2(e). They are eligible for borrowing from RBI for normal banking business. Paid-up capital must be ≥₹5 lakh (historical threshold; raised over time).
- **Non-Scheduled Banks**: Banks not in the Second Schedule; smaller; not eligible for RBI refinance facilities.

- Duality of control: Banking functions → RBI; Management → State/Central Govt

## 2. Regulatory Framework & Key Acts

### 2.1 Banking Regulation Act, 1949 (BR Act)
- **Came into force: 16 March 1949**
- Applicable to all banking companies in India
- **1966 Amendment**: Banking laws extended to Cooperative Societies
- Key sections:
  - **Section 22**: Licensing of banking companies
  - **Section 35A**: Supersession of boards (RBI's supersession power used in YES Bank 2020)
  - **Section 35(6)**: NABARD inspection of StCBs and CCBs
  - **Section 49**: Bank Rate definition
  - **Section 56**: Cooperative societies inclusion

### 2.2 RBI Act, 1934
- Establishes the RBI; defines its functions
- **2016 Amendment**: Statutory basis for Monetary Policy Committee (Section 45ZA, 45ZB)
- Section 22: RBI's authority to regulate monetary policy
- Section 42: Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) provision

### 2.3 Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 (NI Act)
- Defines and regulates negotiable instruments: promissory notes, bills of exchange, cheques
- **Section 138**: Dishonour of cheque (penalty up to 2× cheque amount or ₹50,000)
- **Section 141**: Offences by companies — director/partners' liability
- **Sections 118–138**: Various NI aspects (presentation, acceptance, etc.)

### 2.4 SARFAESI Act, 2002
**Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act, 2002**

Recovery mechanism for NPAs:
1. **Securitisation** — Pooling of financial assets and issuing securities
2. **Asset Reconstruction** — Sale to ARCs (Asset Reconstruction Companies)
3. **Enforcement of Security Interest** — Without court intervention (60-day notice, then take possession)
- Min loan threshold for SARFAESI: ₹2 lakh (originally); raised to ₹20 lakh (for the secured asset to be enforced)
- Excludes: Agriculture land below ₹2 lakh

### 2.5 Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016
- Enacted 2016; came into force: **1 December 2016**
- Key institutions:
  - **NCLT** (National Company Law Tribunal) — Adjudicating Authority
  - **NCLAT** (National Company Law Appellate Tribunal) — Appellate
  - **IBBI** (Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India) — Regulator
- **CIRP (Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process)**:
  1. Default (₹1 crore threshold — raised periodically)
  2. Application to NCLT (financial creditor, operational creditor, or corporate debtor)
  3. Interim Resolution Professional (IRP) appointed
  4. Committee of Creditors (CoC) constituted
  5. Resolution Plan within **180 days + 90 days extension** (max 330 days)
  6. If no resolution → **Liquidation**

### 2.6 Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA), 2002
- Enacted 2002; Amended 2005, 2009, 2012
- **FIU-IND** (Financial Intelligence Unit-India): Set up 2004, under Department of Revenue
- **Section 3**: Offence of money laundering
- **Section 4**: Punishment (rigorous imprisonment 3–7 years + fine)
- **Section 5**: Attachment of property
- **Adjudicating Authority**: PMLA Adjudicating Authority
- **Appellate Tribunal**: PMLA Appellate Tribunal

## 5. Case Study: YES Bank Reconstruction (2020)

**Background**: YES Bank, founded 2004, expanded aggressively. By March 2020, gross NPAs surged; deposit withdrawal panic.

**RBI Action (5 March 2020)**:
1. RBI superseded Board under **Section 35A** of BR Act, 1949
2. Moratorium for 30 days (extended)
3. Withdrawal limit ₹50,000 (later relaxed)
4. Scheme of reconstruction announced

**Rescue Plan**:
- SBI-led consortium invested ₹7,250 crore
- Other banks: ICICI, HDFC, Axis, Kotak, Federal, Bandhan
- Investors: ~75% write-down
- 7 banks collectively hold ~49% stake

**Outcome**: Prashant Kumar appointed MD & CEO (March 2020). Bank stabilised; moratorium lifted 18 March 2020.

**Lessons**:
1. Importance of RBI's prudential oversight
2. Power of Section 35A supersession
3. Coordinated resolution via consortium approach
4. Need for robust risk management in PSBs

### B. Descriptive Questions (3)

**Q16.** Explain the powers of RBI under Section 35A of the Banking Regulation Act, 1949, with reference to the YES Bank case.

**Model Answer**: Section 35A of the BR Act empowers RBI to issue directions to banking companies in public interest, banking policy, or to prevent affairs detrimental to depositors' interests. RBI can also supersede the Board of Directors and appoint an Administrator.

In the YES Bank case (March 2020), RBI used Section 35A to:
1. Supersede YES Bank's Board (5 March 2020)
2. Impose moratorium — initially 30 days
3. Cap withdrawals at ₹50,000 (later relaxed)
4. Announce reconstruction scheme

Subsequently, SBI-led consortium rescued YES Bank with ₹7,250 crore investment. Moratorium lifted 18 March 2020. This demonstrated RBI's decisive intervention capability and section 35A as a key crisis-resolution tool.

**Q17.** Compare Scheduled, Non-Scheduled, Public Sector, Private Sector, Foreign, RRBs, SFBs and Payments Banks. (250 words)

**Model Answer**: Scheduled Banks are listed in the Second Schedule of RBI Act, 1934; eligible for RBI borrowing. Non-Scheduled Banks are not listed.

Public Sector Banks (12 after 2020 merger) are majority government-owned. Private Sector Banks are majority privately-owned (HDFC, ICICI, Axis). Foreign Banks operate via branches (Citi, HSBC).

RRBs (43 in number) are 50% Central, 35% Sponsor Bank, 15% State-owned; primarily for rural credit. SFBs (10 active) target financial inclusion with 75% Priority Sector lending. Payments Banks (6 active) can accept deposits up to ₹2 lakh but cannot lend; targeted at unbanked population.

Key differences:
- **Lending**: PSBs, Private, Foreign, RRBs, SFBs lend; Payments Banks don't
- **Deposit cap**: Payments Banks capped at ₹2 lakh
- **Priority Sector**: All have targets; SFBs highest (75%)

**Q18.** Discuss the role of SARFAESI Act in NPA recovery. What are its limitations?

**Model Answer**: The SARFAESI Act, 2002 enables banks/FIs to recover NPAs without court intervention through three mechanisms: Securitisation, Asset Reconstruction, and Enforcement of Security Interest.

Process:
1. Loan account becomes NPA
2. Bank issues 60-day notice to borrower
3. If borrower fails to pay, bank takes possession
4. Asset sold; proceeds adjusted against dues

**Limitations**:
- Not applicable to loans <₹2 lakh threshold (raised)
- Cannot be used against agricultural land below specified limits
- Secured assets only — unsecured loans require DRT
- Borrowers can approach Debt Recovery Tribunal (DRT)
- SARFAESI vs IBC interplay — IBC takes precedence in some cases
- Restriction on small borrowers
