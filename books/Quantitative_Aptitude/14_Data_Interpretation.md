## 1. Conceptual Foundation

### 1.1 What is Data Interpretation?

**Data Interpretation (DI)** is the process of **analyzing, comparing, and computing** numerical information presented in various visual/numerical formats. The data is "given"; the task is to "interpret" it through calculations.

### 1.2 The Six Major DI Formats

| Format | Description | Difficulty |
|--------|-------------|------------|
| **Table** | Rows/columns of numbers | Easy to Medium |
| **Bar Chart** | Vertical/horizontal bars | Easy to Medium |
| **Pie Chart** | Sectors of a circle (slices) | Medium |
| **Line Graph** | Trends over time | Medium |
| **Caselet** | Paragraph-based (no visual) | Medium to Hard |
| **Data Sufficiency** | "Which statement(s) needed?" | Hard |

### 1.3 The Three Pillars of DI Mastery

1. **Speed reading** — grasp the structure in 30 sec.
2. **Percentage comfort** — convert ratios, fractions, differences to % quickly.
3. **Approximation** — eliminate wrong options without exact calc.

## 2. Tables (Tabular DI)

### 2.1 What is a Table DI?

A **table** presents data in rows and columns. Each row usually represents a category (e.g., year, company, country); each column represents a variable (e.g., sales, profit, population).

### 2.2 How to Read a Table

**5-Step Table DI Strategy:**
1. **Read the title** to understand the context.
2. **Read row headings** and column headings.
3. **Identify units** (lakhs, crores, millions, %).
4. **Scan the numbers** for notable values (max, min, totals).
5. **For each question**, locate the relevant cells, then compute.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS — DI strategy]

### 2.3 Common Table Question Types

**Type 1: Direct Lookup** — "What is the value of X in Y?"
- Read off the table.

**Type 2: Total/Sum** — "What is the total of X over all years?"
- Sum the relevant cells.

**Type 3: Difference** — "What is the difference between A and B in Y?"
- Subtract two cells.

**Type 4: Percentage** — "X is what % of Y?"
- (X/Y) × 100.

**Type 5: Ratio** — "What is the ratio of A to B?"
- A: B = simplify.

**Type 6: Average** — "What is the average of X?"
- Sum/n.

**Type 7: Percentage Change** — "From 2020 to 2021, sales increased by what %?"
- (New - Old)/Old × 100.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS — standard DI classification]

### 2.4 Example: Table on Company Sales

**Table: Annual Sales (in Crores) of 5 Companies**

| Company | 2020 | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 |
|---------|------|------|------|------|------|
| A | 100 | 120 | 150 | 180 | 200 |
| B | 80 | 90 | 110 | 130 | 150 |
| C | 50 | 70 | 80 | 100 | 130 |
| D | 200 | 220 | 250 | 280 | 300 |
| E | 60 | 80 | 100 | 110 | 140 |

**Q1:** What is the total sales of Company C from 2020 to 2024?
**Solution:** 50+70+80+100+130 = 430. **Answer: ₹430 Crores.** *Time: 30 sec.*

**Q2:** Which company has the highest % growth from 2020 to 2024?
**Solution:** A: (200-100)/100 = 100%. B: (150-80)/80 = 87.5%. C: (130-50)/50 = 160%. D: (300-200)/200 = 50%. E: (140-60)/60 = 133.3%. **Highest: C (160%).** *Time: 90 sec.*

**Q3:** What is the average sales of Company D over 5 years?
**Solution:** (200+220+250+280+300)/5 = 1250/5 = 250. **Answer: ₹250 Crores.** *Time: 30 sec.*

**Q4:** What is the ratio of total sales of A to B over 5 years?
**Solution:** A total = 100+120+150+180+200 = 750. B total = 80+90+110+130+150 = 560. Ratio = 750:560 = 75:56. *Time: 60 sec.*

## 3. Bar Charts

### 3.1 What is a Bar Chart?

A **bar chart** uses rectangular bars of varying heights (or lengths) to represent values. Vertical bars are common; horizontal bars used for ranking.

### 3.2 How to Read a Bar Chart

**Key elements:**
- **X-axis** (horizontal): Categories.
- **Y-axis** (vertical): Values (with scale).
- **Bar height/length**: Magnitude.

**Reading approach:**
1. Identify the **scale** of Y-axis (each grid line =?).
2. Note the **units**.
3. For comparison questions, **use a ruler/edge** of paper to compare heights.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS — DI strategy]

### 3.3 Bar Chart Examples

**Example: Bar chart showing production of 5 factories in 2024**

```
Factory A:  ████████ 800
Factory B:  ██████ 600
Factory C:  █████████ 900
Factory D:  ████ 400
Factory E:  █████ 500
            0    200  400  600  800  1000
```

**Q1:** Which factory has the highest production?
**Solution:** Factory C (900 units). *Time: 5 sec.*

**Q2:** What is the average production?
**Solution:** (800+600+900+400+500)/5 = 3200/5 = 640. *Time: 30 sec.*

**Q3:** Production of A and B together is what % of total production?
**Solution:** A+B = 800+600 = 1400. Total = 3200. % = 1400/3200 × 100 = 43.75%. *Time: 60 sec.*

### 3.4 Compound Bar Chart (Stacked/Grouped)

**Stacked Bar:** Bars stacked to show components.
**Grouped Bar:** Side-by-side bars for comparison.

**Example:** Sales of Product X and Product Y for 5 years (grouped):
- 2020: X=100, Y=50
- 2021: X=120, Y=70
- 2022: X=150, Y=80
- 2023: X=180, Y=100
- 2024: X=200, Y=130

**Q:** In which year is the ratio of X to Y the highest?
**Solution:** 2020: 100/50 = 2.0. 2021: 120/70 ≈ 1.71. 2022: 150/80 ≈ 1.88. 2023: 180/100 = 1.8. 2024: 200/130 ≈ 1.54. **Highest: 2020 (2.0).** *Time: 90 sec.*

## 4. Pie Charts

### 4.1 What is a Pie Chart?

A **pie chart** is a circle divided into sectors (slices), where each sector represents a proportion of the total. The whole circle = 100% = 360°.

**Conversion:** 1% = 3.6° (or equivalently, 1° = 1/360 ≈ 0.278%).

### 4.2 Key Pie Chart Concepts

- **Total = 100%** (the whole circle).
- **Angle of slice = (% of total) × 3.6°**.
- **% of total represented by angle = (angle/360) × 100**.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS — standard concept]

### 4.3 Pie Chart Reading Approach

1. Note the **total value** (the actual number the % represents).
2. For each slice: **actual value = (% / 100) × total**.
3. For questions about angles: convert % to angle using × 3.6°.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

### 4.4 Example: Pie Chart of Expenditure (Monthly = ₹60,000)

| Category | % | Angle | Value (₹) |
|----------|---|-------|-----------|
| Rent | 25% | 90° | 15,000 |
| Food | 20% | 72° | 12,000 |
| Transport | 15% | 54° | 9,000 |
| Education | 10% | 36° | 6,000 |
| Savings | 20% | 72° | 12,000 |
| Misc | 10% | 36° | 6,000 |
| **Total** | **100%** | **360°** | **60,000** |

**Q1:** What is the angle for Education?
**Solution:** 10% × 3.6° = 36°. *Time: 10 sec.*

**Q2:** What % of expenditure is on Rent + Food?
**Solution:** 25% + 20% = 45%. *Time: 10 sec.*

**Q3:** What is the difference between Rent and Education in ₹?
**Solution:** ₹15,000 - ₹6,000 = ₹9,000. *Time: 30 sec.*

**Q4:** If savings increase by 50%, what is the new savings?
**Solution:** New savings = 12,000 × 1.5 = 18,000. *Time: 30 sec.*

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS — constructed standard pie chart example]

### 4.5 Double Pie Chart

Two pie charts comparing two time periods or two categories.

**Example:** Pie 1 (2020 expenditure) and Pie 2 (2024 expenditure).

**Q:** "Expenditure on which category has the highest absolute increase?"
**Solution:** Calculate (Pie 2 value) - (Pie 1 value) for each category; find max.

## 5. Line Graphs

### 5.1 What is a Line Graph?

A **line graph** shows trends over time (or any continuous variable). Points are connected by line segments. Best for **trend analysis** (increasing/decreasing/cyclic).

### 5.2 Line Graph Reading Approach

1. Read **X-axis** (usually time) and **Y-axis** (variable).
2. Note the **scale**.
3. For trend: observe **direction of lines** (up, down, zigzag).
4. For values: read at specific points.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

### 5.3 Example: Line Graph of Sales (₹ Lakhs) 2019-2024

- 2019: 100
- 2020: 120
- 2021: 90 (decline)
- 2022: 150
- 2023: 180
- 2024: 200

**Q1:** In which year was the maximum growth rate?
**Solution:** 2019→2020: +20%. 2020→2021: -25%. 2021→2022: +66.7%. 2022→2023: +20%. 2023→2024: +11.1%. **Maximum: 2021→2022 (66.7%).** *Time: 90 sec.*

**Q2:** What is the average sales for 2019-2024?
**Solution:** (100+120+90+150+180+200)/6 = 840/6 = 140. **Answer: ₹140 lakhs.** *Time: 30 sec.*

**Q3:** In how many years did sales decrease from the previous year?
**Solution:** Only 2020→2021 (decreased from 120 to 90). **Answer: 1 year.** *Time: 30 sec.*

## 6. Caselets (Paragraph DI)

### 6.1 What is a Caselet?

A **caselet** is a paragraph (or short passage) that describes a numerical scenario. No chart or table — only text with numbers. The student must extract relevant data and compute.

### 6.2 Caselet Strategy

1. **Read the entire passage** first to understand the scenario.
2. **Highlight/underline** the numbers and their relationships.
3. **Convert to a table** mentally (or on rough paper) for easy access.
4. **For each question**, locate the relevant data and compute.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS — DI strategy]

### 6.3 Example Caselet

**Caselet 1:** "Total students in a school = 1000. 40% are girls. Of the girls, 30% study Science. Of the boys, 50% study Science."

**Convert to table:**
| | Girls | Boys | Total |
|---|-------|------|-------|
| Total | 400 | 600 | 1000 |
| Science | 120 | 300 | 420 |
| Non-Science | 280 | 300 | 580 |

**Q1:** What % of total students study Science?
**Solution:** 420/1000 = 42%. *Time: 30 sec.*

**Q2:** What is the ratio of girls studying Science to boys studying Science?
**Solution:** 120:300 = 2:5. *Time: 30 sec.*

**Q3:** What % of Science students are girls?
**Solution:** 120/420 × 100 ≈ 28.57%. *Time: 30 sec.*

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS — standard caselet pattern]

**Caselet 2:** "A's salary is 40% more than B's. B's salary is 20% less than C's. C earns ₹50,000."
- C = 50,000
- B = 50,000 × 0.80 = 40,000
- A = 40,000 × 1.40 = 56,000

**Q:** Total salary of A, B, C?
**Solution:** 56,000 + 40,000 + 50,000 = 1,46,000. *Time: 60 sec.*

## 7. Data Sufficiency (DS) — Comprehensive Coverage

### 7.1 What is Data Sufficiency?

In a **Data Sufficiency** question, the student is given a question (often involving a numerical computation) and two or three **statements**. The task is to determine **whether the statements alone or together are sufficient to answer the question**.

DS is heavily tested in **IBPS PO/Clerk, SBI PO, RBI Grade B, LIC AAO, Insurance AO**, and **2-3 questions per SSC CGL Tier-I in some shifts**.

### 7.2 The 5-Option Standard DS Format (Banking Mains Pattern)

The **canonical 5-choice format** used in IBPS/SBI/RBI:

| Choice | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| (a) | Statement 1 alone is sufficient, but Statement 2 alone is not. |
| (b) | Statement 2 alone is sufficient, but Statement 1 alone is not. |
| (c) | Both statements together are sufficient, but neither alone. |
| (d) | Each statement alone is sufficient. |
| (e) | Statements together are not sufficient. |

**SSC variant (3-choice format):**
- (1) Statement 1 alone sufficient → answer
- (2) Statement 2 alone sufficient → answer
- (3) Both together or neither → no unique answer

### 7.3 The Three-Step DS Strategy

```
Step 1: Analyze Statement 1 ALONE — Can you uniquely answer?
        If YES → eliminate choices that require Statement 2.
Step 2: Analyze Statement 2 ALONE — Can you uniquely answer?
        If YES → eliminate choices that require Statement 1.
Step 3: If neither alone is sufficient, COMBINE both.
        If combined uniquely answers → answer (c).
        If still not unique → answer (e).
```

### 7.4 Classification of DS Questions

**Type 1: Quantitative DS** (most common — IBPS PO Mains)
- Topics: ages, percentages, ratios, work-time, profit-loss, averages.
- Logic: check if the data has enough equations/values to find unknowns.

**Type 2: Order/Ranking DS**
- "Who is tallest?", "Who came first in the race?"
- Logic: chain of inequalities sufficient to determine the ranking.

**Type 3: Logical Reasoning DS**
- Syllogism, direction, coding-decoding.
- Logic: deduce from logical premises.

**Type 4: Geometry DS**
- "What is the area of the triangle?"
- Logic: use given dimensions/formulas to determine area.

### 7.5 The "Unique Answer" Test

A statement is **sufficient** only if it yields a **unique answer** (one value). If multiple answers are consistent with the data, the statement is **not sufficient**.

**Example:**
- Q: What is X? Statement 1: X² = 25.
- Statement 1 alone: X = 5 OR X = −5. Two valid answers → NOT sufficient.

### 7.6 The Five DS Decision Rules

| Condition | Answer |
|-----------|--------|
| Only Statement 1 gives unique answer | (a) |
| Only Statement 2 gives unique answer | (b) |
| Neither alone, but combined gives unique answer | (c) |
| Each statement alone gives unique answer (and same answer) | (d) |
| Even combined doesn't give unique answer | (e) |

### 7.7 DS Worked Examples (from Standard Exam ReferenceReasoning Ch.8 patterns)

**Example 1 (Quantitative — Easy):**
> Q: What is the value of $x + y$?
> Statement 1: $x = 5$
> Statement 2: $y = 7$
>
> - S1 alone: x = 5, but y unknown. NOT sufficient.
> - S2 alone: y = 7, but x unknown. NOT sufficient.
> - Combined: x + y = 5 + 7 = 12. SUFFICIENT.
>
> **Answer: (c)** [Standard DS pattern from Standard Exam ReferenceCh.8]

**Example 2 (Direction — Easy):**
> Q: In which direction is A facing?
> Statement 1: A is facing North or South.
> Statement 2: If A turns right, A will face East.
>
> - S1 alone: 2 possibilities (N or S). NOT sufficient.
> - S2 alone: "If right turn → East" → A facing North (since N + right turn = E). SUFFICIENT.
>
> **Answer: (b)**

**Example 3 (Ages — Medium):**
> Q: What is A's age?
> Statement 1: A is 5 years older than B.
> Statement 2: B is 3 years younger than C.
>
> - S1 alone: A = B + 5. Without B, can't find A. NOT sufficient.
> - S2 alone: B = C − 3. Without C, can't find B (or A). NOT sufficient.
> - Combined: A = C + 2. Without C, still NOT sufficient.
>
> **Answer: (e)**

**Example 4 (Either-Case — Medium):**
> Q: What is X?
> Statement 1: X + Y = 10.
> Statement 2: 2X = 20 − 2Y.
>
> - S1 alone: 2 unknowns, 1 equation. NOT sufficient.
> - S2 alone: 2X + 2Y = 20 → X + Y = 10. Same as S1. NOT sufficient.
> - Combined: 2 equations but same equation. NOT sufficient.
>
> **Answer: (e)** [Pattern from Standard Exam ReferenceCh.8]

**Example 5 (Either-Case where both alone are sufficient):**
> Q: What is X?
> Statement 1: X = 5.
> Statement 2: 2X = 10.
>
> - S1 alone: X = 5 directly. SUFFICIENT.
> - S2 alone: 2X = 10 → X = 5. SUFFICIENT.
>
> **Answer: (d)** [Pattern from Standard Exam ReferenceCh.8]

**Example 6 (Order/Ranking — Medium):**
> Q: Who is the tallest among A, B, C?
> Statement 1: A is taller than B.
> Statement 2: B is taller than C.
>
> - S1 alone: A > B, but C unknown. NOT sufficient.
> - S2 alone: B > C, but A unknown. NOT sufficient.
> - Combined: A > B > C → A tallest. SUFFICIENT.
>
> **Answer: (c)**

**Example 7 (Logical Reasoning — Medium):**
> Q: Is A taller than B?
> Statement 1: A is taller than C.
> Statement 2: B is shorter than C.
>
> - S1 alone: A > C, but B unknown. NOT sufficient.
> - S2 alone: B < C, but A unknown. NOT sufficient.
> - Combined: A > C > B → A > B. SUFFICIENT.
>
> **Answer: (c)**

**Example 8 (Work-Time — Medium):**
> Q: How many days did Rakesh take to complete the project?
> Statement 1: Rakesh worked 8 hours a day.
> Statement 2: The project took 240 hours of total work.
>
> - S1 alone: hours per day known, but no total hours. NOT sufficient.
> - S2 alone: total hours known, but no hours per day. NOT sufficient.
> - Combined: 240 hours / 8 hours per day = 30 days. SUFFICIENT.
>
> **Answer: (c)**

### 7.8 The 10 Common Traps in DS

1. **"I alone" trap**: Don't combine I and II mentally when testing I alone.
2. **"Sufficient ≠ Unique" trap**: Multiple valid answers ≠ sufficient.
3. **"X² = 25" trap**: X could be 5 or −5.
4. **"X + Y = 10" trap**: Two unknowns, infinite solutions. Insufficient alone.
5. **Negation trap**: "If X is not less than Y" means X ≥ Y, not X > Y.
6. **Range trap**: "X is between 5 and 10" → multiple integers possible.
7. **Odd/Even trap**: "X is even" → doesn't pin down X without more info.
8. **Conditional sufficiency**: "If X > 5, then Y = 10" — only conditional info.
9. **False dichotomy**: Two options listed, but other possibilities may exist.
10. **Reverse logic**: "P is not Q" → doesn't mean P is always the opposite of Q.

## 8. Speed-Solving Shortcuts

### 8.1 The "Approximate Percentage" Trick

For multiple-choice DI questions, **approximate the answer** to within 5% of options. This eliminates 2-3 options quickly.

**Example:** "Sales of A in 2024 is what % of total sales of all companies in 2024?"
- A = 200, Total = 920. 200/920 ≈ 21.7%.
- Look for answer near 21-22%. If options are 10%, 22%, 50%, 75% — choose 22%.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

### 8.2 The "Total First" Trick

For questions like "X is what % of total?" or "What is the ratio of X to others?", first **calculate the total**, then compute.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

### 8.3 The "Cross-Multiplication" Trick

For ratio problems in pie charts: If A:B = 3:2 and total = 500, then A = 500 × 3/5 = 300.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

### 8.4 The "Same Denominator" Trick

When comparing ratios or percentages, **convert all to the same denominator** for easy comparison.

**Example:** Compare 2/3, 3/5, 4/7.
- 2/3 ≈ 0.667, 3/5 = 0.6, 4/7 ≈ 0.571. Order: 2/3 > 3/5 > 4/7.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

### 8.5 The "Net Change" Trick

For trend questions: calculate (Final - Initial) directly instead of computing % change in steps.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

### 8.6 The "Sum of Angles" Trick

For pie chart questions, remember **all angles = 360°** and **all % = 100%**. Use this for verification.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

### 8.7 The "Visual Estimation" Trick

For bar/pie/line questions, **eyeball the answer** first, then verify with calculation. Saves 30-60 sec per question.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

**Q8.** Bar chart: A=80, B=100, C=120, D=140. B is what % of total?

**Solution:** Total = 80+100+120+140 = 440. B% = 100/440 ≈ 22.7%. **Closest: (b) 22.5%.** *Time: 45 sec.*

**Q9.** Line graph: 2019=50, 2020=70, 2021=90, 2022=110, 2023=130. % increase from 2019 to 2023:
(a) 100% (b) 120% (c) 160% (d) 200%

**Solution:** (130-50)/50 = 160%. **Answer: (c) 160%.** *Time: 45 sec.*

**Q10.** Caselet: "School has 600 students. 60% are boys. 40% of girls study science." How many girls study science?
(a) 96 (b) 100 (c) 120 (d) 144

**Solution:** Girls = 600 × 0.4 = 240. Girls in science = 240 × 0.4 = 96. **Answer: (a) 96.** *Time: 45 sec.*

**Q11.** Table: Two products A, B with sales. A=500, B=300. Ratio A:B:
(a) 3:5 (b) 5:3 (c) 1:1 (d) 2:3

**Solution:** 500:300 = 5:3. **Answer: (b) 5:3.** *Time: 20 sec.*

**Q12.** Pie chart: Total=800. A=40%. Value of A:
(a) 200 (b) 320 (c) 400 (d) 480

**Solution:** 40% × 800 = 320. **Answer: (b) 320.** *Time: 15 sec.*

**Q13.** Bar chart: 4 products with sales 100, 200, 300, 400. Average:
(a) 200 (b) 250 (c) 300 (d) 350

**Solution:** (100+200+300+400)/4 = 1000/4 = 250. **Answer: (b) 250.** *Time: 30 sec.*

### Set C — Advanced (Q14–Q18)

**Q14.** Table shows 5 years of company data:
| Year | 2020 | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 |
|------|------|------|------|------|------|
| Sales | 100 | 110 | 121 | 133.1 | 146.4 |

What is the approximate growth rate per year (CAGR)?
(a) 8% (b) 10% (c) 12% (d) 15%

**Solution:** CAGR = (146.4/100)^(1/4) - 1 = (1.464)^0.25 - 1 ≈ 1.10 - 1 = 10%. **Answer: (b) 10%.** *Time: 90 sec.*

**Q15.** Double pie chart: 2020 and 2024. 2020: A=50%, B=30%, C=20%. 2024: A=40%, B=35%, C=25%. If 2020 total = 1000, 2024 total = 1500, the absolute increase in A:
(a) 100 (b) 200 (c) 300 (d) 50

**Solution:** 2020 A = 500. 2024 A = 600. Increase = 100. **Answer: (a) 100.** *Time: 60 sec.*

**Q16.** Caselet: "A's income is 20% more than B's. B's income is 10% less than C's. If C = ₹50,000, find A's income."
(a) ₹45,000 (b) ₹48,000 (c) ₹54,000 (d) ₹60,000

**Solution:** B = 50,000 × 0.9 = 45,000. A = 45,000 × 1.2 = 54,000. **Answer: (c) ₹54,000.** *Time: 60 sec.*

**Q17.** Combined chart: 3 products A, B, C with monthly sales:

Which product has the highest average?

**Solution:** A avg = 725/6 ≈ 120.8. B avg = 595/6 ≈ 99.2. C avg = 460/6 ≈ 76.7. **Answer: (a) A.** *Time: 90 sec.*

**Q18.** A table shows: 5 companies, each with 2 products. Total sales: 1000+2000 = 3000. What % of total is from Company 1?
(a) 25% (b) 33.3% (c) 50% (d) 66.7%

**Solution:** 1000/3000 = 33.3%. **Answer: (b) 33.3%.** *Time: 30 sec.*

## 12. Chapter Summary & Revision Card

### 12.1 Compact Formula Table

| DI Format | Key Strategy | Time/Question |
|-----------|--------------|---------------|
| Table | Read row/column, locate value | 30-60 sec |
| Bar Chart | Use scale on Y-axis | 20-45 sec |
| Pie Chart | Use % × Total = Value; Angle = % × 3.6° | 30-60 sec |
| Line Graph | Track trends, compute % change | 30-60 sec |
| Caselet | Build mental table, extract data | 60-90 sec |
| Data Sufficiency | 3-step analysis (Stmt 1, Stmt 2, Both) | 60-90 sec |

### 12.2 Top 5 Shortcuts

1. **Approximate first** — eliminate 2-3 options, then compute exact.
2. **Read the scale carefully** — wrong scale = wrong answer.
3. **Highlight/underline** key numbers in caselets.
4. **Use a ruler/edge** for comparing bar heights.
5. **Pie chart: angle/3.6 = %; % × total = value**.

### 12.3 5 Common Mistakes

1. **Wrong scale reading** — each grid line =? units.
2. **Confusing rows and columns** in tables.
3. **Forgetting to convert % to actual value** in pie charts.
4. **Calculation errors in caselets** — build a table first.
5. **DS: not checking both statements** — must test each alone, then both.

### 12.4 "If You See ___, Use ___" Decision Rules

| If You See... | Use... |
|---------------|--------|
| "What is the value of X?" | Direct lookup (table/bar) or % × total (pie) |
| "Total of all" | Sum all values |
| "What % of total?" | (X/Total) × 100 |
| "% change from A to B" | ((B-A)/A) × 100 |
| "Ratio of A to B" | A:B = simplify |
| "Average" | Sum/n |
| "Angle of slice" | (% of total) × 3.6° |
| "Data Sufficiency" | 3-step DS strategy |
