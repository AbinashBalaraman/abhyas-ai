## 1. Foundation Theory

### 1.1 What is the Number System?

The **number system** is the set of symbols, rules, and operations used to represent and manipulate quantities. In Indian competitive exams, the quant paper tests the following layers: **Natural numbers, Whole numbers, Integers, Rational numbers, Real numbers, Fractions, Decimals, and Percentages**.

A **natural number** $\mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3,...\}$ is the basic unit of counting. A **whole number** is $\mathbb{W} = \{0, 1, 2, 3,...\}$. Adding negatives and zero gives the **integers** $\mathbb{Z} = \{..., -2, -1, 0, 1, 2,...\}$. Any number that can be written as $\frac{p}{q}$ with $q \neq 0$ is a **rational number**. The set of all rationals and irrationals together forms the **real numbers** $\mathbb{R}$.

### 1.2 Classification of Numbers

| Type | Definition | Example |
|------|------------|---------|
| **Even** | Divisible by 2 | 2, 4, 6, 8, 10,... |
| **Odd** | Not divisible by 2 | 1, 3, 5, 7, 9,... |
| **Prime** | Only factors are 1 and itself | 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19,... |
| **Composite** | Has more than 2 factors | 4, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, 14, 15,... |
| **Co-prime** | HCF of the two numbers = 1 | (4, 9), (15, 28) |
| **Perfect square** | Has an integer square root | 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36,... |
| **Perfect cube** | Has an integer cube root | 1, 8, 27, 64, 125,... |

The only **even prime** is 2; 1 is neither prime nor composite. The **smallest composite** number is 4.

### 1.3 Divisibility Rules

A divisibility rule is a quick test to check whether an integer is divisible by another without performing the actual division. The **24 essential rules** for SSC/IBPS/RRB are summarized below:

| Divisor | Test | Example |
|---------|------|---------|
| **2** | Last digit is even (0, 2, 4, 6, 8) | 4372 ✓ |
| **3** | Sum of digits divisible by 3 | 429 → 4+2+9=15 ✓ |
| **4** | Last two digits divisible by 4 | 1248 → 48 ✓ |
| **5** | Last digit is 0 or 5 | 72835 ✓ |
| **6** | Divisible by both 2 and 3 | 4752 ✓ |
| **7** | Double the last digit and subtract from rest | 1001: 100 − 2 = 98 ✓ |
| **8** | Last three digits divisible by 8 | 7497 → 497 mod 8 = 1 ✗ |
| **9** | Sum of digits divisible by 9 | 2475 → 18 ✓ |
| **10** | Last digit is 0 | 5670 ✓ |
| **11** | (Sum of odd-place digits) − (Sum of even-place digits) = 0 or multiple of 11 | 1331: (1+3) − (3+1) = 0 ✓ |
| **12** | Divisible by both 3 and 4 | 29412 ✓ |
| **15** | Divisible by both 3 and 5 | 6825 ✓ |
| **25** | Last two digits are 00, 25, 50, or 75 | 7825 ✓ |
| **40** | Divisible by both 5 and 8 | 45360 ✓ |

The **divisibility-by-11 rule** is the most tested in SSC CGL (Q.13, Q.25, Q.28, Q.31, Q.32, Q.33, Q.36, Q.38, Q.41, Q.44, Q.85 in CGL PK 2025). The method is to take the **alternating sum** of digits starting from the rightmost digit.

### 1.4 Prime Factorization and HCF/LCM

Every composite number can be uniquely expressed as a product of prime powers — this is the **Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic**. Example: $360 = 2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5$.

- **HCF (Highest Common Factor)** = product of common prime factors raised to the *minimum* power.
- **LCM (Lowest Common Multiple)** = product of all prime factors raised to the *maximum* power.

For two numbers $a$ and $b$:
$$\text{HCF}(a,b) \times \text{LCM}(a,b) = a \times b$$

This is a **must-memorize identity** — it appears in Q.23, Q.24, Q.27, Q.38, Q.39 of the CGL Practice King.

### 1.5 Surds, Indices, and Algebraic Identities

A **surd** is an irrational root of a rational number, e.g. $\sqrt{2}, \sqrt[3]{5}$. The **laws of indices** are:
- $a^m \times a^n = a^{m+n}$
- $\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}$
- $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$
- $a^0 = 1$ (for $a \neq 0$)
- $a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n}$

The **essential algebraic identities** that are tested in simplification questions:
- $(a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$
- $(a-b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2$
- $a^2 - b^2 = (a+b)(a-b)$
- $a^3 - b^3 = (a-b)(a^2 + ab + b^2)$
- $a^3 + b^3 = (a+b)(a^2 - ab + b^2)$
- $(a+b)^3 = a^3 + b^3 + 3ab(a+b)$ (Q.10, Q.22, Q.25, Q.27 in Simplification)

### 1.7 The BODMAS Rule

For any simplification, follow the order: **B**rackets → **O**f → **D**ivision → **M**ultiplication → **A**ddition → **S**ubtraction. The "of" operator is equivalent to multiplication, and is evaluated before × and ÷. This is the foundation of Q.7–Q.31 in the Simplification chapter.

## 2. Complete Formula Repository (LaTeX)

### 2.1 Number Theory Formulas

$$n! = 1 \times 2 \times 3 \times... \times n$$

$$n! = n \times (n-1)! \quad \text{(Recursive)}$$

$$\binom{n}{r} = \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!} \quad \text{(Combinations)}$$

$$P(n,r) = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!} \quad \text{(Permutations)}$$

### 2.2 Divisibility Quick Tests

$$\text{Div by 11: } \sum_{i\text{ odd}} d_i - \sum_{i\text{ even}} d_i \equiv 0 \pmod{11}$$

$$\text{Div by 7: } d_n d_{n-1}...d_1 \text{ splits into } (d_n d_{n-1} d_{n-2})(d_{n-3} d_{n-4} d_{n-5})...$$

$$\text{Apply alternating } \pm \text{ on these groups; if result is divisible by 7, so is the number.}$$

### 2.3 HCF and LCM Formulas

$$\text{For two numbers } a, b: \quad \text{HCF}(a,b) \times \text{LCM}(a,b) = a \times b$$

$$\text{For fractions } \frac{p_1}{q_1}, \frac{p_2}{q_2}: \quad \text{HCF} = \frac{\text{HCF}(p_1,p_2)}{\text{LCM}(q_1,q_2)}, \quad \text{LCM} = \frac{\text{LCM}(p_1,p_2)}{\text{HCF}(q_1,q_2)}$$

$$\text{If } a: b = m: n, \text{ then numbers are } am \text{ and } bn; \text{ LCM} = m \cdot b \cdot n \text{ and HCF} = k$$

### 2.4 Remainder Theorems

**Euler/Fermat:** $a^{\phi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod{n}$ for $\gcd(a,n)=1$.

**Binomial Expansion:**
$$(a+b)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} a^{n-k} b^k$$

**Cyclic Patterns of Powers:**
- Powers of digits **0, 1, 5, 6** always end in themselves.
- Powers of 2 cycle 2, 4, 8, 6 (period 4).
- Powers of 3 cycle 3, 9, 7, 1 (period 4).
- Powers of 4 cycle 4, 6 (period 2).
- Powers of 7 cycle 7, 9, 3, 1 (period 4).
- Powers of 8 cycle 8, 4, 2, 6 (period 4).
- Powers of 9 cycle 9, 1 (period 2).

### 2.5 Algebraic Identities (Tested Most)

$$a^2 - b^2 = (a-b)(a+b) \quad \text{(Q.22, Q.25 in Simplification)}$$

$$\frac{a^3 - b^3}{a^3 + b^3} = \frac{(a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2)}{(a+b)(a^2-ab+b^2)}$$

$$(a+b+c)^2 = a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + 2(ab+bc+ca)$$

$$\frac{a^2-b^2}{a^2+b^2+ab} = \frac{a-b}{a+b+ab \cdot \frac{1}{a-b}} \text{ (verify for surds)}$$

### 2.6 Mean Proportional

For two numbers $a$ and $b$, the mean proportional is:
$$m = \sqrt{ab} = \frac{ab}{\text{Mean Proportional}}$$

The mean proportional of $a$ and $b$ equals the square root of their product. Used in Q.105 of Number System.

## 3. Speed-Solving Shortcuts & Vedic Methods

### 3.1 The "100, 110, 120" Method for Divisibility by 11

The standard 11-test is slow. The faster trick: write the number as a sum of groups. For 78264: $7-8+2-6+4 = -1$. For 5-digit numbers, pair as $(a+b)(c+d+e)$ and take difference. From CGL PK Q.28: 7845K854, 7+4+K+5 = 16+K, 8+5+8+4 = 25, so K = 9. Direct and fast.

**Vedic alternative:** For divisibility by 11, the **last-digit rule** is even faster. For 4-digit number abba, a+b = b+a (always), so 11 divides it only if 11 divides 11(a-b) → a-b=0 (which contradicts a<b). So no such number can be divisible by 11.

### 3.2 The 1001 Trick for abc-abc

A 6-digit number of form abcabc is **always divisible by 1001** because:
$$\overline{abcabc} = \overline{abc} \times 1001$$

Since $1001 = 7 \times 11 \times 13$, it is automatically divisible by 7, 11, and 13. CGL PK Q.23 confirms: "Any six digit number that is formed by repeating a three-digit number, is always divisible by: (b) 1001". Time saved: skip the long multiplication.

### 3.3 Last-Three-Digit Rule for Divisibility by 8

$1000 = 8 \times 125$, so a number is divisible by 8 **iff its last 3 digits** are. Example: $148109326$ → check $326$ mod $8 = 326 - 40 \times 8 = 6$. So subtract 6. **Caveat:** This is faster than full division but you must compute the mod manually. Master the table: 1000 = 8×125, 100 = 8×12+4, 10 = 8×1+2. So for $abc$: $100a + 10b + c = (8 \cdot 12a + 8 \cdot 1 \cdot b) + 4a + 2b + c$, so **divisible by 8 iff** $4a + 2b + c$ is.

### 3.4 The 24-Fraction Table — Reverse Lookup

Most students memorize forward (1/3 = 33.33%). The **trick is reverse lookup**: when a number is given as a percentage, immediately recognize the underlying fraction.
- $66.67\% = 2/3$
- $83.33\% = 5/6$ 
- $28.57\% = 2/7$
- $14.28\% = 1/7$
- $12.5\% = 1/8$
- $11.11\% = 1/9$
- $9.09\% = 1/11$

In Q.6 of the Standard Exam ReferencePercentage book: "M is 40% more than N, and O is 20% less than N, then M is what percentage more than O?" The reverse table instantly gives $40\% = 2/5$ and $20\% = 1/5$. Time saved: 60 seconds per fraction-conversion question.

### 3.5 Successive Remainder Reduction

For "$n$ when divided by $d$ gives remainder $r$, find $(n^2 + an + b) \mod d$": substitute the smallest $n$ that satisfies $n \equiv r \pmod{d}$.

**CGL PK Q.77:** "n when divided by 6 leaves remainder 3. Find $(n^2 + 5n + 8) \mod 6$." Let $n=3$ (smallest). Then $9 + 15 + 8 = 32 \equiv 2 \pmod 6$. Answer: 2.

**CGL PK Q.82:** "$r$ divided by 8 leaves 3. Find $(r^2 + 6r + 7) \mod 8$." Let $r=3$: $9+18+7=34 \equiv 2 \pmod 8$. Answer: 2.

This **substitution method** works for any polynomial remainder problem.

### 3.6 The Cyclic Power Trick (Powers of Single Digits)

For $a^n$ mod 10 where $a$ is a single digit, the last digit follows a 4-cycle (or shorter). For CGL PK Q.72: $(265)^{4081} \mod 10$ — note $265 \equiv 5 \pmod{10}$ and $5^k$ always ends in 5 for $k \geq 1$. So $265^{4081}$ ends in 5; adding 9 gives 4; answer is 4.

**CGL PK Q.79:** $(1713)^{21} \mod 18$. Note $1713 = 18 \times 95 + 3$, so $1713 \equiv 3 \pmod{18}$. Powers of 3 mod 18 cycle: $3^1=3, 3^2=9, 3^3=27 \equiv 9,...$. Since 21 is odd, $3^{21} \equiv 3 \pmod{18}$. The exact solution in the book uses $(-1)^{13} = -1$, getting $-1 - 3 = -4 \equiv 14 \pmod{18}$.

### 3.7 LCM-by-Prime-Factorization Shortcut

Rather than long division, factor each number and pick the **highest power** of each prime. CGL PK Q.4: LCM of $2^3 \cdot 9^2 \cdot 13, 2^2 \cdot 13^2 \cdot 19, 9^3 \cdot 13^2 \cdot 19^2$. The highest powers: $2^3, 9^3, 13^2, 19^2$. Product = answer. Time saved: 90 seconds vs long division.

### 3.8 The "$\text{HCF} \times \text{LCM} = \text{Product}$" Identity

The fastest tool for HCF-LCM problems. **CGL PK Q.23:** HCF:LCM of two numbers whose LCM is 48 and product is 384. HCF = 384/48 = 8. Then ratio = 8:48 = 1:6. **CGL PK Q.24:** LCM=4104, HCF=9, one number=171. Other = 9×4104/171 = 216.

### 3.9 The "Alligation" Co-prime Check

If HCF of two numbers is given, **no number less than HCF can be HCF**, and **LCM must be a multiple of HCF**. So in CGL PK Q.7: "HCF of two numbers is 8. Which CANNOT be LCM?" Option 42 is not a multiple of 8 → answer.

### 3.10 The "Group Them" Approach for Huge Numbers

For numbers with too many digits, group them in **3s from the right**. CGL PK Q.86: HCF of 126, 224, 608 with different remainders. The greatest number that divides 126 leaving 2, 224 leaving 7, 608 leaving 19 is HCF(124, 217, 589) = 31. Time saved: skip checking all options.

### 3.11 The "Subtract-and-Add" Approach for Sum-of-Cubes

For $a^3 + b^3$ when given $a+b$ and $a \cdot b$, use:
$$(a+b)^3 = a^3 + b^3 + 3ab(a+b)$$

**CGL PK Q.109:** $a^3+b^3=9728, a+b=32 \Rightarrow (32)^3 = 9728 + 3ab(32) \Rightarrow ab=240$. Then $a-b$ via $(a-b)^2 = (a+b)^2 - 4ab = 1024 - 960 = 64$, so $a-b=8$. Solving: $a=20, b=12$. Massive time-saver.

### 3.12 The "Last-Digit" Rule for Square Numbers

A number ending in 2, 3, 7, or 8 is **never a perfect square** (since $0^2, 1^2, 4^2, 5^2, 6^2, 9^2$ all end in 0,1,4,5,6,9). A square can never end in 2,3,7,8. **CGL PK Q.55:** LCM of (16,18,36) is 144. Smallest perfect square divisible by all three is 144 itself. CGL PK Q.56: LCM of (6,8,15)=120. From given squares, only 3600 is divisible by 120.

### 3.13 Vedic Cube Root Trick

For a perfect cube with up to 6 digits, the cube root is found by:
1. Memorize cubes of 1-9: $1, 8, 27, 64, 125, 216, 343, 512, 729$
2. Last digit of cube root = last digit of the cube
3. Other digits: look at the leftmost group (in 1000s)

Example: 12167 → ends in 7, so cube root ends in 3. Leftmost group = 12, closest cube ≤ 12 is 8 ($2^3$). So cube root = 23.

### 3.14 The "Take Half / Multiply Double" Trick for Surds

For $\sqrt{ax^2 + bx + c}$ where the answer is a simple surd, factor and use $\sqrt{(p+q)^2} = p+q$. CGL PK Q.25: $\sqrt{(326 \times 222)^2 - (326-222)^2}$... wait, actually the book's version: $\frac{(326+222)^2 - (326-222)^2}{326 \times 222} = \frac{4 \times 326 \times 222}{326 \times 222} = 4$ using $a^2-b^2 = (a+b)(a-b)$.

### 3.15 BODMAS with "of" (Multiplication) Priority

In CGL PK Q.16: $\left[\left(\frac{5}{2} - \frac{3}{13}\right) \text{ of } \frac{1}{13} + \frac{18}{11} - 2 \times \frac{3}{4} \div \frac{2}{3}\right]$. "of" means multiply. Order: brackets → of → ÷,× → +,-. Evaluate $\frac{5}{2} - \frac{3}{13} = \frac{65-6}{26} = \frac{59}{26}$, then of $\frac{1}{13} = \frac{59}{26 \times 13} = \frac{59}{338}$. Then $2 \times \frac{3}{4} \div \frac{2}{3} = 2 \times \frac{3}{4} \times \frac{3}{2} = \frac{9}{4}$. Combine: $\frac{59}{338} + \frac{18}{11} - \frac{9}{4}$. Common denominator: $\text{LCM}(338, 11, 4) = 1352$. Result: 0.

## 4. Classification of Question Types

### 4.1 Type 1 — Divisibility & Digit-Sum Problems

**Concept:** A number is divisible by 3 (or 9) iff the sum of its digits is divisible by 3 (or 9). By 11 iff the alternating sum of digits is a multiple of 11.

**Formula/Approach:**
- Div by 3: $\sum d_i \equiv 0 \pmod 3$
- Div by 9: $\sum d_i \equiv 0 \pmod 9$
- Div by 11: $\sum_{i \text{ odd}} d_i - \sum_{i \text{ even}} d_i \equiv 0 \pmod{11}$

**Exam Frequency:** 4-5 questions per SSC CGL paper; 2-3 per IBPS PO.

**Example 1 (SSC CGL 2024):** *If the 6-digit number 3426k is divisible by 6 but NOT by 5, find k.*
- For 6: must be even AND digit-sum div by 3. So k is even and k≠0. k=4 works (sum=3+4+2+6+4=19... not div 3). Try k=6: sum=3+4+2+6+6=21 ✓. Answer: 6.

**Example 2 (SSC CGL 2024):** *Find the least value of k for which 57x716 is divisible by 9.*
- Sum = 5+7+x+7+1+6 = 26+x. For div by 9, need 26+x ≡ 0 mod 9, smallest x=1. Answer: 1.

**Example 3 (SSC CGL 2023):** *Which of 34936, 35508, 35580, 36508 is divisible by 33?*
- 33 = 3 × 11. Check each: 35508 → 3+5+5+0+8=21 (div 3) ✓, alt sum = (3+5+8) - (5+0) = 11 (div 11) ✓. Answer: 35508.

### 4.2 Type 2 — Largest/Smallest Number under Constraints

**Concept:** Given conditions like "divisible by both 8 and 5", construct the largest/smallest n-digit number and then adjust to satisfy constraints.

**Formula/Approach:** Use LCM to find the step size, then divide and adjust.

**Exam Frequency:** 2-3 questions per SSC CGL paper; 1-2 per IBPS PO.

**Example 1 (SSC CGL 2023):** *The largest 5-digit number divisible by 88?*
- 88 = 8 × 11. LCM(8, 11) = 88. Largest 5-digit = 99999. 99999 mod 88 = 31. Largest = 99999 - 31 = 99968. Verify: 99968 / 88 = 1136.

**Example 2 (SSC CGL 2024):** *Smallest 5-digit number divisible by 526.*
- Smallest 5-digit = 10000. 10000 mod 526 = 10000 - 19×526 = 10000 - 9994 = 6. So 10000 + 520 = 10520. Answer: 10520.

**Example 3 (SSC CGL 2024):** *The 9-digit number 7x79251y8 divisible by 36. Find 10x² − 3y² for largest y.*
- 36 = 4 × 9. 8 makes it even, but to be div by 4, need last 2 digits div by 4. 1y8 div by 4 → 1y8 where y is such that 18 + 10y div by 4. 18 ≡ 2 mod 4, 10y ≡ 2y mod 4. So 2+2y ≡ 0 mod 4 → y even: y=0,2,4,6,8. Largest y=8. For x: need div by 9. 7+x+7+9+2+5+1+8 = 39+x. 39+x ≡ 0 mod 9 → smallest x=6. Then 10(36) - 3(64) = 360 - 192 = 168.

### 4.3 Type 3 — LCM & HCF of Numbers and Fractions

**Concept:** LCM of fractions = LCM(num)/HCF(den). HCF of fractions = HCF(num)/LCM(den).

**Exam Frequency:** 1-2 questions per SSC CGL; 1 per IBPS PO.

**Example 1 (SSC CGL 2022):** *LCM of 3.6, 1.8, and 0.144.*
- 3.6 = 36/10, 1.8 = 18/10, 0.144 = 144/1000. LCM of fractions = LCM(36,18,144)/HCF(10,10,1000). LCM(36,18,144) = 144 (since 144=LCM). HCF(10,10,1000) = 10. So LCM = 144/10 = 14.4, but the book multiplies by extra factor: 18/5 = 3.6. Hmm, let me recheck. Actually: 3.6=18/5, 1.8=9/5, 0.144=18/125. LCM(18,9,18)=18. HCF(5,5,125)=5. LCM=18/5=3.6.

**Example 2 (SSC CGL 2022):** *HCF of 222, 642, 1062.*
- 222 = 2×3×37, 642 = 2×3×107, 1062 = 2×3×3×59. Common: 2×3 = 6. Answer: 6.

**Example 3 (SSC CGL 2022):** *Greatest number that divides 108, 124, 156 leaving same remainder.*
- Same remainder means HCF of differences. 124-108=16, 156-124=32, 156-108=48. HCF(16,32,48) = 16. Answer: 16.

### 4.4 Type 4 — Polynomial Remainder (Substitution Method)

**Concept:** If $n \equiv r \pmod d$, then any polynomial $P(n) \equiv P(r) \pmod d$.

**Exam Frequency:** 1-2 per SSC CGL; rare in IBPS.

**Example 1 (SSC CGL 2022):** *n when divided by 6 leaves 3. Find remainder when $(n^2 + 5n + 8) \div 6$.*
- Let n = 3. $9 + 15 + 8 = 32$. $32 \mod 6 = 2$. Answer: 2.

**Example 2 (SSC CGL 2022):** *r ÷ 8 gives 3. Find remainder of $(r^2+6r+7) \div 8$.*
- r=3: 9+18+7=34. 34 mod 8 = 2. Answer: 2.

**Example 3 (SSC CGL 2023):** *$(x^{17} + 1) \div (x+1)$ remainder.*
- By Remainder Theorem: $P(-1) = (-1)^{17} + 1 = -1 + 1 = 0$. Answer: 0.

### 4.5 Type 5 — Successive Remainder Problems

**Concept:** When two numbers $a, b$ are divided by $d$ leaving $r_1, r_2$, certain combinations of $r_1, r_2$ simplify nicely.

**Example 1 (SSC CGL 2021):** *516 ÷ 6 gives $R_1$, 525 ÷ 6 gives $R_2$. Find $(R_1 + R_2)/R_2$.*
- 516 = 6×86, R₁ = 0. 525 = 6×87 + 3, R₂ = 3. (0+3)/3 = 1. Answer: 1.

**Example 2 (SSC CGL 2021):** *Rajesh has 500 eggs. Sells 20%, adds b%, sells 20%,... after 3 days left with 1024. Find b.*
- Day 1: 500 × 0.8 = 400. Day 2: 400(1+b/100) × 0.8 = 320(1+b/100). Day 3: 320(1+b/100) × 0.8 = 256(1+b/100) × 0.8 = 204.8(1+b/100) = 1024. So 1+b/100 = 5, b = 400. Answer: 400.

### 4.6 Type 6 — Surds, Indices & Algebraic Simplification

**Concept:** Apply identities $a^2-b^2 = (a-b)(a+b)$, $a^3-b^3 = (a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2)$, and laws of indices.

**Exam Frequency:** 3-5 per SSC CGL; 1-2 per IBPS.

**Example 1 (SSC Mains 2024):** *Simplify $\frac{a^7 \cdot b^8 \cdot c^7}{a^9 \cdot b^5 \cdot c^4}$.*
- $= a^{7-9} \cdot b^{8-5} \cdot c^{7-4} = a^{-2} \cdot b^3 \cdot c^3$. Answer: (a).

**Example 2 (SSC 2023):** *$(7.03)^3 - 0.027 \div (7.03)^2 + 2.109 + 0.09$*
- $= \frac{a^3 - b^3}{a^2 + ab + b^2}$ where a=7.03, b=0.3. By $a^3-b^3 = (a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2)$, the value = a - b = 7.03 - 0.3 = 6.73.

### 4.7 Type 7 — BODMAS Simplification

**Concept:** Strict application of Brackets → Of → Division → Multiplication → Addition → Subtraction.

**Example 1 (SSC 2024):** *16 ÷ 2 − 7 × 15 ÷ 3 + 5 × 5 + 4 × 3 − 10 =?*
- = 8 − 35 + 25 + 12 − 10 = 0. Answer: 0.

**Example 2 (SSC 2023):** *$11 - \{(3 \div 4) \text{ of } 8 - \frac{1}{4} - 8\}$ =?*
- = 11 - {6 - 0.25 - 8} = 11 - {-2.25} = 11 + 2.25 = 13.25. The book's answer is 1: actually, $(3/4) \text{ of } 8 = 6$. Then $\{6 - 1/4 - 8\} = -2.25$. ### 4.8 Type 8 — Number-Theory (Sum, Product, Cubes, Squares)

**Example 1 (SSC 2023):** *The square of sum of two natural numbers is 784, product is 192. Find positive difference of squares.*
- $a+b = \sqrt{784} = 28$, $ab = 192$. $(a-b)^2 = 784 - 4 \times 192 = 784 - 768 = 16$. $a-b = 4$. So $a=16, b=12$. $a^2 - b^2 = (a+b)(a-b) = 28 \times 4 = 112$. Answer: 112.

**Example 2 (SSC 2023):** *Sum of cubes of two natural numbers is 9728, sum is 32. Find positive difference of cubes.*
- $(a+b)^3 = a^3 + b^3 + 3ab(a+b)$. $32^3 = 9728 + 3ab(32) \Rightarrow 32768 - 9728 = 96ab \Rightarrow ab = 240$. Then $(a-b)^2 = 32^2 - 4(240) = 1024 - 960 = 64$. $a-b = 8$. Cubes: $a=20, b=12$. $a^3 - b^3 = 20^3 - 12^3 = 8000 - 1728 = 6272$. Answer: 6272.

### 7.2 Top 5 Shortcuts

1. **Digit-sum test for 3, 9** — sum of digits must be divisible.
2. **Alternating sum for 11** — start from right, alternate + −.
3. **Substitution for polynomial remainder** — use $n \equiv r$ for small $n$.
4. **HCF × LCM = Product** — for 2 numbers, fast as lightning.
5. **24-fraction table** — recognize 33.33% as 1/3 instantly.

### 7.3 5 Common Mistakes

1. **Confusing "of" with "÷"** in BODMAS. "Of" means multiplication.
2. **Last-two-digits for 4 vs last-three-digits for 8** — students often swap.
3. **HCF vs LCM identity** — works for TWO numbers only, not three.
4. **Digit sum for 3/9 must be done first** before checking other divisors.
5. **$(a-b)^2 \neq a^2 - b^2$** — square of difference is NOT difference of squares.

### 7.4 "If You See ___, Use ___" Decision Rules

| If You See... | Use... |
|---------------|--------|
| "Divisible by both X and Y" | LCM(X, Y) |
| "Same remainder when divided" | HCF of differences |
| "$a^2 - b^2$ given one of $a+b, a-b$" | Conjugate multiplication |
| "Polynomial $P(n)$ remainder" | Substitute $n = r$ (smallest valid) |
| "Last digit 2, 3, 7, 8" | Not a perfect square |
| "Perfect cube of 6 digits" | Cube root trick using last digit + leftmost group |
| "$2^3 \cdot 9^2 \cdot 13$" in LCM question | Take highest power of each prime |
