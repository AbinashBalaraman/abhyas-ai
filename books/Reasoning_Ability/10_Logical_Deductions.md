## 1. Conceptual Foundation

### 1.1 What is Logical Deduction?

**Logical deduction** is the process of **drawing conclusions from given premises** using valid logical rules. In competitive exams, this is tested through 5 specific question types.

### 1.2 The Five Major Question Types

1. **Statement & Assumption**: What is taken for granted?
2. **Statement & Conclusion**: What logically follows?
3. **Statement & Course of Action**: What should be done?
4. **Cause & Effect**: What caused what?
5. **Assertion-Reason**: Assertion (A) and Reason (R) relationship.

### 1.3 The Two-Valued Logic

Each statement is evaluated as:
- **True/Valid/Logical** — supported by premise
- **False/Invalid/Illogical** — not supported

There is no "maybe" in standard SSC/banking logical deduction.

## 2. Statement & Assumption

### 2.1 Definition

An **assumption** is something **taken for granted or supposed to be true** without which the statement wouldn't make sense.

### 2.2 How to Identify Assumptions

**Strategy:**
1. Read the statement.
2. Ask: "What must be true for this statement to make sense?"
3. The answer is the assumption.

**Rules for valid assumption:**
- It must be **implicit** (not stated explicitly).
- It must be **necessary** (without it, statement fails).
- It must be **reasonable** (not absurd or extreme).

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS — standard reasoning pedagogy]

### 2.3 Example

**Statement:** "The municipal corporation has issued a notice asking residents to use covered dustbins."

**Assumption (valid):** Residents do not use covered dustbins currently.
**Assumption (invalid):** All residents have dustbins.

**Solution:** Valid assumption = "Residents do not use covered dustbins currently." The notice implies a problem exists.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS — typical banking example]

### 2.4 Common Assumption Patterns

| Statement Type | Implicit Assumption |
|----------------|---------------------|
| "X has been done to solve Y" | Y is a problem |
| "Please use the back door" | Front door is unavailable/crowded |
| "Don't smoke here" | People may smoke here |
| "Sale: 50% off" | Customers want discounts |
| "New traffic rule" | Old rule is insufficient |

## 3. Statement & Conclusion

### 3.1 Definition

A **conclusion** is a **statement that logically follows** from the given statement(s). The student must determine which conclusions are valid.

### 3.2 Types of Conclusions

**Definite Conclusion:** Follows with 100% certainty.
**Probable Conclusion:** Follows with reasonable probability but not certainty.

In SSC/banking, **only definite conclusions** are typically asked.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

### 3.3 How to Identify Valid Conclusions

**Strategy:**
1. Read the statement.
2. For each conclusion, ask: "Does this logically follow?"
3. If yes → valid. If no or uncertain → invalid.

**Rules:**
- Must be **logically derived** from the statement.
- Cannot introduce **new information** not in the statement.
- Cannot **contradict** the statement.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

### 3.4 Example

**Statement:** "The company has decided to increase the price of all products by 10%."

**Conclusion 1 (valid):** The price of products will be higher than before.
**Conclusion 2 (invalid):** The quality of products will improve.
**Conclusion 3 (valid):** Customers will have to pay more.

**Solution:** Conclusions 1 and 3 follow directly. Conclusion 2 is not stated/implied.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

### 3.5 Multiple Statements and Conclusions

**Statement 1:** "All cats are animals."
**Statement 2:** "All animals are living beings."

**Conclusion 1 (valid):** All cats are living beings. (transitive)
**Conclusion 2 (invalid):** Some living beings are cats. (not necessarily, but could be)
**Conclusion 3 (invalid):** All animals are cats. (reverse not valid)

## 4. Statement & Course of Action

### 4.1 Definition

A **course of action** is a **practical step that should be taken** in response to the problem/situation described in the statement.

### 4.2 How to Identify Valid Course of Action

**Rules for valid course of action:**
- Must **address the problem** described.
- Must be **practical and feasible**.
- Must be **logical** (not extreme/illegal/immoral).
- Should be **immediate** (action-oriented).

**Avoid courses of action that:**
- Don't solve the problem.
- Are too drastic (e.g., "shut down the company" for a minor issue).
- Are illegal or unethical.
- Are vague or non-actionable.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

### 4.3 Example

**Statement:** "The city has been facing water shortage for the past month due to inadequate rainfall."

**Course of Action 1 (valid):** The municipality should implement water rationing.
**Course of Action 2 (valid):** The government should explore alternative water sources.
**Course of Action 3 (invalid):** People should stop drinking water.
**Course of Action 4 (invalid):** The city should be relocated.

**Solution:** Actions 1 and 2 are practical. Actions 3 and 4 are extreme/impractical.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

### 4.4 Common Action Patterns

| Problem Type | Typical Action |
|--------------|----------------|
| Traffic congestion | Improve public transport, signals |
| Pollution | Implement emission controls, plant trees |
| Crime | Increase police, stricter laws |
| Education quality | Teacher training, better resources |
| Health issues | Awareness campaigns, more hospitals |

## 5. Cause & Effect

### 5.1 Definition

In **Cause & Effect** questions, two statements are given. The student must determine which is the **cause** and which is the **effect**.

### 5.2 How to Identify Cause and Effect

**Rules:**
- The **cause** happens FIRST (in time).
- The **effect** is the RESULT of the cause.
- The cause and effect must be **directly related**.
- Independent events (no causal link) = both invalid.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

### 5.3 Example

**Statement 1:** "The price of onions has increased by 50% in the last month."
**Statement 2:** "The government has banned onion exports."

**Cause & Effect Analysis:**
- Banning exports → reduces supply → increases price. So **Statement 2 is the cause**, **Statement 1 is the effect**.

**Answer:** Statement 2 is the cause; Statement 1 is the effect.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

### 5.4 Independent Events

**Statement 1:** "The cricket match was won by India."
**Statement 2:** "The stock market went up today."

**Analysis:** No causal relationship. Both are independent events. So **neither is cause nor effect** of the other.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

### 5.5 Common Cause-Effect Patterns

| Cause | Effect |
|-------|--------|
| Heavy rain | Floods, traffic jams |
| Drought | Crop failure, water shortage |
| New technology | Job losses, efficiency |
| Price hike | Reduced demand, inflation |
| Pandemic | Lockdowns, remote work |

## 6. Assertion-Reason

### 6.1 Definition

**Assertion (A):** A statement of fact or opinion.
**Reason (R):** A statement that explains/supports/justifies the assertion.

### 6.2 The Standard 4-Option Format

**Options:**
- (a) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
- (b) Both A and R are true, but R is NOT the correct explanation of A.
- (c) A is true, but R is false.
- (d) A is false, but R is true.
- (e) Both A and R are false.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS — standard Assertion-Reason format]

### 6.3 Solving Strategy

**Step 1:** Evaluate Assertion (A) — true or false?
**Step 2:** Evaluate Reason (R) — true or false?
**Step 3:** If both true, check if R explains A.
**Step 4:** Match to option.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

### 6.4 Examples

**Example 1:**
- **A:** "India is a developing country."
- **R:** "India has a large population and growing economy."

**Analysis:** Both A and R are true. R explains A. **Answer: (a).**

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

**Example 2:**
- **A:** "Smoking is harmful to health."
- **R:** "Many people enjoy smoking."

**Analysis:** A is true. R is true (some people do). But R doesn't explain A. **Answer: (b).**

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

**Example 3:**
- **A:** "All swans are white."
- **R:** "Black swans exist in Australia."

**Analysis:** A is false. R is true. **Answer: (d).**

## 7. Speed-Solving Shortcuts

### 7.1 The "Necessity Test" for Assumptions

For an assumption to be valid, **the statement must fail without it**. If you remove the assumption and the statement still works, the assumption is invalid.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

### 7.2 The "Conclusion vs. New Info" Test

A valid conclusion **cannot introduce new information** not in the statement. If the conclusion contains facts not implied by the statement, it's invalid.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

### 7.3 The "Practicality Test" for Courses of Action

A valid course of action must be **practical and feasible**. Extreme or vague actions are usually invalid.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

### 7.4 The "Time Order" Test for Cause & Effect

The cause must come **before** the effect in time. If Statement 1 happens after Statement 2, Statement 2 is the cause.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

### 7.5 The "Causal Link" Test

For a true cause-effect pair, there must be a **direct logical link**. Coincidental or unrelated events = both invalid.

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

### 7.6 The "R explains A" Test for Assertion-Reason

R is the "correct explanation" only if it **logically justifies A**. If R is true but doesn't explain A, it's option (b), not (a).

[NOT IN EXTRACTED BOOKS]

## 8. Classification of Question Types

### 8.1 Type 1 — Identify Valid Assumption

**Example:** Statement: "Wear helmets while riding." Find the assumption.
**Solution:** Riders don't always wear helmets currently. *Time: 30 sec.*

### 8.2 Type 2 — Identify Valid Conclusion

**Example:** Statement: "The bridge is closed for repairs." Find valid conclusion.
**Solution:** Traffic will be rerouted. *Time: 30 sec.*

### 8.3 Type 3 — Identify Course of Action

**Example:** Statement: "Crime rate has increased." Find valid action.
**Solution:** Increase police patrolling. *Time: 30 sec.*

### 8.4 Type 4 — Identify Cause & Effect

**Example:** Two statements; find cause and effect.
**Solution:** Apply time-order and causal-link tests. *Time: 45 sec.*

### 8.5 Type 5 — Assertion-Reason

**Example:** Evaluate A and R; choose option.
**Solution:** 3-step evaluation. *Time: 60 sec.*

### 8.6 Type 6 — Multiple Statement Syllogism (Mixed with Conclusion)

**Example:** Given 2-3 statements, find valid conclusions.
**Solution:** Apply syllogistic rules. *Time: 60-90 sec.*

### 8.7 Type 7 — Critical Reasoning (Inference)

**Example:** From a passage, what can be inferred?
**Solution:** Find the most reasonable inference. *Time: 45 sec.*

## 11. Chapter Summary & Revision Card

### 11.1 Compact Decision Table

| Question Type | Key Test | Valid If... |
|---------------|----------|-------------|
| Assumption | Necessity | Without it, statement fails |
| Conclusion | Logical follow | Derived from statement, no new info |
| Course of Action | Practicality | Addresses problem, feasible |
| Cause | Time order | Cause before effect |
| Effect | Causal link | Direct result of cause |
| Assertion | Truth value | Verifiable |
| Reason | Explanation | Logically justifies assertion |

### 11.2 Top 5 Shortcuts

1. **Necessity test** for assumptions — without it, statement fails.
2. **New info test** for conclusions — if it adds facts, it's invalid.
3. **Practicality test** for actions — extreme/vague = invalid.
4. **Time order** for cause-effect — cause precedes effect.
5. **R explains A** test — R must justify A specifically.

### 11.3 5 Common Mistakes

1. **Marking valid assumptions as invalid** (or vice versa) by misreading necessity.
2. **Adding new information in conclusions** — not allowed.
3. **Choosing extreme actions** like "ban everything" — usually invalid.
4. **Confusing correlation with causation** in cause-effect.
5. **In Assertion-Reason, marking (a) when R is true but doesn't explain A**.

### 11.4 "If You See ___, Use ___" Decision Rules

| If You See... | Use... |
|---------------|--------|
| "What is assumed?" | Necessity test |
| "What follows?" | Logical follow test |
| "What should be done?" | Practicality + addresses problem |
| "Cause and effect?" | Time order + causal link |
| "Assertion A and Reason R" | 3-step evaluation |
| "Multiple statements" | Syllogistic reasoning |
| "Most likely cause" | Identify direct cause |
