# Chapter 1: Analogy, Classification & Series

> **Chapter Overview:** Analogy (Word / Letter / Number), Classification (Odd-One-Out), and Series Completion form the **highest-weightage Reasoning topic in SSC CGL Tier-I** (typically 5-7 questions per shift), with **3-5 questions in SSC CHSL, 2-4 in SSC CPO, and 3-5 in IBPS PO Prelims/Mains**. This chapter unifies the three closely-related "pattern-completion" reasoning families: Analogy tests your ability to spot a parallel relationship, Classification asks which option does not share the common property, and Series asks what number/letter/word continues the established pattern. Together they account for **~25-30% of SSC reasoning marks**.
>
> **Comprehensive exam-aligned study material covering standard syllabi for SSC, RRB, Banking, and State PSC examinations.

## 1. Conceptual Foundation

### 1.1 What is Analogy?

**Analogy** means **parallelism**. In an analogy question, a **particular relationship** is given and a **similar relationship** must be identified from the alternatives. Analogy tests a candidate's overall knowledge, power of reasoning, and ability to think concisely and accurately.

The standard analogy form is:

$$A: B:: C: D$$

Read as: "A is to B as C is to D" — the relationship between A and B is parallel to the relationship between C and D.

### 1.4 Competitive Exam Question Bank's 12-Type Analogy Breakdown

Competitive Exam Question Bank Reasoning SSC breaks Analogy into 12 finer sub-types:

- **Type 1-5:** Meaningful words / Similar words / Related words (factual knowledge)
- **Type 6-9:** Letter-cluster analogies (2-letter, 3-letter, word)
- **Type 10-12:** Number pair / Related numbers / Number sets

This finer breakdown is Competitive Exam Question Bank's signature teaching device — it lets students target each relationship family separately.

### 1.5 Classification (Odd-One-Out)

**Classification** asks you to identify the item that **does not belong** to the common group. The common group shares one property (category, function, rule); the odd one violates it.

**Three sub-types of Classification:**

| Sub-Type | Form | Example |
|----------|------|---------|
| **Word Classification** | Pick the odd word | Tiger, Lion, Leopard, Wolf → Wolf (not a big cat) |
| **Number Classification** | Pick the odd number | 4, 9, 16, 23 → 23 (others are perfect squares) |
| **Letter Classification** | Pick the odd letter-pair | AZ, BY, CX, DW → DW (others follow opposite-letter rule) |

### 1.6 Series Completion

**Series Completion** gives a sequence of numbers, letters, or alpha-numeric combinations and asks you to find the missing term or the next term.

Standard ReferenceCh.3 (Number Series, 11 pages) lists the **6 standard patterns**:

| Pattern | Form | Example |
|---------|------|---------|
| **Single series (arithmetic)** | a, a+d, a+2d... | 2, 5, 8, 11,? → +3 pattern |
| **Double series (alternating)** | odd positions one pattern, even another | 3, 5, 8, 12, 14, 17, 20,? |
| **Triple series (3 interleaved)** | three arithmetic sequences | 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9,? |
| **Difference-of-difference** | second differences form pattern | 1, 4, 11, 22, 37,? |
| **Square / Cube series** | $(n)^2 \pm k$ or $(n)^3 \pm k$ | 4, 9, 16, 25, 36 → $n^2$ |
| **Mathematical-operation series** | alternate operations | ×2+1, ×2+1... |

### 1.7 The Opposite-Letter Rule (Alphabet Analogy)

A signature shortcut taught in Standard ReferenceCh.1:

> **Rule:** Opposite pairs sum to 27. A↔Z (1+26), B↔Y (2+25), C↔X (3+24), D↔W (4+23)...

**Example:** AZ: BY:: CX:? → the next opposite pair after CX is DW. Answer: **DW**.

### 1.8 Reverse Letter Rule +13 (Alphabet Coding Foundation)

Most alphabet-coding uses **+13** (or -13) shift because ROT13 is the universal cipher. Standard Referenceshows examples in the solution block:

```
FLMO → BZYS
F+6=B (mod 26 wrap), L+14=Z, M-13=B... wait...
Standard: F-6=B, L-14=Z, M-13=B (off by one in OCR), O-2=M
ROT13: F→S, L→Y, M→Z, O→B (but book writes "BZYS")
```

The principle is **fixed numerical shift** in either direction. The student should treat each alphabet pair as a numeric shift puzzle and test the shift on all letters.

## 2. Step-by-Step Solving Algorithm

### 2.1 For Analogy (Word / Number / Letter)

```
Step 1: Identify the relationship between A and B.
        - Factual (country-capital, instrument-unit, animal-youngone)?
        - Functional (tool-action, worker-tool)?
        - Structural (part-whole, member-group)?
        - Opposite or synonym?

Step 2: Determine the relationship direction.
        - Is A "containing" B or B "containing" A?
        - Example: "Cat: Kitten" = "Cat produces Kitten"; don't reverse.

Step 3: Apply same relationship to C.
        - Find D such that C: D matches the A: B relation.

Step 4: Eliminate options that mismatch the direction.
        - A common trap: option offers "C is to D" where D is the
          reverse of what's needed (e.g., asks "Sound of Dog" instead
          of "Young one of Dog").

Step 5: Verify by substitution. Plug D back: does C: D feel parallel?
```

### 2.2 For Classification (Odd-One-Out)

```
Step 1: List all options.
Step 2: Find the COMMON property shared by 3 of the 4 options.
Step 3: The 4th is the answer.
Step 4: If multiple properties are shared, look for the most
        specific (e.g., "are mammals" is more specific than "are
        animals").
Step 5: Common trap — two properties are common; pick the
        more specific / higher-order property.
```

### 2.3 For Series Completion

```
Step 1: Calculate first differences (term[n] - term[n-1]).
Step 2: If first differences form a constant → arithmetic series.
Step 3: If first differences form a pattern → check second
        differences (difference-of-difference).
Step 4: For alternating series, separate odd-indexed and
        even-indexed terms into two series; solve each.
Step 5: Check for square/cube patterns: term ≈ n² ± k.
Step 6: Check for ×N + k or ×N - k patterns.
```

### 2.4 The +6/+7/+13 Reverse Alphabet Trick

For alphabet-coding and analogy problems, the master rule is:

| Shift | Mapping | When used |
|-------|---------|-----------|
| **+13 (ROT13)** | A↔N, B↔O, C↔P... | Universal cipher |
| **+2** | ACE, BDF, CEG... | Small-shift word ladder |
| **Opposite (+26 wrap)** | A↔Z, B↔Y... | Opposite-letter rule |

For each coded pair, calculate the shift on the first letter, then apply the same shift to the second letter. If it matches an option, that's the answer.

## 3. Trap Detection & Error Prevention Guide

### 3.1 Top 12 Analogy Traps

1. **Reversed direction.** "Dog: Bark" is the *sound* of dog, NOT the *animal that barks*. A common trap option offers "Cat: Mew" (correct) vs. "Wing: Bird" (wrong direction).

2. **Same category, wrong specific.** Among "Tiger, Lion, Leopard, Wolf" the odd one is Wolf (canine, not feline). Trap: students pick "Lion" thinking tigers are unique.

3. **Same shape, different function.** "Triangle, Square, Pentagon, Circle" → all shapes, but if one option is "Cube" (3D), that's the odd one. Trap: students ignore dimensionality.

4. **Capital of a state vs. capital of a country.** "Lucknow" is the capital of Uttar Pradesh (state), not India (country). Trap: mixing state/country.

5. **Currency confusion.** "$" is the symbol for US Dollar (USD), but Canada uses CAD (Canadian Dollar), Australia uses AUD (Australian Dollar). Trap: assuming "$" means USD only.

6. **Multiple correct relationships.** In a classification set, two properties might be shared by 3-of-4. Pick the **most specific** shared property.

7. **Square vs. Rectangle.** All squares are rectangles, but not all rectangles are squares. If asked "which is most specific?" — pick square over rectangle.

8. **Young-one vs. adult.** "Hen: Chick" is correct. Trap: option offers "Hen: Chicken" (which is the species name, not the young-one).

9. **National-game confusion.** Cricket is the national game of India (de facto), but Hockey was the national sport of India (de jure until 2012). Both have been answers in different exam years.

10. **Alphabet +13 vs. +14.** A shift of +13 maps A→N, B→O. A shift of +14 maps A→O, B→P. **Compute the shift on the first letter before applying to the second.**

11. **Series: arithmetic vs. geometric.** $2, 4, 8, 16$ is geometric ($\times 2$). $2, 4, 6, 8$ is arithmetic (+2). Same first difference set can be misleading.

12. **Series: difference-of-difference.** $1, 4, 11, 22, 37$ — first differences $3, 7, 11, 15$; second differences $4, 4, 4$ (constant). The series is quadratic: $a_n = 2n^2 - n - 1$? Test: $n=1: 0$? Let me recompute: $a_1=1, a_2=4, a_3=11, a_4=22, a_5=37$. First diffs: $3, 7, 11, 15$ (increment +4). So $a_6 = 37 + 19 = 56$. Trap: assuming linear difference.

### 3.2 Sample trap sentence + correct interpretation

> **Trap sentence:** "Coal: Heat:: Wax:?"
> **Correct interpretation:** Coal produces heat (combustion). Wax (in a candle) produces light. Answer is **Light** (or Candle, depending on interpretation).
>
> **Solution from Standard ReferenceCh.1, p. 8:** Answer is (c) Light.
> **Logic:** "Coal is burnt to produce heat; similarly wax (in a candle) is burnt to produce light." This is a **functional** analogy (source: produced result), not a **structural** analogy.

## 4. Type-Wise Question Bank with Solutions

### Type 1: Factual Word Analogy (SSC Standard)

**Logic:** Real-world relationships between countries, capitals, currencies, animals, instruments.
**Difficulty:** Easy-Medium
**Exam Source:** SSC CGL Tier-I, SSC CHSL, SSC MTS, RRB NTPC

**Solved Example 1 (SSC CGL 2017):**
> "Fox: Cunning:: Rabbit:?"
> (a) Courageous (b) Dangerous (c) Timid (d) Ferocious
>
> **Solution:** "Animal behaviour" relationship. Fox is characterized by cunningness; Rabbit is characterized by timidity.
> **Answer: (c) Timid**

**Solved Example 2 (SSC CGL 2017):**
> "Train: Passenger:: Aeroplane:?"
> (a) Pilot (b) Aeronaut (c) Driver (d) Traveller
>
> **Solution:** "Mode-of-travel → Traveller" relationship. A passenger travels by train; an aeronaut travels by aeroplane.
> **Answer: (b) Aeronaut**

**Solved Example 3 (SSC CGL 2017):**
> "Coal: Heat:: Wax:?"
> (a) Energy (b) Candle (c) Light (d) Bee
>
> **Solution:** Combustion product. Coal → Heat. Wax (candle) → Light.
> **Answer: (c) Light**

### Type 2: Number Analogy (SSC Standard)

**Logic:** Mathematical operation between number pairs.
**Difficulty:** Easy-Medium
**Exam Source:** SSC CGL Tier-I, SSC CHSL, IBPS PO

**Solved Example 4:**
> "Given set (6, 14, 30) — find the analogous set."
> (a) 4, 16, 28 (b) 7, 12, 22 (c) 6, 12, 22 (d) 5, 12, 20
>
> **Solution:** Pattern is ×2 + 2: 6×2+2 = 14; 14×2+2 = 30.
> Apply to options:
> (a) 4×2+2 = 10, not 16. ✗
> (b) 7×2+2 = 16, not 12. ✗
> (c) 6×2+2 = 14, not 12. ✗
> (d) 5×2+2 = 12; 12×2+2 = 26, not 20. Hmm.
>
> Recheck pattern. 6, 14, 30: differences are 8, 16. Ratio 1:2. Pattern: ×2 + 2.
> Try (c) 6, 12, 22: ×2 + 0 = 12; ×2 - 2 = 22. Different pattern.
> Try (b) 7, 12, 22: ×2 - 2 = 12; ×2 - 2 = 22. Wait — 7×2 = 14 - 2 = 12. 12×2 = 24 - 2 = 22. Pattern: ×2 - 2.
> Try (a) 4, 16, 28: ×2 + 8 = 16. 16×2 - 4 = 28. Mixed.
> Try (d) 5, 12, 20: ×2 + 2 = 12; 12×2 - 4 = 20. Mixed.
>
> The most consistent is **(b) 7, 12, 22** with pattern ×2 - 2.
> **Answer: (b)**

### Type 3: Alphabet Analogy (Standard ReferenceStandard)

**Logic:** Letter-pair relationships (opposite rule, +N shift, alphabetical order).
**Difficulty:** Medium
**Exam Source:** SSC CGL, SSC CPO, IBPS Clerk

**Solved Example 5:**
> "T R A C K: M E C T V::?:?"
> Answer pattern: shift each letter by +13.
>
> T + 13 = G? Wait, T is the 20th letter; +13 → 33 → wrap to 7 → G.
> But book says **M** for T. Let me check: T → M is -7 shift.
>
> Actually, the solution from Standard Referenceshows: TRACK → MECTV using **+13 reverse** or specific shift.
>
> Standard mapping: T-7=M, R+?=E... Actually, this is a **reverse +9** with wrapping. Let me test:
>
> T(20) - 7 = M(13)
> R(18) - 13 = E(5)
> A(1) + 2 = C(3)
> C(3) + 17 = T(20) [actually wait, +17 wrap is wrong]
> K(11) + 10 = V(22)
>
> The shifts are: -7, -13, +2, +17, +10. Inconsistent! This is actually a **wrong OCR**; the intended solution likely uses a single consistent shift. **Treat the original TRACK↔MECTV as garbled and use the principle: each letter maps by a single consistent shift.**

**Solved Example 6 (Clean Opposite Rule):**
> "P U V: M R S:: A E D M:?"
>
> P-Opp = K, but here P→M is -3 shift. So the rule is "subtract 3":
> P-3=M, U-3=R, V-3=S. Confirmed.
> Apply to AEDM: A-3=X, E-3=B, D-3=A, M-3=J. **Answer: XBAJ**.
>

### Type 4: Word Classification (Odd-One-Out — SSC Standard)

**Logic:** Identify the option that does not share the common property.
**Difficulty:** Easy
**Exam Source:** SSC CHSL, SSC MTS, SSC GD

**Solved Example 7:**
> "Which is the odd one out: Tiger, Lion, Leopard, Wolf?"
> (a) Tiger (b) Lion (c) Leopard (d) Wolf
>
> **Solution:** Tiger, Lion, Leopard are all big cats (family Felidae). Wolf is a canine (family Canidae).
> **Answer: (d) Wolf**

**Solved Example 8:**
> "Find odd: 4, 9, 16, 23"
> (a) 4 (b) 9 (c) 16 (d) 23
>
> **Solution:** 4 = 2², 9 = 3², 16 = 4². 23 is not a perfect square.
> **Answer: (d) 23**

### Type 5: Letter Classification

**Solved Example 9:**
> "Find odd: AZ, BY, CX, DW"
> (a) AZ (b) BY (c) CX (d) DW
>
> **Solution:** AZ = (1,26), BY = (2,25), CX = (3,24). All pairs sum to 27 (opposite rule). DW = (4, 23)? Wait — D=4, W=23, sum = 27. So DW also fits.
>
> Re-examine: A-Z, B-Y, C-X, D-W. All opposite pairs. **No odd one — this is a trap question.** In exam, if forced, look for the next inconsistent pair. (If all 4 fit, the question is wrong.)
>

**Solved Example 10:**
> "Find odd: BD, EG, HJ, MO"
> (a) BD (b) EG (c) HJ (d) MO
>
> **Solution:** B-D = (+2), E-G = (+2), H-J = (+2), M-O = (+2). All consistent.
> Re-check: B(2)-D(4)=+2; E(5)-G(7)=+2; H(8)-J(10)=+2; M(13)-O(15)=+2. All +2.
> Hmm, all fit. Likely an answer requires "+1 between pairs" or some other meta-rule. If question forces choice, suspect **MO** if BD/EG/HJ form a different sub-pattern. [NOT IN BOOKS — pattern analysis]

### Type 6: Number Series Completion

**Solved Example 11:**
> Find next: 2, 5, 8, 11,?
>
> **Solution:** Arithmetic, +3.
> **Answer: 14**

**Solved Example 12:**
> Find next: 3, 5, 8, 12, 14, 17, 20,?
>
> **Solution:** Alternating series. Odd positions: 3, 8, 14, 20 → +5, +6, +6 (or check 3, 8, 14, 20 = differences +5, +6, +6). Even positions: 5, 12, 17 → +7, +5. Inconsistent.
>
> Re-examine: 3, 5, 8, 12, 14, 17, 20
> 3→5 (+2), 5→8 (+3), 8→12 (+4), 12→14 (+2), 14→17 (+3), 17→20 (+3). Hmm.
>
> Try alternating: odd-indexed (1st, 3rd, 5th, 7th): 3, 8, 14, 20 → +5, +6, +6. Even-indexed (2nd, 4th, 6th, 8th): 5, 12, 17,? → +7, +5, +?
>
> Likely 8th term = 17+5 = 22 if even series is +7, +5, +5 (alternating). Or 17+7 = 24 if even series has +7, +5, +7 pattern.
>
> **Most consistent:** odd-indexed series ends at 20; if asked for the 8th term, it's the even series: 5, 12, 17,? → probably +7, +5, +7 = 24. **Answer: 24**

**Solved Example 13 (Square series):**
> 4, 9, 16, 25, 36,?
>
> **Solution:** $n^2$ for n=2,3,4,5,6. Next = 7² = 49.
> **Answer: 49**

**Solved Example 14 (Difference-of-difference):**
> 1, 4, 11, 22, 37,?
>
> **Solution:** First differences: 3, 7, 11, 15. Second differences: 4, 4, 4. So next first difference = 19. Next term = 37 + 19 = **56**.
>

**Solved Example 15 (Cube series):**
> 1, 8, 27, 64,?
>
> **Solution:** $n^3$ for n=1,2,3,4. Next = 5³ = **125**.
>

**Solved Example 16 (Mathematical operation series):**
> 5, 11, 23, 47,?
>
> **Solution:** Pattern ×2 + 1: 5×2+1=11, 11×2+1=23, 23×2+1=47. Next: 47×2+1 = **95**.
>

**Solution:** 8 = 2³, 27 = 3³, 64 = 4³. 100 is not a perfect cube (4³=64, 5³=125).

## 6. Quick Revision Sheet

### 6.1 Core Rules in Bullet Form

1. **Analogy form:** A: B:: C: D — find D such that C:D matches A:B.
2. **Direction matters.** Don't reverse: "Capital: Country" ≠ "Country: Capital".
3. **22 relationship families.** Master Country-Capital, Country-Currency, Instrument-Unit, Animal-Youngone, Worker-Tool, Part-Whole, Synonym-Opposite.
4. **Classification: most specific common property wins.**
5. **Opposite-letter rule:** A↔Z, B↔Y, C↔X... Each pair sums to 27.
6. **Alphabet shift:** Compute shift on first letter, apply to all.
7. **Series arithmetic:** Differences are constant → linear.
8. **Series geometric:** Ratios are constant → exponential.
9. **Series difference-of-difference:** Second differences constant → quadratic.
10. **Series square/cube:** $a_n \approx n^2$ or $n^3$ → check.

### 6.2 Decision Tree

| If you see... | Then do... |
|---------------|------------|
| Two country names | Look for capital / currency / national game |
| Two animal names | Look for young-one / sound / habitat / class |
| Two instrument names | Look for measurement type |
| Three numbers in arithmetic | Check first difference; if constant, linear |
| Three numbers in series | Check ratio; if constant, geometric |
| Three numbers not linear | Try difference-of-difference, then square/cube |
| Two letter pairs | Compute letter shift; apply to test options |
| One letter pair with -ve shift | Check opposite-letter rule (sum = 27) |
| 4 words, "odd one out" | Find common property of 3; 4th is answer |
